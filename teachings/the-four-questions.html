Feelings Are Witnesses, Not Judges — Part 5 of 5

The Four Questions

We walk by faith, not by sight.

  • 2 Corinthians 5
  • Feelings
  • Faith

"For we walk by faith, not by sight."

2 Corinthians 5:7 (ESV)

Part 5 of “Feelings Are Witnesses, Not Judges,” a five-part devotional series from The Hope Connection.

Next time the verdict lands at 3 a.m., you don't need a new feeling. You need four questions.

All week we have built one skill: hearing feelings as witnesses without handing them the gavel. Here is the whole practice, small enough to carry into the hardest hour — the psalmist's own examination, arranged as four questions.

One: What am I feeling? Say it plainly to God, unedited. The psalms are full of raw, honest speech — lament is not a lack of faith; it is faith talking honestly. Feelings brought into the light lose half their power to rule from the dark.

Two: What conclusion am I drawing from it? Drag the interpretation into the open. "I feel abandoned, therefore…" — that "therefore" is where the lie usually lives. Name it in a full sentence so it can be examined instead of obeyed.

Three: What does Scripture actually say? Let the Judge speak. Match the conclusion against a clear promise — Hebrews 13:5 against abandonment, Romans 8:1 against condemnation, Psalm 42:5 against despair. The Word decides; the witness does not.

Four: What obedient response follows — even if my emotions lag behind? Then walk it out. Faith, Paul says, walks "by faith, not by sight" (2 Corinthians 5:7) — and sight includes the view from inside a heavy heart. Obedience while feelings lag is not hypocrisy; it is what walking actually looks like.

Your feelings are witnesses, and honest ones deserve a hearing. But they were never given the gavel. The verdict over your life was rendered at the cross, and it does not change with the weather: in Christ, no condemnation — and a Redeemer who lives. Feelings report. The Word decides. Hope in God.

The Puritans called this "applying the Word to the conscience" — taking one promise and pressing it, deliberately, onto the exact spot where the accusation lands. It is the opposite of vague comfort. One text, one lie, one verdict from the only bench that counts — and then, as the old divines would say, rest the whole weight there.

Next time the verdict lands at 3 a.m., you don't need a new feeling. You need four questions.

Reflect — Questions & Answers

Which question do people skip most? Question two. We jump from the feeling straight to despair without ever saying the conclusion out loud. Spoken plainly, "therefore God has left me" often collapses the moment it is heard next to Hebrews 13:5.

Can I use the four questions for someone else's pain? Gently, and mostly by listening first. The questions are a lamp for your own soul; for a hurting brother or sister, sit with them, pray Psalm 42 with them, and let the Word do its work in God's timing.
