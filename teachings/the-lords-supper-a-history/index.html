The Lord's Supper — A History

The Table Christ Set, and How His Church Has Kept It

  • Teachings
  • Church History
  • The Lord's Supper

The Hope Connection — Lewis Fuller

1. The Table the Lord Himself Set

Of all the things Jesus left His church, only two can be touched. He gave us water, and He gave us a table. Baptism marks a believer once, at the door of the Christian life. The Lord's Supper feeds that believer again and again, all the way home. On the last night of His earthly life — the night He was betrayed, with the cross only hours away — Jesus did not spend His final evening giving lectures. He sat down at a table with His friends, took bread in His hands, and set a meal before the church that she is still eating two thousand years later.

His words over that meal were simple, and they were a command:

"And he took bread, and when he had given thanks, he broke it and gave it to them, saying, 'This is my body, which is given for you. Do this in remembrance of me.'" — Luke 22:19 (ESV)

"Do this." Not "watch this." Not "admire this." Do this — eat, drink, remember, together, until He comes again. The Supper is not the church's invention, and it is not an optional extra for especially devoted Christians. It is Christ's own ordinance, given by Christ's own hands, on Christ's own last night.

This is the story of that table: where it came from, what Jesus meant when He set it, how the early church kept it, how its meaning was slowly buried under human tradition, how God recovered it in the Reformation, and how a believer should come to it today. It is a story worth knowing, because the table is not a museum piece. It is set fresh every time the church gathers around it — and there is a place at it for every sinner who has trusted in the Lamb.

2. The Roots: A Meal Built on the Passover

The Lord's Supper did not appear out of nowhere. Jesus set His table on top of a much older one — the Passover, the meal God gave Israel fifteen hundred years before, on the night He brought them out of slavery in Egypt. To understand the Supper, you have to understand that night, because Jesus understood it, and He meant His disciples to see His death in its light.

The details are given in Exodus 12, and they are exact. On the tenth day of the month, every household of Israel was to take a lamb — a male, a year old, without blemish, taken from the sheep or the goats. If a household was too small for a whole lamb, it was to share with its nearest neighbor. The lamb was kept until the fourteenth day, and then, at twilight, the whole assembly of Israel killed their lambs at the same hour.

The blood was not wasted. With a bunch of hyssop, the head of the house dipped into the blood caught in a basin and struck the lintel and the two doorposts of his house. The flesh was roasted whole in fire — head, legs, and inner parts together — and eaten that night with unleavened bread and bitter herbs, by people dressed for a journey: belt fastened, sandals on their feet, staff in hand, eating in haste. Nothing was to be left until morning; what remained was burned. And no bone of the lamb was to be broken.

Why blood on the door? Because judgment was passing through Egypt that night, and the blood was God's appointed shelter from it:

"The blood shall be a sign for you, on the houses where you are. And when I see the blood, I will pass over you, and no plague will befall you to destroy you, when I strike the land of Egypt." — Exodus 12:13 (ESV)

God did not say, "When I see your goodness," or "When I see your effort." He said, "When I see the blood." The lamb died, and the people under its blood lived. And God commanded that this meal be kept forever, year after year, so that when the children asked, "What do you mean by this service?" their fathers could tell them the story of the night the Lord passed over the houses of His people and brought them out of slavery (Exodus 12:26–27).

Year after year, century after century, Israel ate that meal. There were dark generations when it was neglected, and bright ones when it was recovered — King Hezekiah reopened the temple and kept the Passover with such joy that the feast was extended a second week (2 Chronicles 30), and King Josiah, after the Book of the Law was found in the temple, kept a Passover so faithful that Scripture says none like it had been kept since the days of Samuel the judge (2 Kings 23:22). And every year, without knowing the half of it, they were rehearsing. The bitter herbs taught them the bitterness of slavery. The unleavened bread taught them the haste of God's deliverance — no time for dough to rise when God says, "Go." The lamb taught them that freedom costs a life, and that the life God accepts is the life of a substitute, slain in the sinner's place, its blood marking the door between a family and judgment.

So when Jesus sat down in an upper room in Jerusalem to eat the Passover with His disciples — knowing that before the next evening He would hang on a cross — He was not improvising. He was taking the oldest meal of God's people and filling it, at last, with its full meaning. John the Baptist had already named Him: "Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!" (John 1:29). Paul would later say it in one sentence: "For Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed" (1 Corinthians 5:7). The new meal was set inside the old one like a jewel in its setting.

3. The Institution: "This Is My Blood of the Covenant"

Matthew's account of that night is plain and full, and it deserves to be read whole:

"Now as they were eating, Jesus took bread, and after blessing it broke it and gave it to the disciples, and said, 'Take, eat; this is my body.' And he took a cup, and when he had given thanks he gave it to them, saying, 'Drink of it, all of you, for this is my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins.'" — Matthew 26:26–28 (ESV)

Notice what Jesus did. He took the bread of the Passover — the bread of affliction, the bread of haste — and He said, in effect: this bread has a new name now. It is My body, given for you. He took the cup and said: this cup has a new name too. It is My blood of the covenant, poured out for many, for the forgiveness of sins.

Every word carries weight. Covenant — the solemn bond between God and His people. The word reached back to Mount Sinai, where Moses took the blood of sacrificed oxen, threw half of it against the altar and half of it on the people, saying, "Behold the blood of the covenant that the LORD has made with you in accordance with all these words" (Exodus 24:8). That covenant was sealed in the blood of animals, and Israel broke it within weeks, dancing around a golden calf before the mountain had stopped smoking. Jesus speaks of a new covenant, sealed not with the blood of oxen but with His own — a covenant that does what the old one could only picture, because this blood actually reaches the conscience and actually forgives. Poured out — this is sacrifice language; His death would not be an accident of history but an offering, deliberately given. For many — not for Himself, for He had no sins to pay for, but for others, a great company of them. For the forgiveness of sins — here is the purpose of the cross stated by the Lord Himself, the night before He endured it: sins, forgiven, through His blood.

And notice what Jesus gave them: He gave them bread to eat and a cup to drink — both of them, to all of them. "Drink of it, all of you." The table was set for the whole company of disciples, and the whole of it was given to the whole of them. That simple fact will matter later in this story, because there came a time when the church took half of it back.

Luke adds the command we have already heard: "Do this in remembrance of me." Paul adds the church's mission in it: "For as often as you eat this bread and drink the cup, you proclaim the Lord's death until he comes" (1 Corinthians 11:26). So the Supper looks in three directions at once. It looks back, in remembrance, to a finished cross. It looks around, in fellowship, at a family gathered at one table. And it looks forward, in hope, to a coming King. A meal with a memory, a family, and a future — that is what Jesus set before His church on the night He was betrayed.

4. The Supper in the Early Church: A Family at the Table

The first Christians obeyed their Lord's command the way children obey a father they love — simply, gladly, and often. Luke's picture of the church in its first days puts the table near the center of its life:

"And they devoted themselves to the apostles' teaching and the fellowship, to the breaking of bread and the prayers." — Acts 2:42 (ESV)

"And day by day, attending the temple together and breaking bread in their homes, they received their food with glad and generous hearts." — Acts 2:46 (ESV)

In the years that followed, the pattern settled: the church gathered on the first day of the week — the Lord's Day, the day of the resurrection — and the breaking of bread belonged to that gathering. When Paul waited a week in Troas, Luke tells us why the church was together on Sunday: "On the first day of the week, when we were gathered together to break bread, Paul talked with them" (Acts 20:7). Around the year 155, a Christian writer named Justin Martyr described the Sunday service of the churches for the Roman emperor: the writings of the apostles and prophets were read, the president of the congregation preached and prayed, and then bread and a cup of wine mixed with water were brought, thanks was given, the people said Amen, and everyone present ate and drank — while deacons carried portions to believers who were absent, sick, or in prison. The table was for the whole family, including the ones who could not be in the room.

The Supper also taught the church how to treat the poor. At those same Sunday gatherings Justin describes, the believers made a collection — each giving freely as he was able — and the money was entrusted to the president of the congregation for the relief of orphans and widows, the sick, prisoners, and strangers passing through. This was no accident of scheduling. A church that had just eaten from one loaf and drunk from one cup could not walk past a hungry brother on the way out the door. The meal preached a sermon the wallet had to answer. When the Supper is healthy, generosity follows it the way warmth follows a fire.

Joined to the Supper in those early centuries was a shared meal of love — the agape, or love feast, which Jude mentions when he speaks of those who "feast with you without fear" at "your love feasts" (Jude 12). The church ate together as families eat: rich and poor at the same table, slaves and free side by side, because at Christ's table the world's rankings do not apply.

But the early church also knew this meal was holy, and Paul's teaching to the Corinthians is the fullest instruction we have. He handed on to them what he himself had received from the Lord:

"For I received from the Lord what I also delivered to you, that the Lord Jesus on the night when he was betrayed took bread, and when he had given thanks, he broke it, and said, 'This is my body, which is for you. Do this in remembrance of me.' In the same way also he took the cup, after supper, saying, 'This cup is the new covenant in my blood. Do this, as often as you drink it, in remembrance of me.' For as often as you eat this bread and drink the cup, you proclaim the Lord's death until he comes." — 1 Corinthians 11:23–26 (ESV)

Paul wrote those words because Corinth was abusing the table — the rich were gorging themselves and getting drunk while the poor went hungry, turning the Lord's meal into a display of the very divisions Christ died to destroy. His correction is solemn. The Supper is a proclamation: every time the church eats and drinks, she preaches the cross without opening her mouth. And because the meal is holy, it is not to be taken carelessly: "Whoever, therefore, eats the bread or drinks the cup of the Lord in an unworthy manner will be guilty concerning the body and blood of the Lord" (1 Corinthians 11:27). The early church took that seriously. The Didache, a manual of church practice from the late first century, instructs believers to confess their sins before the breaking of bread "that your sacrifice may be pure," and bars the unbaptized from the table: "Let no one eat or drink of your Eucharist except those baptized into the name of the Lord." Ignatius of Antioch, on his way to martyrdom around the year 107, called the bread of the Supper "the medicine of immortality." These were not careless people with a casual meal. They were a family at a holy table, eating often, remembering deeply, and proclaiming Christ's death to a watching world.

5. How the Meaning Was Buried — and Why It Had to Be Dug Out

So how did a family meal of remembrance become something the family mostly watched? It happened slowly, over centuries, the way a garden is lost — not in a day, but by neglect and by weeds no one pulled.

In the early centuries, the language of the church about the Supper was rich and reverent, and rightly so. But gradually, reverence for the elements hardened into a doctrine about the elements. Teachers began to speak of the bread and wine not merely as signs that point to Christ, but as changing into Christ's very body and blood. The turning point can be seen in the case of Berengar of Tours, an eleventh-century teacher in France who argued that the bread and wine remain what they are, and that Christ is received spiritually, by faith. Berengar was hauled before councils and forced, more than once, to sign statements of belief worded to crush his teaching — confessions so crudely physical that later theologians were embarrassed by them. His treatment showed the direction the wind was blowing: the question was no longer what the believer receives by faith, but what the priest's consecration does to the elements. In 1215, the Fourth Lateran Council in Rome gave this teaching official form and a name: transubstantiation — the claim that at the priest's words of consecration, the whole substance of the bread is changed into the substance of Christ's body, and the whole substance of the wine into His blood, while only the outward appearance of bread and wine remains.

Let that claim be stated fairly, because it was held by sincere men who believed they were honoring Christ: they taught that Christ is truly, substantially present in the consecrated elements, that the Mass makes present the one sacrifice of Calvary, and that the priest offers Christ to the Father on behalf of the living and the dead. From that root, other fruits grew. If the consecrated bread was Christ Himself, then it was to be adored, carried in procession, and gazed upon — and so the practice spread of the people attending Mass not to eat and drink, but to see the host lifted up. If Christ was offered afresh in every Mass, then the Supper was no longer chiefly a meal where Christ feeds His people; it was a sacrifice where a priest acts for people who watch. And in time the cup was taken from the laity altogether: at the Council of Constance in 1415, the church in council formally forbade giving the cup to the common people, against the plain words of the Lord who said, "Drink of it, all of you." The people received the bread only — often only a few times a year, later at most once a year by requirement — while the priest ate and drank on their behalf, in a language most of them could not understand.

Against all of this stands one passage of Scripture like a lighthouse. The book of Hebrews will not allow the cross to be repeated, supplemented, or re-offered, because it does not need to be:

"And by that will we have been sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all. And every priest stands daily at his service, offering repeatedly the same sacrifices, which can never take away sins. But when Christ had offered for all time a single sacrifice for sins, he sat down at the right hand of God, waiting from that time until his enemies should be made a footstool for his feet. For by a single offering he has perfected for all time those who are being sanctified." — Hebrews 10:10–14 (ESV)

Once for all. A single sacrifice. He sat down — because the work was finished. The priests of the old covenant stood, because their work never ended; Christ sat, because His did. A Supper that presents Christ as offered again denies, however politely, the finished work that the Supper itself was given to proclaim. The table was buried under an altar. It would take the Reformation to dig it out.

6. The Reformation Debates: Three Men, One Table, and a Word in Chalk

When the Reformation came, the Reformers were united about the great thing: the Mass as a repeated sacrifice of Christ had to go, because Christ's one sacrifice is finished and sufficient. They restored the cup to the people. They put the service into the language of the people. They set a table again where an altar had stood. But among themselves, the Reformers argued — honestly, publicly, and sometimes painfully — about what Jesus meant by four small words: "This is my body." The argument matters, because what a church believes about the table shapes how her people come to it.

Martin Luther rejected transubstantiation and the sacrifice of the Mass root and branch. But Luther held with an iron grip to the plain words of Christ: "This is my body." He taught that Christ's true body and blood are really present in the Supper and received by all who eat and drink — later Lutherans would describe it as Christ present "in, with, and under" the bread and wine. Luther would not let the words of institution be explained away, and he trusted them the way a child trusts a father's promise.

Ulrich Zwingli, the reformer of Zurich, read the same words differently. For Zwingli, "This is my body" means "This signifies my body." The Supper, in his teaching, is a memorial: the church looks back to the cross, gives thanks, professes her faith, and pledges herself to Christ and to one another. Christ's body, Zwingli argued, is in heaven at the Father's right hand, and a body can only be in one place. What the Supper gives the believer is remembrance, gratitude, and fellowship — real gifts, but the elements themselves remain bread and wine.

In 1529, Prince Philip of Hesse, desperate to unite the German and Swiss reformations against the emperor, brought Luther and Zwingli face to face at his castle in the Marburg Colloquy. It is one of the great scenes of church history. The two men agreed on fourteen of the fifteen articles before them — on the Trinity, on Christ, on baptism, on justification by faith. They disagreed on one: the Supper. Luther had written the words in chalk on the table before him, in Latin: Hoc est corpus meum — "This is my body" — and he would not move from them, tapping the table as he insisted on the plain sense of Christ's words. Zwingli, in tears, offered his hand in fellowship; Luther, believing the difference touched the gospel itself, would not take it on those terms. They parted as men who agreed on nearly everything and could not unite on this. It is a sad and honest picture: the recovery of the gospel was real, and so was the cost of sincere disagreement among sincere men.

John Calvin sought the road between Wittenberg and Zurich, and the Reformed churches have walked it ever since. Calvin agreed with Zwingli that Christ's human body is in heaven and does not descend into the bread; he agreed with Luther that the Supper is far more than a bare memory. His teaching, set out in his Institutes of the Christian Religion, is this: at the table, Christ truly feeds His people with Himself — not by the bread changing, and not by Christ coming down, but by the Holy Spirit lifting believers up, so that by faith they genuinely receive and feed upon the crucified and risen Christ. The signs are not empty. What they picture, God actually gives to faith through them. Paul's words were Calvin's foundation:

"The cup of blessing that we bless, is it not a participation in the blood of Christ? The bread that we break, is it not a participation in the body of Christ?" — 1 Corinthians 10:16 (ESV)

A participation — a real sharing, a real communion. That is the Reformed confession Lewis holds, and it can be stated plainly: at the Lord's table, Jesus Christ truly feeds His people with Himself, by the Holy Spirit, received by faith. The bread remains bread and the cup remains the fruit of the vine, but the meal is no empty ceremony and no mere mental exercise. The host is alive, He is present by His Spirit, and He nourishes every believer who comes hungry. The unbeliever receives bread and wine and judgment; the believer receives bread and wine and Christ. The difference is not in the elements. The difference is faith — the open hand that takes what the table promises.

This teaching shaped a practice. In Scotland under John Knox and his successors, where parishes were vast and ministers few, the Supper grew into the famous communion season: days of preparation sermons, self-examination, and reconciliation between neighbors, gathering whole districts to the table, with simple metal tokens given to those who had been examined and admitted, so the table would be kept with care. Critics called it infrequent; no one called it casual. The instinct behind it is the Reformed instinct everywhere — the table is the Lord's, the meal is real, and because Christ truly meets His people there, His people should come prepared, examined, and hungry.

7. The Supper Today: Who Should Come, and How

All of this history lands in a practical question every believer faces: when the bread is passed and the cup is offered, should I take it? The Scripture's answer is warm and serious at the same time.

Who should come? The table is for sinners — but for sinners of a particular kind: those who know they are sinners and have fled to Christ. It is not for the righteous, for there are none; it is not for the worthy, for none are worthy in themselves. It is for the baptized people of God who trust in Jesus alone, who are at peace with His church as far as it depends on them, and who come hungry. The Heidelberg Catechism, one of the Reformed church's beloved teaching tools, says that those who are displeased with themselves because of their sins, yet trust that their sins are forgiven for Christ's sake, and who desire more and more to strengthen their faith and amend their lives — these are the welcome guests. A trembling faith is still faith. A weak hand can still take bread.

How should we come? Paul gives the instruction, and it is an examination — but notice, a man examines himself, not his neighbor:

"Let a person examine himself, then, and so eat of the bread and drink of the cup. For anyone who eats and drinks without discerning the body eats and drinks judgment on himself." — 1 Corinthians 11:28–29 (ESV)

Self-examination is not a hunt for worthiness; it is a check for honesty. Do I know my sin? Do I trust Christ for it? Do I mean to follow Him? Churches that love their people practice what is called fencing the table: the minister, before the Supper, plainly invites believers to come and plainly warns the unbelieving and the unrepentant to stay back — not in cruelty, but in love, the way you would warn a friend away from a cliff edge. A fence around a table is not there to keep the hungry out. It is there to keep the careless from harming themselves at a holy place.

One loaf, one family. The Supper also binds the church to herself. Paul argues from the table to the congregation: "Because there is one bread, we who are many are one body, for we all partake of the one bread" (1 Corinthians 10:17). Men and women who have eaten together at Christ's table cannot easily hold grudges at the door. This is why the church has historically urged believers to be reconciled with one another before coming, and why unresolved bitterness is a reason to pause and make peace first. The table is a family meal, and families are meant to be whole.

How often? Scripture commands the doing of it, not the calendar of it, and faithful churches have differed — some weekly, as the earliest churches appear to have done; some monthly; some quarterly, as many Reformed churches historically practiced. Calvin himself longed for the Supper to be spread before the people far more often than the city of Geneva would allow. What no church may do is treat it as rare and optional, for Christ did not say "Do this occasionally." A table your Lord set for your nourishment should not gather dust.

And every time the church eats this meal, she tastes the future. The Supper is an appetizer. Jesus promised He would not drink of the fruit of the vine again "until that day when I drink it new with you in my Father's kingdom" (Matthew 26:29). There is a greater table ahead, and Scripture names it at the end of the Book:

"And the angel said to me, 'Write this: Blessed are those who are invited to the marriage supper of the Lamb.'" — Revelation 19:9 (ESV)

Every Lord's Supper is a rehearsal dinner for that feast. The same Lamb who was slain will host it. The same people who ate by faith at His table here will eat by sight at His table there — and the remembrance will be over, because the Remembered will be in the room.

8. Come to the Table

Here is the whole history in one sentence: Jesus set a table for sinners, and no century — not persecution, not corruption, not controversy — has ever managed to clear it away.

Maybe you read this as someone who has stayed back from that table for a long time. Maybe you have told yourself you are too far gone, too stained, too late. Hear the order of things one more time: the lamb was slain before the people were freed, the blood was on the door before the judgment passed over, and Christ was crucified before you ever took your first step toward Him. The table does not ask what you bring. It asks only whether you are hungry — and whether you will trust the Host.

The bad news is real, and the table does not hide it: we are sinners, and we cannot feed our own souls. But the good news is better: Christ has died, Christ is risen, and Christ still says to people like us, "Take, eat." Come to Him first by faith, and then come to His table as often as His church spreads it, until the day remembrance gives way to sight.

That is why the church, in every age, has guarded this meal the way a family guards its own table. Empires have tried to ban it. Traditions have buried it. Believers have argued over it, sometimes to tears. And still, every Lord's Day, in cathedrals and country chapels, in rented rooms and prison cells and houses that must keep their voices low, the bread is broken and the cup is lifted, and the death of the Lord is proclaimed — until He comes. He is coming. The table says so, every single time it is set.

Let us pray. Lord Jesus, our Passover Lamb, thank You for the table You set on the night You were betrayed — for bread that speaks of Your body given, and a cup that speaks of Your blood poured out for the forgiveness of sins. Forgive us for every time we have treated Your table lightly, and for every time we have stayed away from it in unbelief, as though our sins were greater than Your mercy. Feed us by Your Spirit with Yourself, until we hunger for nothing else. And keep us proclaiming Your death with glad and examined hearts, until we eat with You at the marriage supper of the Lamb. In Your name we pray. Amen.

Christ Proclaimed. Jesus is Lord.
