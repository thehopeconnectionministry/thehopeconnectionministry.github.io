John 19 — It Is Finished: The Cross and the Burial

Jesus in John — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Gospel of John
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

In John 19, Jesus reveals Himself as the King crowned with thorns who finishes the work on the cross — paid in full — providing for His mother from the cross, fulfilling Scripture down to the unbroken bones and the pierced side, and buried like a king by two secret disciples who come out of hiding at last. John was there. He writes as an eyewitness.

Commentary

Behold the man (19:1–16). Pilate takes Jesus and flogs Him — a scourging that tore flesh from bone — and the soldiers stage a coronation in mockery: a crown of thorns pressed onto His head, a purple robe on His torn back, and a liturgy of slaps and sarcasm: "Hail, King of the Jews!" John wants you to see the irony the soldiers cannot: this is the coronation. The King of the Jews is being crowned, in the only crown this world had for Him, and He wears it without a word of retaliation. Pilate brings Him out wearing it — hoping the sight of the beaten, thorn-crowned man will satisfy the crowd's blood and let him release a prisoner he knows is innocent: "Behold the man!" It is more true than Pilate knows. Behold the Man — the second Adam, the true Human, standing where every human should look to see both what sin does and what God does about it. The chief priests and officers cry, "Crucify him, crucify him!" Pilate protests his own verdict again — "I find no guilt in him" — and they finally play their true card: "We have a law, and according to that law he ought to die because he has made himself the Son of God." When Pilate hears Son of God, he is more afraid than ever — the pagan smells heaven in the case — and interviews Jesus again: "Where are you from?" Jesus gives no answer. "You will not speak to me? Do you not know that I have authority to release you and authority to crucify you?" And the bound prisoner instructs the governor on the limits of his power: "You would have no authority over me at all unless it had been given you from above. Therefore he who delivered me over to you has the greater sin." Even Pilate's authority is borrowed, and the cross is happening under heaven's government, not Rome's improvisation. Pilate seeks to release Him; the leaders deploy the political threat that finally bends him: "If you release this man, you are not Caesar's friend." From noon — the sixth hour, as the Passover lambs are being prepared — Pilate sits on the judgment seat and delivers his sentence with words that ring through the centuries: "Behold your King!" And God's own people, through their leaders, give the answer that seals the tragedy: "We have no king but Caesar." The nation that was promised a King from David's line renounces Him for Caesar, on the record, at Passover. "So he delivered him over to them to be crucified."

The King on the cross (19:17–24). "So they took Jesus, and he went out, bearing his own cross, to the place called The Place of a Skull, which in Aramaic is called Golgotha. There they crucified him, and with him two others, one on either side, and Jesus between them." John's telling is spare and regal. No Simon carrying the cross here, no long road — John keeps his eyes on the King. Pilate writes the title and puts it on the cross: "Jesus of Nazareth, the King of the Jews" — in Hebrew, in Latin, and in Greek, the languages of religion, empire, and culture, so the whole world can read its King's name. The chief priests protest: write "he said, I am King." Pilate, finding at last one inch of backbone, answers: "What I have written I have written." God used a vacillating governor to publish the truth over the cross in three languages, and no committee could get it taken down. Below, the soldiers divide His garments and cast lots for the seamless tunic — "that the Scripture might be fulfilled" (Psalm 22:18). While the King dies for the world, men gamble at the foot of the cross for His clothes. John records it without rage. It is simply what the world does at the foot of a cross: it does not notice.

Woman, behold your son (19:25–27). But some do notice. "Standing by the cross of Jesus were his mother and his mother's sister, Mary the wife of Clopas, and Mary Magdalene." The women stay when the men have scattered — and one man is there too: "the disciple whom he loved," John himself. From the cross, in His agony, Jesus does the work of a faithful son: "Woman, behold, your son!" and to John, "Behold, your mother!" "And from that hour the disciple took her to his own home." The dying Savior arranges His mother's care. The commandment to honor father and mother is kept from the cross itself. And a new family is formed at the foot of the cross — mother and disciple bound to each other not by blood but by Him — a small picture of the church He is purchasing. No detail of love is beneath Him, even now. Especially now.

It is finished (19:28–30). "After this, Jesus, knowing that all was now finished, said (to fulfill the Scripture), 'I thirst.'" Even His thirst fulfills the Psalms. A sponge of sour wine is lifted on a hyssop branch — hyssop, the branch of the Passover night — to His lips. "When Jesus had received the sour wine, he said, 'It is finished,' and he bowed his head and gave up his spirit." One word in Greek: tetelestai (τετέλεσται) — "It is finished." In the ancient world the word was written across receipts: paid in full. Jesus does not say "I am finished," as though He were used up. He says it is finished — the work accomplished, the debt discharged completely, permanently, and for others. And John is precise about the manner of His death: He bowed His head — a deliberate, almost restful act — and gave up His spirit. No one took His life. As He promised in chapter 10, He laid it down of His own accord, at the moment of completion, like a King signing the last document and laying down the pen.

The pierced side (19:31–37). Because it is the day of Preparation, the bodies must not remain on the cross during the Sabbath, so the soldiers break the legs of the two crucified with Him to hasten death. "But when they came to Jesus and saw that he was already dead, they did not break his legs." The Passover lamb's rule holds, centuries later, on a Roman cross: not a bone of Him broken (Exodus 12:46; Psalm 34:20). "But one of the soldiers pierced his side with a spear, and at once there came out blood and water." John stops the narrative to swear to it — the only place he breaks the frame: "He who saw it has borne witness — his testimony is true, and he knows that he is telling the truth — that you also may believe." Blood and water: the blood that cleanses, the water that washes — the two sacraments of the church have been seen in that flow, and at minimum the fact itself certifies a real death of a real body. The Son of God did not seem to die. He died, and an eyewitness with a soldier's spear for evidence stakes his credibility on it. And Zechariah's word stands over the scene: "they shall look on him whom they have pierced" (Zechariah 12:10). One day every eye will. John is training ours now.

A king's burial (19:38–42). After these things, the secret disciples come out of hiding — and it takes a cross to do it. Joseph of Arimathea, "a disciple of Jesus, but secretly for fear of the Jews," asks Pilate for the body. Nicodemus comes too — the night visitor of chapter 3, the cautious defender of chapter 7, now in broad daylight — "bringing a mixture of myrrh and aloes, about seventy-five pounds in weight." That is a king's quantity of burial spice. The man who came by night now carries a fortune in spices through a public street to a tomb. Grace grows men slowly, and it grows them surely. They wrap the body in linen with the spices, according to Jewish burial custom, and lay Him in a new tomb in a garden near the place of crucifixion, where no one had yet been laid. John notes the garden, and the newness of the tomb. The story that began in a garden where man hid from God, and turned in a garden where the Son stepped forward, now rests in a garden tomb. Wait there with the women and the spices. The garden has one more morning in it.

Key verses

"Pilate also wrote an inscription and put it on the cross. It read, 'Jesus of Nazareth, the King of the Jews.'" — John 19:19 (ESV)

"When Jesus had received the sour wine, he said, 'It is finished,' and he bowed his head and gave up his spirit." — John 19:30 (ESV)

"He who saw it has borne witness — his testimony is true, and he knows that he is telling the truth — that you also may believe." — John 19:35 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of John 19 is displayed without mercy: the King crowned with thorns and robed in mockery, God's own people crying "We have no king but Caesar," soldiers gambling under the cross, and a debt of sin so real that only a death could pay it. But the good news is one Greek word spoken from that cross: tetelestai — it is finished, paid in full, nothing left owing. He bowed His head and gave up His spirit of His own accord, and the receipt stands written over every believer's debt forever.

A note from the Reformers. The finished work is the Reformation's beating heart. Luther's theology of the cross says God hides His strength under weakness and His victory under defeat — and that the believer adds nothing to a salvation announced as finished. Grace Alone, Christ Alone, Faith Alone are all just footnotes to one Greek word spoken from a cross.

Reflection question: "It is finished" means nothing is left for you to pay. What debt do you keep trying to pay God for — guilt you rehearse, penance you perform — as if the receipt were not already stamped?
