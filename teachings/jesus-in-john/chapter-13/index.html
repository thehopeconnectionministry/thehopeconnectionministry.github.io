John 13 — He Loved Them to the End: Feet Washed and a New Commandment

Jesus in John — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Gospel of John
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

In John 13, the hour has come and the King kneels. Jesus reveals Himself as the Lord who loves His own to the end — who rises from supper, takes a towel, and washes the feet of men who will deny, scatter, and betray Him before the night is out — and who leaves them, and us, a new commandment measured by His own love.

Commentary

He loved them to the end (13:1–5). John sets the scene with the sentence that governs everything that follows: "Now before the Feast of the Passover, when Jesus knew that his hour had come to depart out of this world to the Father, having loved his own who were in the world, he loved them to the end." The word is telos (τέλος, Greek) — the end, the goal, the uttermost. He loved them all the way — to the last hour, to the last drop, to the full extent love can go. There is no point past the cross where His love for His own runs out. And John tells us Jesus acts in full knowledge: knowing the Father had given all things into His hands, knowing He had come from God and was going back to God. It is from that height — all things in His hands, heaven behind and ahead of Him — that He does the next thing. "He laid aside his outer garments, and taking a towel, tied it around his waist. Then he poured water into a basin and began to wash the disciples' feet and to wipe them with the towel that was wrapped around him." The hands that hold all things hold a basin. The Lord of glory does the slave's task — the task no Jewish servant could even be required to do. Omnipotence, on its knees, with a towel. If you want to see what God is like at the height of His power, John says, look at the floor of the upper room.

You shall never wash my feet (13:6–11). Peter resists, as Peter does: "Lord, do you wash my feet?" "What I am doing you do not understand now, but afterward you will understand." "You shall never wash my feet." Peter means it as reverence; it is actually refusal — he will have Christ on his own terms, a Lord who does not stoop to him. Jesus answers with the line that separates religion from salvation: "If I do not wash you, you have no share with me." The washing is not optional decoration. To have a share with Christ — any part in Him at all — you must let Him wash you. You cannot wash yourself into His company. Peter, all heart as always, swings the other way: "Lord, not my feet only but also my hands and my head!" Jesus teaches him the difference between the bath and the basin: "The one who has bathed does not need to wash, except for his feet, but is completely clean. And you are clean, but not every one of you." The believer is bathed once — justified, cleansed wholly in Christ — and then, walking dusty roads daily, needs the daily washing of feet: confession, forgiveness, the ongoing cleansing of the Word. One bath; many basins. "But not every one of you" — for He knew who was to betray Him. Judas' feet were washed too. Let that sit. The Lord washed the feet of His betrayer with the same hands and the same water, hours before the betrayal. That is what "loved them to the end" looks like when it is not a slogan.

Do as I have done (13:12–20). When He has resumed His place, He interprets the acted sermon: "Do you understand what I have done to you? You call me Teacher and Lord, and you are right, for so I am. If I then, your Lord and Teacher, have washed your feet, you also ought to wash one another's feet. For I have given you an example, that you also should do just as I have done to you." The order is everything: the washing received first, then the washing given. A servant is not greater than his master — if the Master kneels, no disciple stands too tall to kneel. "If you know these things, blessed are you if you do them." Blessing is not in the knowing. It is in the doing — in towels taken up in kitchens and hospital rooms and church basements, in low work for people who cannot repay. Jesus also speaks, with Psalm 41, of the one at the table who has lifted his heel against Him — and He tells them beforehand, "so that when it does take place you may believe that I am he." Even the betrayal is folded into His purpose and offered as evidence: He is not a victim of events; He foretells them.

One of you will betray me (13:21–30). "After saying these things, Jesus was troubled in spirit, and testified, 'Truly, truly, I say to you, one of you will betray me.'" Troubled — the betrayal is not a chess move to Him; it is a wound, foreseen and felt. The disciples look at one another, uncertain — no one points at Judas, which tells you how well hidden a heart can be inside a community. Peter signals to John — "the disciple whom Jesus loved," leaning back against Jesus at the table — to ask who it is. "It is he to whom I will give this morsel of bread when I have dipped it." The dipped morsel was a host's gesture of honor and friendship. Jesus gives it to Judas. Even now, an honor — a last appeal. "So when he had dipped the morsel, he gave it to Judas... Then after he had taken the morsel, Satan entered into him." The gift refused becomes the door for the enemy. Jesus says, "What you are going to do, do quickly." The others, hearing only table talk, think it is about supplies or alms for the poor. "So, after receiving the morsel of bread, he immediately went out. And it was night." John writes weather reports for the soul. Judas walks out of the lighted room into the literal night, carrying the darkness he has chosen. It was night — outside, and in him.

A new commandment (13:31–35). And now — with Judas gone — Jesus speaks as though a weight has lifted: "Now is the Son of Man glorified, and God is glorified in him." The betrayal sets the glory in motion; the machinery of the cross is the machinery of glorification. Then the legacy: "Little children" — His only use of the tender word in this Gospel — "yet a little while I am with you... A new commandment I give to you, that you love one another: just as I have loved you, you also are to love one another. By this all people will know that you are my disciples, if you have love for one another." Why new? "Love your neighbor" is as old as Leviticus. The newness is the measure and the source: just as I have loved you — towel love, cross love, love to the end — and love now flowing from a washed people. And it is the church's public mark: not our politics, not our polish, but our love for one another is how the world identifies His disciples. Where that love is absent, the sign over the door is blank, whatever the doctrine inside.

Where I am going (13:36–38). Peter, warm with the moment, asks, "Lord, where are you going?" "Where I am going you cannot follow me now, but you will follow afterward." "Lord, why can I not follow you now? I will lay down my life for you." Peter means it. That is what makes the answer so sad and so merciful: "Will you lay down your life for me? Truly, truly, I say to you, the rooster will not crow till you have denied me three times." Jesus knows Peter better than Peter knows Peter — knows the denial coming by a charcoal fire before dawn — and loves him anyway, washes his feet anyway, and has already planned the restoration of chapter 21. He tells Peter the truth about Peter without withdrawing one ounce of love from Peter. That is how He deals with all of His own: no illusions, and no abandonment.

Key verses

"Having loved his own who were in the world, he loved them to the end." — John 13:1 (ESV)

"Jesus answered him, 'If I do not wash you, you have no share with me.'" — John 13:8 (ESV)

"A new commandment I give to you, that you love one another: just as I have loved you, you also are to love one another." — John 13:34 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of John 13 is that at the table of love sit a betrayer who will walk out into the night and a boaster who will deny before the rooster crows — and that we cannot wash ourselves clean enough to have a share with Christ. But the good news is that Jesus kneels anyway: He washes the feet of deniers and even of His betrayer, He makes the unclean clean by His own act, and He loves His own to the end — to the uttermost, past the cross, with no point where His love runs out.

A note from the Reformers. The new commandment comes after the washing, not before it — love is the fruit of being loved, never the price of it. Luther's rule for the Christian life was exactly this order: faith first receives Christ's cleansing, and then, because the heart is full, love pours out toward the neighbor without being commanded twice.

Reflection question: Jesus washed the feet of men He knew would deny, scatter, and betray Him. Whose feet, literally or not, is He calling you to wash without keeping score?
