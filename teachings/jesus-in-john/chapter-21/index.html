John 21 — Feed My Sheep: Breakfast by the Sea and Peter Restored

Jesus in John — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Gospel of John
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

In John 21, the risen Jesus reveals Himself as the Lord who restores failures for service. By a charcoal fire — the second charcoal fire in this Gospel — He counts three loves for three denials, hands the shepherd his sheep back, and leaves Peter, and us, with the only orders that finally matter: "Follow me."

Commentary

I am going fishing (21:1–3). After everything — the resurrection appearances, the commission, the promise of the Spirit — Peter says, "I am going fishing," and the others say, "We will go with you." They go back to the boats, to the old life, to the trade they knew before Jesus called them out of it. It is not rebellion. It is something quieter and more common: after failure — after a denial Peter still has not faced with the Lord — a man can quietly quit, drifting back to what he knew before, because the old life at least does not ask him what he did by the fire. "That night they caught nothing." A whole night of expert fishermen, on their own water, with their own nets — nothing. John lets the empty nets preach: the old life, resumed on our own terms, does not even deliver what it used to. Apart from Him — chapter 15's word again — we can do nothing, and sometimes He lets the nets stay empty until morning to prove it kindly.

Breakfast on the shore (21:4–14). "Just as day was breaking, Jesus stood on the shore; yet the disciples did not know that it was Jesus." Dawn on the Sea of Tiberias, a figure on the beach. "Children, do you have any fish?" Children — the word is tender, almost playful. "No." "Cast the net on the right side of the boat, and you will find some." They obey a stranger's fishing advice, and the net fills past hauling — one hundred fifty-three large fish, counted by fishermen who count, "and although there were so many, the net was not torn." The beloved disciple's memory fires first — this happened before, in Luke 5, at the first call: "It is the Lord!" And Peter — impulsive, restored-in-advance Peter — puts on his outer garment, throws himself into the sea, and swims for shore, because swimming is faster than rowing when it is Him. The others bring the boat and the miraculous net. And on the shore: "they saw a charcoal fire in readiness, and fish laid out on it, and bread." A charcoal fire. John uses that word only twice in his Gospel: here, and in the high priest's courtyard in chapter 18, where Peter warmed himself and denied. The risen Lord has built Peter's restoration on the very set of his fall — same fire, same smell of charcoal in the morning air — because grace does not avoid the scene of the failure; it reclaims it. And notice: before a single word is said about the denial, Jesus feeds them. "Jesus said to them, 'Come and have breakfast.'" He adds their fish to His fire — their catch counts, though He provided the fire, the bread, and fish already cooking before they caught a thing. Grace provides; our work is gathered in, not needed and not despised. "None of the disciples dared ask him, 'Who are you?' They knew it was the Lord." A holy quiet hangs over that breakfast. It is the third appearance to the disciples, John says — and the most pastoral meal in Scripture.

Do you love me? (21:15–19). When they have finished breakfast, Jesus turns to the unfinished business — gently, publicly, and by name: "Simon, son of John, do you love me more than these?" Not "Peter" — Simon, his old name, the name of the man before the calling; and not "do you regret," or "can you explain," but do you love me. The restoration of a fallen man is not an interrogation about the fall. It is a question about love, asked three times — three loves for three denials, counted out one by one beside a charcoal fire, so that every denial is answered in the open and none is left to fester. "Yes, Lord; you know that I love you." Peter's answers grow humbler as they go; by the third he appeals not to his own record but to Christ's knowledge: "Lord, you know everything; you know that I love you." That is the voice of a man who has stopped trusting his own self-assessment — the man who once said "I will lay down my life for you" now says only, "You know." And each answer is met not with probation but with a commission: "Feed my lambs." "Tend my sheep." "Feed my sheep." The fallen denier is handed the flock. Restoration in Christ's hands is not a return to the audience; it is a return to responsibility — the sheep are His ("my sheep"), the shepherd is forgiven, and the feeding resumes. Then Jesus tells him the truth about his future, as He always does: in youth Peter dressed himself and walked where he wanted; in old age he will stretch out his hands, be dressed by another, and carried where he does not want to go — John explains: "This he said to show by what kind of death he was to glorify God." Peter's death will glorify God, and Jesus calls that a thing to walk toward, not away from. And after the prophecy, the simplest orders in the Gospel, the same two words that began it all by this same sea: "Follow me."

Follow me — and the beloved disciple (21:20–25). Peter, restored, immediately does what restored people do — he looks sideways. Turning, he sees the beloved disciple following and asks, "Lord, what about this man?" It is the first comparison question of the restored life, and Jesus declines it with a sentence every believer needs framed: "If it is my will that he remain until I come, what is that to you? You follow me!" Another man's calling, lifespan, and assignment are not your business. Your business is two words: follow Me. A rumor grows from the saying — that this disciple would not die — and John corrects it with an old man's precision: Jesus did not say he would not die. The beloved disciple is the witness of all of it: "This is the disciple who is bearing witness about these things, and who has written these things, and we know that his testimony is true." And then the Gospel ends with a smile as wide as the world: "Now there are also many other things that Jesus did. Were every one of them to be written, I suppose that the world itself could not contain the books that would be written." Twenty-one chapters, seven signs, one finished work — and the author, an old man who was there for all of it, tells you the world is not big enough to hold the library of what he left out. We have what we need. We do not have all there is. Believe, and have life — and there is always more of Him.

Key verses

"Jesus said to them, 'Come and have breakfast.'" — John 21:12 (ESV)

"He said to him, 'Lord, you know everything; you know that I love you.' Jesus said to him, 'Feed my sheep.'" — John 21:17 (ESV)

"Jesus said to him, 'If it is my will that he remain until I come, what is that to you? You follow me!'" — John 21:22 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of John 21 is that after denial, a man can quietly quit — drift back to the boats and the old life, and fish all night and catch nothing. But the good news is that the risen Lord builds a fire on the shore, feeds His failures breakfast before He asks them anything, counts three loves for three denials, and hands the shepherd his sheep back. Restoration is not probation. It is breakfast, and then a flock, and then two words: follow Me.

A note from the Reformers. Calvin pointed to Peter's fall and restoration as God's deliberate provision for weak consciences: the worst fall in the Gospels, by the chief apostle, is written down together with its pardon, so that no believer who has denied his Lord in a weak hour need despair of being received again. If Peter could be restored to shepherding, there is hope for every fallen sheep — and shepherd.

Reflection question: Jesus restored Peter publicly, gently, and by name. Is there a failure you believe has quietly disqualified you, that the risen Lord is ready to restore you from — and feed you first?
