Jesus in John

A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

The Hope Connection
Lewis Fuller

"but these are written so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that by believing you may have life in his name." — John 20:31 (ESV)

Introduction — John's Gospel at a Glance

John's Gospel does not begin in Bethlehem. It begins before time. Matthew starts with a family tree, Mark with a voice in the wilderness, Luke with an angel in the temple — but John starts in eternity: "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God." This is the Gospel that soars highest. It is also, strangely, the Gospel that stoops lowest — to a wedding that has run out of wine, to a woman drawing water alone at noon, to feet in a basin, to a weeping woman in a garden before dawn. John holds both together on every page: the eternal Son of God, and the flesh-and-blood Friend of sinners. The Word became flesh. That is John's whole book in four words, and it is the wonder the church has never gotten over.

Who wrote it. The church has always believed this Gospel came from John the apostle, the son of Zebedee, the brother of James — the disciple whom Jesus loved, the one who leaned back against Jesus at the Last Supper and asked who the betrayer would be. John was an eyewitness. He says so himself, plainly, at the cross: "He who saw it has borne witness — his testimony is true, and he knows that he is telling the truth — that you also may believe" (John 19:35). He stood at the foot of the cross when the others had fled, and Jesus gave His own mother into John's care from the cross itself. John outlived all the other apostles, and he wrote this Gospel as an old man, looking back over a lifetime of walking with Jesus. That is why this book reads the way it does — not hurried, like Mark, but deep and still, like a well. An old man who has thought about these things for fifty or sixty years is telling you what they mean.

Who it was written for. John wrote for everyone who would ever believe — and for everyone who might. This is the most evangelistic of the four Gospels. John tells us his purpose outright, near the end: these things are written "so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that by believing you may have life in his name" (John 20:31). He wrote to produce faith in those who do not yet have it, and to deepen faith in those who do. The Gospel was most likely written from Ephesus, around AD 85–95, late in the first century, after the temple in Jerusalem had already been destroyed. John explains Jewish customs and translates Hebrew and Aramaic words for readers who would not know them, because his readers are the whole world — Jew and Gentile, the religious and the ruined, the Nicodemuses who come by night and the Samaritan women who come at noon. If you are wondering whether Jesus is really God, really enough, and really for you, this Gospel was written to answer you.

The Word made flesh. John's name for Jesus before Bethlehem is the Word — in Greek, Logos. The Word is God's own reason, speech, and self-giving: the One by whom everything was made, in whom life and light live. And then comes the sentence the whole Bible leans toward:

"And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we have seen his glory, glory as of the only Son from the Father, full of grace and truth." — John 1:14 (ESV)

God did not send information about Himself. He sent Himself, in flesh. He pitched His tent among us — that is what "dwelt" means — and He was full of grace and truth, both of them, all the way, in one Person.

Seven signs. John does not call Jesus' miracles "miracles," mostly. He calls them signs — in Greek, sēmeion. A sign is not a spectacle. A sign points beyond itself to something greater. John selects seven great signs and sets them like signposts along the road of the first half of his Gospel: water turned to wine at Cana (chapter 2), a dying boy healed at a distance (chapter 4), a lame man walking at Bethesda (chapter 5), five thousand fed (chapter 6), Jesus walking on the sea (chapter 6), a man born blind given sight (chapter 9), and — the greatest of them — Lazarus called out of a four-days tomb (chapter 11). Read the signs rightly, John says, and they will tell you who Jesus is, so that you believe.

The "I AM" sayings. Seven times in this Gospel Jesus takes God's own covenant name onto His lips — the name spoken from the burning bush, "I AM WHO I AM" (Exodus 3:14). I am the bread of life (6:35). I am the light of the world (8:12). I am the door of the sheep (10:9). I am the good shepherd (10:11). I am the resurrection and the life (11:25). I am the way, and the truth, and the life (14:6). I am the true vine (15:1). And once, without any picture attached at all, He says it bare: "Before Abraham was, I am" (8:58) — and the hearers pick up stones, because they understand exactly what He is claiming. Every "I AM" is a sermon in itself, and this commentary will stop at each one and listen.

Long discourses. Where Mark hurries from deed to deed, John lingers over words. Whole chapters here are conversations: a night talk with Nicodemus, a noon talk at a well, long teaching in the temple courts, and then — the treasure of the church — the farewell discourse of chapters 13–17, spoken on the night He was betrayed, ending in the great prayer of John 17, where we are allowed to listen in on the Son praying to the Father. If you want to hear Jesus explain Himself, John is your Gospel.

The hour of glory. John has a phrase that tolls through the book like a bell: the hour. For the first half of the Gospel, the hour has not yet come (2:4; 7:30; 8:20). Then, in chapter 12, Jesus announces, "The hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified" — and He is talking about the cross. In John's Gospel the cross is not a defeat that the resurrection has to fix. The cross is the glory. The lifting up of the Son of Man is His enthronement. When Jesus cries "It is finished" and bows His head, John wants you to see a King completing His work, not a victim expiring. The hour of glory is the cross — and then the empty tomb is the Father's Amen to it.

The key verse. John hands us his purpose in his own words, and there is no better summary of this Gospel — or of why this commentary exists:

"but these are written so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that by believing you may have life in his name." — John 20:31 (ESV)

Believe, and have life. That is the whole errand of the book. And the theme running through every chapter is this: Jesus in John is the eternal Word made flesh — the I AM, the Light of the World, the Resurrection and the Life.

Read in the Reformation way. This commentary reads John the way the Reformers taught the church to read every book of the Bible, and John's Gospel may be the Reformers' best friend in all of Scripture. Sola scriptura — Scripture alone is our rule; Jesus Himself says, "You search the Scriptures... and it is they that bear witness about me" (5:39), and John wrote his Gospel down so that faith would rest on the written Word. Sola gratia — grace alone saves; John opens by telling us God's children are born "not of blood nor of the will of the flesh nor of the will of man, but of God" (1:13), and that from Christ's fullness "we have all received, grace upon grace" (1:16). Sola fide — faith alone receives that grace; John's verb, more than a hundred times, is believe — "For God so loved the world, that he gave his only Son, that whoever believes in him should not perish but have eternal life" (3:16) — and Jesus leaves no room for any other way in: "No one can come to me unless the Father who sent me draws him" (6:44). Solus Christus — Christ alone is the Savior; "No one comes to the Father except through me" (14:6) is the Lord's own words, and the Reformers simply refused to add a second door. And soli Deo gloria — glory to God alone; even doubting Thomas ends on his knees with the fullest confession in the Gospels: "My Lord and my God!" (20:28). Along the way you will meet brief nods to Luther, Calvin, and Spurgeon where they genuinely shed light on a passage — a line or two, never a lecture. And in every chapter the law and the gospel are kept distinct, the way the Reformers insisted: the law shows us our sin and our need (the bad news), and the gospel shows us Christ and His finished work (the good news). Mix those two up and you lose both. Keep them straight and John's Gospel will do what it was written to do — give you life in His name.

How to use this commentary. Each chapter section begins with "Jesus in this chapter" — a short look at how Christ is revealed there. Then the commentary walks the chapter's movements in order, explaining the text plainly and fully. Key verses are quoted in full from the English Standard Version. Each chapter closes with "The bad news and the good news" — the law-then-gospel pattern, the weight of the chapter and the hope in Christ — and one reflection question to carry into prayer. Read it slowly. Read it with your Bible open. And remember, as you read, that this is not a book about a dead teacher. This is the living Christ, the eternal Word made flesh, meeting you where you are.

How to Use This Commentary

This commentary was built to be used, not just read. Here is how to get the most out of it.

The order of every session. Work through each chapter the same way, in this order:

  1. Read the chapter in your own Bible first. Open your Bible to the chapter of John and read it all the way through before you read a word of commentary. The commentary serves the Scripture; it never replaces it. Read it out loud if you can — John wrote for hearers as much as readers, and so much of this Gospel is spoken words that you will catch things with your ears that your eyes slide past.
  2. Then read the commentary section for that chapter. Keep your Bible open beside it and look up the verses as they come. Start with "Jesus in this chapter" — before anything else, fix your eyes on how Christ is revealed there. Charles Spurgeon said a passage is not finished until Christ has been seen in it, and that is the aim of every section here.
  3. Then sit with the reflection question. Each chapter ends with one reflection question and a "bad news and good news" paragraph. Do not rush past them. Read the bad news honestly — let the law do its searching work — and then read the good news slowly, out loud if you can. Answer the reflection question in prayer, out loud to God, not just in your head.
  4. Write in a notebook. Keep a simple notebook with this study. Write down your answer to the reflection question, your answer to the study guide question for that chapter (see the John Study Guide below), and the memory verse for the chapter. A truth is not really yours until you have written it, prayed it, and lived it. You do not need anything fancy — a dollar notebook and a pen will do the whole Gospel.

The rhythm: three sessions a week. This fits the study rhythm used across The Hope Connection: three sessions a week, one book at a time, until the book is done. At one chapter per session, three sessions a week, you will walk through all twenty-one chapters of John in seven weeks. If you want to go slower — and slower is often better — spread one chapter across the whole week: session one, read the chapter in your Bible and the "Jesus in this chapter" opening; session two, read the commentary; session three, return to the chapter, work through the questions, and pray it back to God. Either pace is fine. What matters is the steadiness, not the speed.

No catch-up piles. This rule is not negotiable, because it protects the whole habit: if you miss a day, you do not owe anybody two chapters. You simply pick up at the next chapter at the next session. Life happens — sickness, family, work, hard weeks. A study rhythm that piles up guilt every time life interrupts will not survive a month. This one bends and keeps going. Missed a week? Start again at the next chapter. The Lord is not keeping a tally, and neither are we.

Using it alone. Everything above is written for one reader, one Bible, one notebook, and the Lord. Pray before you open the book — a short, real prayer: "Lord, open my eyes, and let this Word search me." Pray again when you finish, turning the chapter's good news into thanksgiving and its bad news into confession.

Using it with a spouse or a friend. Read the chapter out loud together at the start of the session — take turns reading. Then read the commentary, or take turns reading it aloud too. Talk through the reflection question and the study guide question together, honestly. Pray for each other out of what the chapter showed you. John is a Gospel about abiding in Jesus together — the branches are never alone on the vine — and two people studying it together will see things one reader alone will miss.

Using it in a small group. One chapter per meeting. Have someone read the chapter aloud from the Bible to open. Walk through the commentary section together, stopping wherever the text raises a question. Save real time at the end for the reflection question and the study guide question — that is where study becomes life. Close in prayer, and let the chapter's memory verse be the group's verse for the week: read it together at the start of the next meeting and see who can say it from memory. Keep the group small enough that everybody talks, and keep the door open — John's Gospel is good news for people who have never opened a Bible, and every chapter here is written plain enough for a brand-new believer to follow.

The memory verses. The study guide below gives one memory verse for each chapter — twenty-one verses, the spine of John's Gospel. If you take one verse a week alongside the chapter rhythm, by the time you finish the book you will carry its whole story in your heart: the Word made flesh, the new birth, the living water, the Bread of Life, the Good Shepherd's unbreakable grip, the Resurrection and the Life, "It is finished," and life in His name. That is treasure no one can take from you.

Study Questions — One per Chapter

Work each question in your notebook after the chapter's reflection question. Many of them follow the Bible's own pattern of putting off the old and putting on the new (Ephesians 4:22–24; Colossians 3:8–10).

  1. John 1 — Receive the birth-gift. John says God's children are born of God, not of human will. Put off trying to manufacture your own new life; put on receiving it as a gift. Where have you been trying to build a new life by effort that Jesus offers to give you by new birth — and what would receiving look like this week?
  2. John 2 — Bring Him what has run dry. The wine ran out at Cana, and Jesus filled the lack with better than was lost. Put off hiding your emptiness and managing appearances; put on bringing your lack to Him plainly. What has run dry in your life — joy, strength, hope — that you will name to Him in prayer this week?
  3. John 3 — Stop polishing, start believing. Nicodemus had religion, Scripture, and sincerity, and Jesus told him he needed a birth, not an improvement. Put off polishing the old life; put on looking to the lifted-up Son. What part of your old life are you still trying to improve when Jesus offers to make you new?
  4. John 4 — Leave the jar. The woman left her water jar behind when she went to tell the town. Put off returning to the same old wells — the habits and comforts that never quench; put on drinking deeply of Christ and telling someone. What is your old well, and who is one person you could tell about the living water this week?
  5. John 5 — Do you want to be made well? Jesus asked a man sick for thirty-eight years if he wanted to be well. Put off settling into a brokenness you have stopped expecting Him to touch; put on hearing His word and rising. Name the long-standing place, and write down one step of obedience you will take at His word this week.
  6. John 6 — Love the Giver more than the gift. The crowd wanted bread that perishes and walked away from the Bread of Life. Put off seeking Jesus mainly for what He gives; put on coming to Him for who He is. What good gift of God have you been tempted to love more than the Giver — and how will you come to Him this week simply because He is enough?
  7. John 7 — Come and drink. Jesus cries His invitation to anyone who thirsts. Put off satisfying your thirst everywhere except at His feet; put on coming to Him daily and drinking. What thirst have you been carrying to other places, and what will a daily coming to Him look like this week?
  8. John 8 — Free indeed. The Son's freedom is real freedom, not a better-managed slavery. Put off managing a chain — hiding it, pacing it, making peace with it; put on bringing it to the Son to be broken outright. Name the chain, and name one person who will stand with you while you walk free of it.
  9. John 9 — Say your one thing. The healed man's whole theology fit in one sentence about what Jesus had done for him. Put off waiting until you can answer every question; put on simple witness. Write your "one thing I do know" in one sentence — and say it to one person this week.
  10. John 10 — Rest in His grip. Jesus says His sheep are held in His hand and the Father's. Put off trusting your grip on God; put on resting in His grip on you. When you feel yourself slipping, whose grip are you trusting? Write down John 10:28–29 and carry it with you this week.
  11. John 11 — Bring Him your "if only." Both sisters met Jesus with "Lord, if you had been here." Put off holding your grief at a distance from Him; put on bringing it to the One who wept at the grave Himself. Where do you need to know that the Resurrection is not only a future day but a Person standing in front of you now?
  12. John 12 — Let the grain fall. Jesus says fruit comes through a grain that falls and dies. Put off holding back the part of your life God is asking you to lay down; put on losing it for His sake and finding it multiplied. What are you holding back from the ground — and what is one step of letting go this week?
  13. John 13 — Wash feet without keeping score. Jesus washed the feet of men who would deny, scatter, and betray Him — and loved them to the end. Put off serving only those who deserve it or repay it; put on towel-and-basin love. Whose feet, literally or not, is He calling you to wash this week?
  14. John 14 — Take His road, not yours. Jesus says He is the way — not a way among ways. Put off trying to reach God, peace, or worth by a road of your own making; put on trusting the Person who is the road. Where are you still mapping your own way, and what would trusting Him there look like today?
  15. John 15 — Remain. Jesus' one command in this chapter is to abide. Put off the hurry and noise that pull you away from Him; put on staying close — His words in you, prayer, obedience. What pulls you away, and what would staying close look like this week, practically, one day at a time?
  16. John 16 — Take the peace He actually promised. Jesus promised trouble and peace in the same breath. Put off expecting a trouble-free life; put on the peace of the One who has overcome the world. Where do you need to stop demanding that the trouble leave before you will take His peace?
  17. John 17 — He prayed for you. Jesus prayed for everyone who would believe through the apostles' word — that includes you. Put off praying as if you were on your own; put on remembering you are prayed for by the Son Himself. What does it do to your heart to know He prayed for you by category before you were born — and how will you pray for another believer this week the way He prayed for you?
  18. John 18 — Stay by the right fire. Peter warmed himself at the wrong fire and denied the Lord in small sentences. Put off going quiet about Jesus where comfort and fear press you; put on standing with Him openly. Where do comfort and fear most tempt you to go quiet — and what is one plain sentence of witness you will not swallow this week?
  19. John 19 — Stop paying a paid debt. "It is finished" means nothing is left for you to pay. Put off trying to pay God back for what Christ finished; put on resting in the receipt. What debt do you keep trying to pay — guilt you rehearse, penance you perform — as if the receipt were not already stamped?
  20. John 20 — Believe without seeing. Jesus blessed those who have not seen and yet have believed — that is you, reading words on a page. Put off demanding a sign before you will trust; put on believing His word about the fear or doubt He is meeting today. Name the doubt, and write Thomas's confession under it: "My Lord and my God!"
  21. John 21 — Let Him restore you. Jesus restored Peter publicly, gently, and by name — three loves for three denials. Put off the belief that a failure has quietly disqualified you; put on following Him again and feeding His sheep. What failure do you believe has sidelined you, and what is the first small act of service He is handing back to you this week?
