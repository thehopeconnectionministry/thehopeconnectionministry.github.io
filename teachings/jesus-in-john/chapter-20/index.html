John 20 — Peace Be with You: The Empty Tomb and Thomas's Confession

Jesus in John — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Gospel of John
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

In John 20, the risen Jesus reveals Himself by coming through locked doors with peace on His lips, calling a weeping woman by name, meeting an honest doubter with His wounds, and blessing everyone who will ever believe without seeing. John closes the chapter by telling us why the whole book exists: that you may believe, and believing, have life in His name.

Commentary

While it was still dark (20:1–10). "Now on the first day of the week Mary Magdalene came to the tomb early, while it was still dark, and saw that the stone had been taken away from the tomb." The resurrection chapter opens in the dark, with a woman weeping at a tomb. Mary — out of whom Jesus cast seven demons, who stood at the cross when it was dangerous and at the tomb before it was light — runs to Simon Peter and to John with the only explanation grief can construct: "They have taken the Lord out of the tomb, and we do not know where they have laid him." The bad news is that even resurrection morning does not begin with faith; it begins with sorrow, fear, and doubt — and the risen Jesus meets every single one, personally, before the chapter ends. The two disciples run. John outruns Peter — the young man is faster — but stops at the entrance and looks in: the linen cloths lying there. Peter, true to form, goes straight in. He sees the cloths, and the face cloth "folded up in a place by itself" — not the disorder of grave robbers, who do not fold, but the calm of a Man who rose and set His wrappings in order. Then John enters, "and he saw and believed; for as yet they did not understand the Scripture, that he must rise from the dead." Saw and believed — faith is born in John at the sight of an empty tomb and folded cloth, before any appearance. The disciples go home. Mary stays. Staying is about to matter.

Rabboni (20:11–18). Mary stands weeping outside the tomb, bends to look in, and sees two angels in white sitting where the body had lain. "Woman, why are you weeping?" "Because they have taken away my Lord, and I do not know where they have laid him." She turns around and sees Jesus standing — and does not know it is Jesus, because grief and tears are poor opticians, and because the risen body is the same and more than the same. "Woman, why are you weeping? Whom are you seeking?" Supposing Him to be the gardener — and in the deepest sense she is righter than she knows; the second Adam is indeed the Gardener of the new creation — she pleads: "Sir, if you have carried him away, tell me where you have laid him, and I will take him away." She is offering to carry a corpse. Love at the end of its strength makes impossible offers. Jesus answers with one word — her name: "Mary." The Shepherd calls His own sheep by name (chapter 10), and the sheep knows the voice. "She turned and said to him in Aramaic, 'Rabboni!' (which means Teacher)." One word from Him does what angels and evidence could not. "Do not cling to me, for I have not yet ascended to the Father; but go to my brothers and say to them, 'I am ascending to my Father and your Father, to my God and your God.'" My Father and your Father — not "our Father" lumped together, because His sonship is by nature and ours by grace, and the distinction is itself the gospel: the ascended Son brings His brothers into His own relation with the Father. And the first preacher of the resurrection is a woman with a past, sent to apostles: "Mary Magdalene went and announced to the disciples, 'I have seen the Lord' — and that he had said these things to her." The church's Easter proclamation begins on the lips of Mary Magdalene. Let no one tell you your past disqualifies your witness.

Peace be with you (20:19–23). "On the evening of that day, the first day of the week, the doors being locked where the disciples were for fear of the Jews, Jesus came and stood among them and said to them, 'Peace be with you.'" Locked doors are no barrier to the risen body, and fear is no barrier to His coming. He does not knock and wait for courage. He comes, He stands, He speaks — and His first word to the men who fled and denied is not rebuke but peace. "When he had said this, he showed them his hands and his side. Then the disciples were glad when they saw the Lord." The wounds are kept — the risen body carries the cross's marks as credentials and as glory. Peace is not a mood here; it is a verdict grounded in those wounds: the debt is paid, the war is over, God is reconciled. Then the commission: "Peace be with you. As the Father has sent me, even so I am sending you." And He breathes on them — the breath of the Creator in Genesis, the wind of Ezekiel's valley — "Receive the Holy Spirit," a first installment and pledge of Pentecost. "If you forgive the sins of any, they are forgiven them; if you withhold forgiveness from any, it is withheld." The church is entrusted with the keys of the gospel's announcement: to declare, on Christ's authority, the forgiveness He purchased — and to warn, honestly, where it is refused. The peace He brought through the door is the peace they are sent out the door to announce.

My Lord and my God (20:24–29). Thomas was not there. "So the other disciples told him, 'We have seen the Lord.' But he said to them, 'Unless I see in his hands the mark of the nails, and place my finger into the mark of the nails, and place my hand into his side, I will never believe.'" Thomas's doubt is honest doubt — the doubt of a man who loved much and was hurt much, who in chapter 11 was ready to die with Jesus and in chapter 14 confessed he did not know the way. He states his terms and stays with the company — give him that; he does not leave the fellowship over his doubt. Eight days later, doors locked again, Jesus comes again, and His first word is again peace. Then He turns to Thomas with an invitation that must have undone the man: "Put your finger here, and see my hands; and put out your hand, and place it in my side. Do not disbelieve, but believe." Jesus had heard the terms — spoken in a locked room He was not visibly in — and He meets them one by one. He does not shame the doubter. He offers the wounds. Whether Thomas touched, John does not say; the sight and the voice are enough, and Thomas answers with the fullest confession in all four Gospels, the confession toward which this whole book has been building since "the Word was God":

"Thomas answered him, 'My Lord and my God!'" — John 20:28 (ESV)

Not "the Lord and God" in general — my Lord and my God, personal, possessive, surrendered. And Jesus accepts the worship without a syllable of correction — the final proof, from the doubter's own mouth, of who He is. Then the blessing that lands on every reader of this Gospel ever since: "Have you believed because you have seen me? Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have believed." That is you, reading words on a page. Jesus pronounced a blessing over you, by category, in that locked room.

Why this book was written (20:30–31). John closes with the Gospel's colophon, and it is the sentence this commentary took for its front door: "Now Jesus did many other signs in the presence of the disciples, which are not written in this book. But these are written so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that by believing you may have life in his name." Selection with a purpose: not everything, but these — these signs, these words, these wounds — chosen and written so that readers who never saw Him would do what Thomas did: believe, and have life. The book is not a biography for the curious. It is an instrument of life for the believing.

Key verses

"Jesus said to her, 'Mary.' She turned and said to him in Aramaic, 'Rabboni!' (which means Teacher)." — John 20:16 (ESV)

"Jesus came and stood among them and said to them, 'Peace be with you.'" — John 20:19 (ESV)

"Thomas answered him, 'My Lord and my God!'" — John 20:28 (ESV)

"but these are written so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that by believing you may have life in his name." — John 20:31 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of John 20 is that resurrection morning begins with a weeping woman, frightened men behind locked doors, and a disciple who will not believe without touching — sorrow, fear, and doubt are the raw materials Easter starts with. But the good news is that the risen Jesus meets every one personally: He calls Mary by name, He comes through locked doors speaking peace to the men who fled, He offers His wounds to the doubter, and He blesses those who believe without seeing — which is to say, He blesses you.

A note from the Reformers. Faith Alone — John states the purpose of his whole Gospel in the language of the Reformation: believe, and have life in His name. Thomas believed because he saw; the blessing of verse 29 rests on those who believe because they have heard — which is to say, because they have read. This book exists so that you may believe. That is not our claim for it. It is John's.

Reflection question: Jesus blessed "those who have not seen and have believed" — that is you, reading words on a page. What fear or doubt of yours is He meeting today the way He met Mary's tears and Thomas's terms?
