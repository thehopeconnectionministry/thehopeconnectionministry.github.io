John 8 — The Son Makes You Free: The Light of the World and Before Abraham Was, I Am

Jesus in John — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Gospel of John
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

In John 8, Jesus reveals Himself as the Light of the world, who exposes slaves of sin and sets them free. The argument is the sharpest in the Gospel, and it lands on respectable, religious people: everyone who practices sin is a slave to sin — but if the Son sets you free, you will be free indeed. And at the chapter's summit, He takes God's own name onto His lips: "Before Abraham was, I am."

Commentary

A note on John 7:53–8:11 — the woman caught in adultery. Honesty requires a brief word here, in the same spirit as the note on the ending of Mark in this series, and there is no cause for alarm in it. The beloved passage that opens this section of John — the woman caught in adultery, the scribes and Pharisees dragging her before Jesus, His writing on the ground, "Let him who is without sin among you be the first to throw a stone at her," and His final word to her, "Neither do I condemn you; go, and from now on sin no more" — is not found in the earliest manuscripts of John's Gospel. It appears in later copies, sometimes at a different place in John, and sometimes in Luke; the earliest church writers commenting on John pass over it, while the church of later centuries received it and has read it with profit ever since. Its picture of Christ — His refusal to be trapped between law and mercy, His silencing of accusers who must examine themselves, His word of pardon joined to a call to holiness — is true to the Jesus of the undisputed chapters, and nothing in it teaches anything not taught plainly elsewhere in Scripture. So the church has received it, and we may read it the same way: gratefully, and honestly. No doctrine is built on it alone, and our faith rests on Christ, not on any single paragraph. With that said plainly, we can read it for all it is worth.

Neither do I condemn you (7:53–8:11). The trap is set in the temple, in public, with a woman's life as the bait. She has been caught in adultery — caught, note, without the man, which tells you the aim was never justice. "Teacher, this woman has been caught in the act of adultery. Now in the Law, Moses commanded us to stone such women. So what do you say?" If He says stone her, His mercy is a fraud and Rome's law is broken besides; if He says spare her, He is against Moses. Jesus bends down and writes on the ground — the only time in Scripture God writes with His finger twice, once on stone at Sinai and once in dust in the temple. Pressed, He straightens up: "Let him who is without sin among you be the first to throw a stone at her." And one by one, beginning with the older ones, they go away — conscience doing in silence what argument could not. Jesus is left alone with the woman. "Woman, where are they? Has no one condemned you?" "No one, Lord." And Jesus says the sentence that is the gospel in two halves: "Neither do I condemn you; go, and from now on sin no more." Pardon first, then holiness — never the reverse order, and never pardon without the call. He does not say her sin did not matter. He says she is not condemned, and that her life must now turn. That is how the Son deals with caught sinners. It is how He has dealt with every one of us who came to Him with no defense.

The Light of the world (8:12–20). Then, in the temple treasury, likely with the feast's great lamps still in view, Jesus makes the second I AM claim:

"I am the light of the world. Whoever follows me will not walk in darkness, but will have the light of life." — John 8:12 (ESV)

Light does two things: it shows the way, and it shows what is in the room. Jesus promises both. The Pharisees challenge His testimony as self-witness, and He answers with the witness of the Father: "In your Law it is written that the testimony of two people is true. I am the one who bears witness about myself, and the Father who sent me bears witness about me." They ask, "Where is your Father?" — and His answer is the knife of the chapter: "You know neither me nor my Father. If you knew me, you would know my Father also." A man's knowledge of God is measured by his reception of Jesus. There is no knowing the Father around the Son. John adds the quiet note of providence again: "no one arrested him, because his hour had not yet come."

You will die in your sins (8:21–30). Jesus warns them plainly: "I am going away, and you will seek me, and you will die in your sin. Where I am going, you cannot come." To die in your sin — singular, the great sin of unbelief, carrying all the others with it — is the most terrible sentence in the chapter, and He speaks it to religious men. "You are from below; I am from above. You are of this world; I am not of this world. I told you that you would die in your sins, for unless you believe that I am he you will die in your sins." Unless you believe that I am — there the name stands again, egō eimi (ἐγώ εἰμι, Greek): "I AM," God's covenant name from the burning bush (Exodus 3:14), taken onto Jesus' lips. Faith is not believing Jesus is a fine teacher; it is believing He is I AM. He promises them a sign they are not expecting: "When you have lifted up the Son of Man, then you will know that I am he" — the cross itself will be the revelation. And John records a mercy inside the argument: "As he was saying these things, many believed in him." Even in the sharpest chapter, the Word gathers people.

The truth will set you free (8:31–38). To those who had believed, Jesus gives the test of real discipleship: "If you abide in my word, you are truly my disciples, and you will know the truth, and the truth will set you free." Abiding — staying, remaining, living in His word — is the mark; a start is not the same as a stay. Their reply reveals how thin their believing was: "We are offspring of Abraham and have never been enslaved to anyone. How is it that you say, 'You will become free'?" Never enslaved — said by a nation that had been enslaved in Egypt, exiled in Babylon, and was at that moment occupied by Rome. Self-deception about slavery is slavery's best defense. Jesus answers with the chapter's law, and it lands on respectable people as hard as on ruined ones:

"Truly, truly, I say to you, everyone who practices sin is a slave to sin." — John 8:34 (ESV)

Practices — keeps on doing, lives in — sin, and he is not a free man misbehaving; he is a slave obeying. "The slave does not remain in the house forever; the son remains forever. So if the Son sets you free, you will be free indeed." A slave can be put out of the house any day. The Son remains — and the Son can emancipate. His freedom is not parole, not management, not a better class of chains. Free indeed. Real freedom, from the only One with the standing to grant it.

Whose children? (8:39–47). The argument descends to parentage, and Jesus will not let bloodline substitute for likeness. "If you were Abraham's children, you would be doing the works Abraham did, but now you seek to kill me, a man who has told you the truth." Abraham rejoiced to see Christ's day; his supposed children plot Christ's death. "You are doing the works your father did" — and at last He names the father plainly: "You are of your father the devil, and your will is to do your father's desires. He was a murderer from the beginning, and does not stand in the truth, because there is no truth in him. When he lies, he speaks out of his own character, for he is a liar and the father of lies." These are among the hardest words Jesus ever spoke, and He spoke them not to pagans but to the religious establishment of God's own people. The test of parentage is not profession but practice: whose desires do you do? Whose words do you believe? "Whoever is of God hears the words of God. The reason why you do not hear them is that you are not of God." Hearing is the family resemblance.

Before Abraham was, I am (8:48–59). They answer with slur — "Are we not right in saying that you are a Samaritan and have a demon?" — and Jesus, unruffled, keeps His honor with the Father: "If I glorify myself, my glory is nothing. It is my Father who glorifies me." Then the promise that makes them certain He is mad: "Truly, truly, I say to you, if anyone keeps my word, he will never see death." They protest: Abraham died, the prophets died — "Who do you make yourself out to be?" And Jesus answers by letting Abraham himself testify: "Your father Abraham rejoiced that he would see my day. He saw it and was glad." The patriarch, from the far side of death, rejoiced over the day of Christ — Genesis' promises, the ram in the thicket, the blessing to all nations, all of it aimed here. "You are not yet fifty years old, and have you seen Abraham?" And then the summit of the chapter, and one of the highest sentences in Scripture: "Truly, truly, I say to you, before Abraham was, I am." Not "I was." I am. Abraham came into being; Jesus is being itself, the eternal I AM of the burning bush, standing in a temple court in a body not yet fifty years old. "So they picked up stones to throw at him, but Jesus hid himself and went out of the temple." The stones are the proof the hearers understood exactly what He was claiming. Nobody stones a man for claiming to be a good teacher. They stone a man for claiming to be God — if it is not true. John has spent eight chapters showing you it is true.

Key verses

"I am the light of the world. Whoever follows me will not walk in darkness, but will have the light of life." — John 8:12 (ESV)

"So if the Son sets you free, you will be free indeed." — John 8:36 (ESV)

"Jesus said to them, 'Truly, truly, I say to you, before Abraham was, I am.'" — John 8:58 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of John 8 is that everyone who practices sin is a slave to sin — respectable slaves, religious slaves, slaves who insist they have never been enslaved — and that those who refuse the Son die in their sins. But the good news is that the Son remains in the house forever, and if the Son sets you free, you will be free indeed — not managed, not paroled, free — and the One who sets you free is no mere teacher but the great I AM, the Light of the world, in whose light no one who follows Him walks in darkness.

A note from the Reformers. Luther's little book On the Freedom of a Christian opens with this chapter's paradox: a Christian is a perfectly free lord of all, subject to none — and a perfectly dutiful servant of all, subject to everyone. The freedom is the Son's gift (verse 36); the service is its fruit. A freed man can afford to serve. A slave can only perform.

Reflection question: The Son's freedom is real freedom, not a better-managed slavery. What chain have you been managing — hiding, pacing, making peace with — that Jesus wants to break outright?
