John 1 — The Word Became Flesh: In the Beginning Was the Word

Jesus in John — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings

Jesus in this chapter

Before Bethlehem there is forever. John 1 shows Jesus as the eternal Word — with God and God before anything was made, the Maker of all things, the Life and the Light of men — who then, in the fullness of time, became flesh and lived among us, full of grace and truth. Everyone who receives Him becomes a child of God, born not of man's will but of God's. And the chapter ends the way the whole Gospel will run: with ordinary men hearing a voice, coming, seeing, and following.

Commentary

The Word in eternity (1:1–5). John opens with the deepest sentence in his Gospel:

"In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God." — John 1:1 (ESV)

The word is Logos (λόγος, Greek) — the Word. It is John's name for Jesus before Bethlehem: God's own reason, speech, and self-giving, the One by whom everything was made and in whom life and light live. God does not send information about Himself. He sends the Word — Himself, in flesh. Notice the three truths packed into one verse. The Word was — He already existed when the beginning began; He did not come into being. The Word was with God — a real Person, face to face with the Father, in eternal fellowship. And the Word was God — not a lesser god, not an angel, not a created helper, but God Himself. "He was in the beginning with God. All things were made through him, and without him was not any thing made that was made." Everything — galaxies and sparrows, angels and ants — was made through Him. If it was made, He made it; therefore He Himself was not made.

"In him was life, and the life was the light of men. The light shines in the darkness, and the darkness has not overcome it." Hold onto that word shines — present tense. The light is still shining. The darkness has tried everything — Herod's sword, the Pharisees' plots, a cross, a sealed tomb — and it has not overcome the light, and it will not.

The witness (1:6–8, 19–28). "There was a man sent from God, whose name was John." John the Baptist is the first of this Gospel's great chain of witnesses, and John the apostle is careful to fence his role: "He was not the light, but came to bear witness about the light." John the Baptist knew his place, and there is freedom in knowing yours. He was a voice, not the Word; a witness, not the Light; a friend of the bridegroom, not the Bridegroom. When the priests and Levites come from Jerusalem to interrogate him — "Who are you?" — he confesses freely: "I am not the Christ." Not Elijah, not the Prophet. "I am the voice of one crying out in the wilderness, 'Make straight the way of the Lord,' as the prophet Isaiah said." And he points past himself to the One standing among them whom they do not know, the strap of whose sandal John is not worthy to untie. Every true witness does what John does: he gets smaller and points bigger.

Received and rejected (1:9–13). "The true light, which gives light to everyone, was coming into the world. He was in the world, and the world was made through him, yet the world did not know him. He came to his own, and his own people did not receive him." There is the bad news, walking in quietly in the first chapter: the Maker came to His own world, to His own people, and was refused. The darkness's first instinct is refusal — and that is our story too, every one of us. Left to ourselves, we do not receive Him.

"But to all who did receive him, who believed in his name, he gave the right to become children of God, who were born, not of blood nor of the will of the flesh nor of the will of man, but of God." This is the Reformers' charter, written by an apostle. God's children are not born of blood — you do not inherit this from your parents. Not of the will of the flesh — you cannot manufacture it by effort. Not of the will of man — no one can decide it for you or produce it in you. Born of God. The new birth is God's gift before it is ever our experience. Receiving is believing in His name — empty hands, a trusted Person.

The Word became flesh (1:14–18). Then the verse the whole Bible leans toward:

"And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we have seen his glory, glory as of the only Son from the Father, full of grace and truth." — John 1:14 (ESV)

Became flesh — not seemed to be flesh, not visited flesh, but became it, truly, while never ceasing to be God. Dwelt among us — the word means He pitched His tent, His tabernacle, among us; the glory that once filled the tabernacle in the wilderness now walks around in a body. Full of grace and truth — both, undiluted. Grace without truth becomes softness that never saves; truth without grace becomes hardness that never heals. In Jesus they are one Person. "For from his fullness we have all received, grace upon grace" — grace in place of grace, wave after wave on a shore, one gift arriving as another recedes, forever. "For the law was given through Moses; grace and truth came through Jesus Christ." The law is not called bad — it was given, and it was God's. But grace and truth did not merely get given. They came, in a Person. "No one has ever seen God; the only God, who is at the Father's side, he has made him known." If you want to know what God is like, John says, do not guess. Look at Jesus.

Behold the Lamb (1:29–34). The next day John sees Jesus coming toward him and says the words that echo through the whole Bible:

"Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!" — John 1:29 (ESV)

Lamb reaches back to Passover, to Abraham's promise on the mountain — "God will provide for himself the lamb" — and to Isaiah's suffering Servant, led like a lamb to the slaughter. And notice: He takes away the sin — singular, the whole mass of it — of the world. John testifies that at the baptism he saw the Spirit descend from heaven like a dove and remain on Jesus. Prophets had visits from the Spirit; on Jesus the Spirit stays. "And I have seen and have borne witness that this is the Son of God."

Come and see (1:35–51). The Gospel's first disciples come the way disciples still come: by a witness pointing, and by an invitation to look. Two of John's disciples hear "Behold, the Lamb of God!" and follow Jesus. Jesus turns and asks the first question He asks in this Gospel — and it is a searching one: "What are you seeking?" They ask where He is staying, and He gives the Gospel's great invitation: "Come and you will see." Christianity is not first an argument to win but a Person to come and see. Andrew finds his brother Simon first — the first evangelist in John is a brand-new believer fetching his own family — and says, "We have found the Messiah." Jesus looks at Simon and renames him on sight: "You shall be called Cephas" — Peter, the rock. He knows who you are, and He knows who you will be, and the second knowledge governs His dealings with you.

Philip follows, and Philip finds Nathanael: "We have found him of whom Moses in the Law and also the prophets wrote, Jesus of Nazareth." Nathanael's answer is famous and blunt: "Can anything good come out of Nazareth?" Philip does not argue. He borrows his Lord's words: "Come and see." And Jesus, seeing Nathanael coming, honors the skeptic's honesty: "Behold, an Israelite indeed, in whom there is no deceit!" Nathanael is stunned that Jesus knew him under the fig tree before Philip called him, and he confesses, "Rabbi, you are the Son of God! You are the King of Israel!" Jesus promises him greater things: heaven opened, and the angels of God ascending and descending on the Son of Man — Jacob's ladder, with a Person standing where the ladder stood. Jesus is the meeting place of heaven and earth. He was under no fig tree when He said it. He is the ladder Himself.

Key verses

"In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God." — John 1:1 (ESV)

"But to all who did receive him, who believed in his name, he gave the right to become children of God." — John 1:12 (ESV)

"And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we have seen his glory, glory as of the only Son from the Father, full of grace and truth." — John 1:14 (ESV)

"Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!" — John 1:29 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of John 1 is that the Light came into the world He made, came to His own people, and was not received — the darkness in us refuses before it believes, and none of us is born a child of God by blood, effort, or will. But the good news is that the Word became flesh anyway, full of grace and truth, and to all who receive Him — who believe in His name — He gives the right to become children of God, born of God Himself. The Lamb of God has come to take away the sin of the world. Come and see.

A note from the Reformers. Grace Alone is John's opening charter: children of God are born of God (verse 13), grace is piled on grace (verse 16), and the law given through Moses stands in permanent contrast to the grace and truth that came through Jesus Christ. Everything in this Gospel is gift before it is ever duty.

Reflection question: Jesus' first question in this Gospel is "What are you seeking?" If you answered Him honestly — not religiously, honestly — what are you seeking from Him right now, and will you take His answer, "Come and you will see"?
