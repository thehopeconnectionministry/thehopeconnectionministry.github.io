John 10 — No One Will Snatch Them: The Door and the Good Shepherd

Jesus in John — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Gospel of John
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

In John 10, Jesus reveals Himself as the Door of the sheep and the Good Shepherd who lays down His life for them. The sheep are His — called by name, known, given eternal life, and held in an unbreakable double grip: His hand and the Father's. No wolf, thief, or death can pry them loose.

Commentary

The sheepfold and the thief (10:1–6). Jesus carries the blind man's expulsion straight into a picture. A sheepfold in that country was an enclosure with one door, and the shepherd himself often slept across the opening — he was the door. "Truly, truly, I say to you, he who does not enter the sheepfold by the door but climbs in by another way, that man is a thief and a robber. But he who enters by the door is the shepherd of the sheep. To him the gatekeeper opens. The sheep hear his voice, and he calls his own sheep by name and leads them out." In the mixed folds of the East, many flocks spent the night in one enclosure, and in the morning each shepherd simply called — and his own sheep separated out at his voice, following a sound they knew in their bones. That is how personal this is. He calls His own by name. Not by number, not by category — by name. "When he has brought out all his own, he goes before them, and the sheep follow him, for they know his voice. A stranger they will not follow, but they will flee from him, for they do not know the voice of strangers." John notes, with gentle honesty, that the hearers did not understand the figure of speech. So Jesus opens it wider, twice.

I am the door (10:7–10). "Truly, truly, I say to you, I am the door of the sheep." The door is the only lawful way in, and it is a Person. "All who came before me are thieves and robbers, but the sheep did not listen to them. I am the door. If anyone enters by me, he will be saved and will go in and out and find pasture." Saved, secure, and fed — in and out, at home in both directions, pasture provided. Then the mission statement of the Shepherd, set against the thief's: "The thief comes only to steal and kill and destroy. I came that they may have life and have it abundantly." Every false shepherd, every false gospel, every addiction and lie that ever courted you has the thief's three verbs: steal, kill, destroy. Jesus' verbs are life, and life abundantly — more than survival, a life with overflow. Test every voice by its verbs.

I am the good shepherd (10:11–18). Then the claim that has comforted more dying saints than almost any other:

"I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep." — John 10:11 (ESV)

Good — not merely competent, but beautiful, noble, true. And the proof of His goodness is not His skill but His death. "He who is a hired hand and not a shepherd, who does not own the sheep, sees the wolf coming and leaves the sheep and flees, and the wolf snatches them and scatters them. He flees because he is a hired hand and cares nothing for the sheep." The bad news is that the sheep have enemies and hirelings: false shepherds always abandon the flock at the moment of danger, because the sheep are not theirs. The good news is the Shepherd who does the opposite — He runs toward the wolf. "I am the good shepherd. I know my own and my own know me, just as the Father knows me and I know the Father; and I lay down my life for the sheep." The knowing between shepherd and sheep is patterned on the knowing between Father and Son. That is the depth of it. "And I have other sheep that are not of this fold. I must bring them also, and they will listen to my voice. So there will be one flock, one shepherd." The other sheep are the Gentiles — the world of chapter 3, the Samaritans of chapter 4, you and me — gathered not into a second fold but into one flock, because there is one Shepherd. And His death is wholly voluntary, a matter of authority, not accident: "No one takes it from me, but I lay it down of my own accord. I have authority to lay it down, and I have authority to take it up again. This charge I have received from my Father." The cross is not the wolf winning. It is the Shepherd choosing.

Held in two hands (10:19–30). Division follows, as always — some say demon, some say these are not the words of a man oppressed. At the Feast of Dedication, in winter, in Solomon's Colonnade, they surround Him: "How long will you keep us in suspense? If you are the Christ, tell us plainly." His answer goes to the root: "I told you, and you do not believe. The works that I do in my Father's name bear witness about me, but you do not believe because you are not among my sheep." Unbelief is not a conclusion from evidence; it is a condition of not belonging. Then He describes His sheep, and gives the promise on which the doctrine of the perseverance of the saints stands:

"My sheep hear my voice, and I know them, and they follow me. I give them eternal life, and they will never perish, and no one will snatch them out of my hand. My Father, who has given them to me, is greater than all, and no one is able to snatch them out of the Father's hand. I and the Father are one." — John 10:27–30 (ESV)

Read the grip carefully. The sheep are in the Son's hand. The sheep are in the Father's hand. It is not two competing holds; it is one unbreakable double grip — His hand and the Father's, and no one, nothing, no wolf or thief or death or the sheep's own wandering strength, is able to snatch them out. Lewis's own signature line says it as plainly as it can be said: "I am not held up by my grip on God. I am held by His grip on me." John 10 is where that line lives. The sheep persevere not because sheep are strong but because the Shepherd is. Your security is not the strength of your holding on. It is the strength of the hands holding you.

Stones again (10:31–42). "The Jews picked up stones again to stone him" — for blasphemy, "because you, being a man, make yourself God." Jesus answers from their own Scripture (Psalm 82:6, "I said, you are gods") — if the word of God could call mere men "gods" without blasphemy, how much more the One the Father consecrated and sent — and then returns to the evidence: "If I am not doing the works of my Father, then do not believe me; but if I do them, even though you do not believe me, believe the works." They seek to arrest Him again, and He escapes — the hour still governs — and withdraws across the Jordan to where John first baptized. And there, away from the temple courts and the stones, "many came to him... And many believed in him there." The Shepherd's voice keeps gathering sheep, in the quiet places, one by one.

Key verses

"The thief comes only to steal and kill and destroy. I came that they may have life and have it abundantly." — John 10:10 (ESV)

"I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep." — John 10:11 (ESV)

"I give them eternal life, and they will never perish, and no one will snatch them out of my hand." — John 10:28 (ESV)

"My Father, who has given them to me, is greater than all, and no one is able to snatch them out of the Father's hand. I and the Father are one." — John 10:29–30 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of John 10 is that the sheep have enemies — thieves who steal and kill and destroy, wolves that snatch and scatter, and hired hands who run at the moment of danger because the sheep are not theirs. But the good news is the Shepherd who runs the other way: who calls His own by name, lays down His life for the sheep of His own accord, gives them eternal life, and holds them in His hand and the Father's hand, from which no one is able to snatch them. Ever.

A note from the Reformers. The Reformers' doctrine of the perseverance of the saints stands on chapters like this one. The sheep persevere not because sheep are strong but because the Shepherd is. Your grip on God was never the ground of your safety; His grip on you is — and His hand is greater than all.

Reflection question: Jesus says His sheep are held in His hand and the Father's. When you feel yourself slipping, whose grip are you trusting — yours on Him, or His on you?
