John 7 — Rivers of Living Water: At the Feast of Tabernacles

Jesus in John — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Gospel of John
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

In John 7, Jesus reveals Himself as the Rock of the wilderness standing in the temple — the true source of living water. At the Feast of Tabernacles, amid whispered argument, family unbelief, and plots to seize Him, He stands up on the last and greatest day and cries the invitation to anyone who thirsts: come to Me and drink, and rivers will flow where dryness was.

Commentary

Even His brothers did not believe (7:1–9). The chapter opens in Galilee, because Judea has become dangerous: "the Jews were seeking to kill him." The Feast of Booths — Tabernacles — is near, the great autumn harvest festival when Israel lived in tents to remember the wilderness years. Jesus' brothers give Him worldly career advice: "Leave here and go to Judea, that your disciples also may see the works you are doing. For no one works in secret if he seeks to be known openly. If you do these things, show yourself to the world." It sounds like support. John strips the varnish off: "For not even his brothers believed in him." The bad news of this chapter starts at home — how close a person can live to Jesus and still not believe. Familiarity can harden as surely as distance. These are the men who grew up at His table, and they are treating Him as a publicity problem. Jesus answers with His settled submission to the Father's clock: "My time has not yet come... You go up to the feast. I am not going up to this feast, for my time has not yet fully come." He will go — but on heaven's schedule, not on His brothers' marketing plan. (His brothers' story is not over, by the way. After the resurrection, James the brother of Jesus becomes a pillar of the church. Unbelief at home is not the end of the story for a family member — keep praying.)

Teaching in the temple (7:10–24). Jesus goes up privately, and halfway through the feast He goes into the temple and begins teaching openly. The Jews marvel: "How is it that this man has learning, when he has never studied?" He has no rabbinic diploma, no named teacher, no school. His answer relocates all authority: "My teaching is not mine, but his who sent me. If anyone's will is to do God's will, he will know whether the teaching is from God or whether I am speaking on my own authority." That is a promise worth underlining: obedience opens understanding. The man who sets his will to do God's will will find the light to know. The one who waits to understand everything before obeying anything waits forever. Jesus exposes the leaders' inconsistency — they circumcise on the Sabbath to keep Moses' law, yet rage at Him for making a man's whole body well on the Sabbath (the Bethesda healing of chapter 5 still rankles). "Do not judge by appearances, but judge with right judgment." Right judgment looks past the surface rule to the mercy at the heart of the Lawgiver. The crowd is divided and muttering: some say He is a good man, others that He leads the people astray, "yet for fear of the Jews no one spoke openly of him." Fear of man runs through this chapter like a draft through a house — and it keeps mouths shut that ought to confess Him.

Can this be the Christ? (7:25–36). Some Jerusalemites ask the dangerous question out loud: "Is not this the man whom they seek to kill? And here he is, speaking openly, and they say nothing to him! Can it be that the authorities really know that this is the Christ?" Their objection is folklore: "We know where this man comes from" — Nazareth, Joseph's house — "but when the Christ appears, no one will know where he comes from." Jesus answers in the temple with a cry: "You know me, and you know where I come from? But I have not come of my own accord. He who sent me is true, and him you do not know. I know him, for I come from him, and he sent me." They think knowing His hometown is knowing Him. Knowing where a man is from is not knowing a man. They seek to arrest Him, "but no one laid a hand on him, because his hour had not yet come." That sentence is John's quiet theology of providence: plots are real, hatred is real, and none of it can move one minute ahead of the Father's hour. Yet many of the people believe: "When the Christ appears, will he do more signs than this man has done?" It is a fair question, and it has never been answered against Him. Jesus announces His soon departure — "I will be with you a little longer, and then I am going to him who sent me. You will seek me and you will not find me. Where I am you cannot come" — and the saying confuses them into talk of the Dispersion. He is talking about the Father's house. They are talking about geography. That gap between His meaning and their hearing runs through the whole Gospel, and only the cross and the Spirit close it.

Rivers of living water (7:37–39). "On the last day of the feast, the great day" — the day when, by long tradition, the priest drew water from the pool of Siloam and poured it out at the altar, remembering the rock in the wilderness that gave water to a thirsting nation — Jesus stands up and cries out. John rarely says Jesus cried out. This is a shout in the temple courts:

"If anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink. Whoever believes in me, as the Scripture has said, 'Out of his heart will flow rivers of living water.'" — John 7:37–38 (ESV)

If anyone — the invitation is as wide as thirst. Let him come to me — not to a ceremony, not to a system, but to a Person. And drink — faith pictured as drinking, taking Him in. And the result is not survival but overflow: not drops, not a cup, but rivers of living water flowing out of the believer's heart. John, the careful evangelist, pauses to explain: "Now this he said about the Spirit, whom those who believed in him were to receive, for as yet the Spirit had not been given, because Jesus was not yet glorified." The rivers wait for the cross and the empty tomb and Pentecost — but they are coming, and they will run through ordinary believers in ordinary towns, Samaria and beyond, to the end of the earth. The Rock that followed Israel in the wilderness is standing in the temple, offering Himself as the water.

Division and the officers' report (7:40–52). The word divides the crowd, as the Word always does. "This really is the Prophet." "This is the Christ." Others object on geography — Scripture says the Christ comes from Bethlehem, and this man is from Galilee — an objection that a single question to the eyewitnesses could have settled, since He was in fact born in Bethlehem; but men who have decided rarely check. The officers sent to arrest Him return empty-handed, and their report is the finest sermon review ever given: "No one ever spoke like this man!" The Pharisees rage — "Are you also deceived? Have any of the authorities or the Pharisees believed in him? But this crowd that does not know the law is accursed." Contempt for ordinary people is doing their theology for them. And then a quiet voice inside the council: Nicodemus — the night visitor of chapter 3, growing bolder by inches — asks, "Does our law judge a man without first giving him a hearing and learning what he does?" They sneer him down: "Are you from Galilee too?" The chapter ends in stalemate for the council and in shame for its fairness. But note the trajectory of Nicodemus: night visitor, then procedural defender, then — in chapter 19 — a man carrying a king's weight of burial spices in broad daylight. Grace grows men slowly, and it grows them surely.

Key verses

"If anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink." — John 7:37 (ESV)

"Whoever believes in me, as the Scripture has said, 'Out of his heart will flow rivers of living water.'" — John 7:38 (ESV)

"The officers answered, 'No one ever spoke like this man!'" — John 7:46 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of John 7 is that a person can grow up in Jesus' own house and not believe in Him, that fear of man can silence a whole crowd, and that religious leaders can call the thirsty crowd accursed rather than point it to water. But the good news stands up on the last and greatest day of the feast and cries out: if anyone thirsts — anyone — let him come to Jesus and drink, and out of his heart will flow rivers of living water. Not drops. Rivers. The Spirit Himself, given when Jesus was glorified, turns dry hearts into springs.

A note from the Reformers. The Reformers pointed to verse 17 as the order of all true knowledge of God: the will set to obey comes before the understanding that sees. Faith is not the reward of the man who has figured God out; it is the light by which he comes to know anything at all.

Reflection question: Jesus cries His invitation to "anyone" who thirsts. What thirst in your life have you been trying to satisfy everywhere except at His feet?
