John 6 — The Bread of Life: Five Thousand Fed and a Hard Saying

Jesus in John — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Gospel of John
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

In John 6, Jesus reveals Himself as the Bread of Life — the true manna who came down from heaven. He feeds five thousand with a boy's lunch, walks on the sea in the dark, and then offers the crowd not more bread but Himself. Many walk away. Peter's confession stands for every believer who has ever stayed: "Lord, to whom shall we go? You have the words of eternal life."

Commentary

Five thousand fed (6:1–15). Jesus crosses the Sea of Galilee, and a large crowd follows because they saw the signs. It is near the Passover. Lifting up His eyes and seeing the crowd coming, Jesus tests Philip: "Where are we to buy bread, so that these people may eat?" Philip does the math of unbelief: "Two hundred denarii worth of bread would not be enough for each of them to get a little." Andrew finds a boy — John alone tells us it was a boy — with five barley loaves and two fish, and asks the honest question of small resources: "But what are they for so many?" Barley was the bread of the poor. A child's lunch. Jesus takes it, gives thanks, and distributes it — and John makes sure we see the abundance: "And when they had eaten their fill, he told his disciples, 'Gather up the leftover fragments, that nothing may be lost.' So they gathered them up and filled twelve baskets with fragments." Five thousand men fed, and the leftovers outmeasure the lunch. The God who provides does not deal in barely-enough. The crowd, stomachs full and minds on revolution, wants to take Him by force and make Him king — a bread-king, a stomach-king. Jesus withdraws to the mountain by Himself. He will be King, but on His Father's road, which runs through a cross, not around it.

Walking on the sea (6:16–21). Evening comes, and the disciples row out into a rising storm. "When they had rowed about three or four miles, they saw Jesus walking on the sea and coming near the boat, and they were frightened. But he said to them, 'It is I; do not be afraid.'" In the Greek, It is I is egō eimi — I AM. The divine name walks on the water in the dark. The God who made the sea is not endangered by it, and He comes to His frightened people in the night watch, on top of the very thing that terrifies them. "Then they were glad to take him into the boat, and immediately the boat was at the land to which they were going." Storm, presence, arrival. John will do the same for you.

Seeking bread that perishes (6:22–34). The crowd hunts Jesus down in Capernaum, and He tells them the truth about their motives — He always does: "Truly, truly, I say to you, you are seeking me, not because you saw signs, but because you ate your fill of the loaves." The bad news is how quickly full stomachs replace hungry hearts. Then the command: "Do not work for the food that perishes, but for the food that endures to eternal life, which the Son of Man will give to you." They ask what work God requires, and His answer overturns their whole religion of earning: "This is the work of God, that you believe in him whom he has sent." The one work God asks is faith — and even that is called God's work. They demand a sign like the manna and quote Scripture at Him: "He gave them bread from heaven to eat." Jesus corrects the tense and the giver: "It was not Moses who gave you the bread from heaven, but my Father gives you the true bread from heaven. For the bread of God is he who comes down from heaven and gives life to the world." And they say, in the prayer this Gospel keeps putting on human lips, "Sir, give us this bread always." Like the woman at the well — give me this water. The ask is right. The understanding lags behind, and Jesus is patient with the gap.

I am the bread of life (6:35–51). Then the first great I AM with a picture:

"Jesus said to them, 'I am the bread of life; whoever comes to me shall not hunger, and whoever believes in me shall never thirst.'" — John 6:35 (ESV)

Coming is believing; believing is feeding. And underneath the invitation stands the Father's keeping hand, the bedrock of this chapter: "All that the Father gives me will come to me, and whoever comes to me I will never cast out. For I have come down from heaven, not to do my own will but the will of him who sent me. And this is the will of him who sent me, that I should lose nothing of all that he has given me, but raise it up on the last day." Lose nothing. The Son's errand is the Father's will, and the Father's will is that not one of the given ones is lost — and the guarantee ends at resurrection: "I will raise him up on the last day." The crowd grumbles at the claim — "Is this not Jesus, the son of Joseph, whose father and mother we know?" Familiarity breeds contempt in Capernaum as surely as in Nazareth. Jesus answers with the drawing of God: "No one can come to me unless the Father who sent me draws him" (6:44). Our coming is real, and it is also God's work from first to last — grace alone, in the Lord's own teaching. "Everyone who has heard and learned from the Father comes to me." And then He presses the bread image all the way to the cross: "I am the living bread that came down from heaven. If anyone eats of this bread, he will live forever. And the bread that I will give for the life of the world is my flesh." The manna generation ate and died. This Bread gives His own flesh, and those who feed on Him live forever.

Eat my flesh — and the great leaving (6:52–71). The saying gets harder before it gets clearer: "Unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man and drink his blood, you have no life in you. Whoever feeds on my flesh and drinks my blood has eternal life, and I will raise him up on the last day." Jesus is speaking, in the strongest possible picture-language, of receiving Him wholly by faith — taking His sacrificed life into yourself as your very life, the way food becomes the eater. The offense is deliberate: He will not be sampled; He must be fed on. "It is the Spirit who gives life; the flesh is no help at all. The words that I have spoken to you are spirit and life." And then the saddest verse in the chapter: "After this many of his disciples turned back and no longer walked with him." Not enemies — disciples. The saying was too hard, the bread too strange, the cost too high. Crowds are easy; discipleship is not. Jesus turns to the Twelve with a question that must have cost Him to ask: "Do you want to go away as well?" And Peter answers for every believer who has ever stayed through a hard season:

"Lord, to whom shall we go? You have the words of eternal life, and we have believed, and have come to know, that you are the Holy One of God." — John 6:68–69 (ESV)

Peter does not say he understands everything. He says there is nowhere else to go. That is mature faith: not full comprehension, but a settled direction. Even here, though, John darkens the edge — Jesus answers, "Did I not choose you, the twelve? And yet one of you is a devil." Judas is at the table of the Bread of Life, and his heart is already leaving. Proximity is not possession. A man can handle holy things for years and never feed on them.

Key verses

"Jesus said to them, 'I am the bread of life; whoever comes to me shall not hunger, and whoever believes in me shall never thirst.'" — John 6:35 (ESV)

"All that the Father gives me will come to me, and whoever comes to me I will never cast out." — John 6:37 (ESV)

"Simon Peter answered him, 'Lord, to whom shall we go? You have the words of eternal life.'" — John 6:68 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of John 6 is that men will follow Jesus for bread that perishes and walk away from the Bread of Life when His words get hard — full stomachs and empty hearts, and even disciples turning back. But the good news is that the Father gives a people to the Son, and everyone the Father gives comes, and whoever comes He will never cast out, and He will lose nothing — not one — but raise them up on the last day. He is the Bread. Come, feed, and live.

A note from the Reformers. Spurgeon preached on "whoever comes to me I will never cast out" (verse 37) as one of the widest-open doors in the Bible. No case is excluded, no past is too heavy, no comer is refused. He urged sinners to try the promise the way you try a bridge — by putting your whole weight on it.

Reflection question: The crowd wanted bread that perishes and walked away from the Bread of Life. What good gift of God have you been tempted to love more than the Giver — and will you stay, with Peter, when His words get hard?
