John 2 — The Best Wine, Saved for Last: Water into Wine and the Temple Cleansed

Jesus in John — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Gospel of John
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Jesus' public work opens at a wedding and a cleansing. In John 2 He reveals Himself as the Lord of joy who turns empty jars into the best wine, saved for last — the first of His signs, at which His disciples believed in Him — and as the true Temple, the meeting place with God, who speaks of His own body destroyed and raised in three days. Joy and zeal, gentleness and fire, stand side by side in Him from the start.

Commentary

The wedding at Cana (2:1–11). "On the third day there was a wedding at Cana in Galilee, and the mother of Jesus was there. Jesus also was invited to the wedding with his disciples." Do not rush past that. The Son of God's first sign happens not in a temple or a courtroom but at a wedding feast, among ordinary people eating and drinking and dancing. The Christian faith is not against joy. Its Lord opens His public work by providing it.

"When the wine ran out, the mother of Jesus said to him, 'They have no wine.'" In that culture this was more than an empty cupboard; it was a shame hanging over a young couple's marriage feast, a failure of provision everyone would remember. The bad news is in that empty jar: joy fails at our best celebrations, and everything we stock for happiness runs dry eventually. Every earthly wine runs out. Jesus answers His mother with a word that sounds distant to our ears but is respectful in His: "Woman, what does this have to do with me? My hour has not yet come." There is the bell of John's Gospel tolling for the first time — my hour. Jesus will not be managed, even by His mother; He moves on the Father's timetable, toward an hour of glory that is a cross. Mary, to her credit, does not argue. She says to the servants the last recorded words of hers in this Gospel, and they are the best advice anyone ever gave: "Do whatever he tells you."

There stand six stone water jars, used for the Jewish rites of purification — twenty or thirty gallons each. Jesus says, "Fill the jars with water." And they fill them up to the brim. "Now draw some out and take it to the master of the feast." The master tastes the water now become wine and calls the bridegroom aside in amazement: "Everyone serves the good wine first, and when people have drunk freely, then the poor wine. But you have kept the good wine until now." Jesus saves the best for last. That is not only what He did with the wine; it is what He does with lives. The world serves its best first and its dregs last. Christ reverses it. And notice what this sign really is. The word John uses is sēmeion (σημεῖον, Greek) — a sign. A sign is not a spectacle; it points beyond itself to something greater. John's signs are signposts: read them rightly and they tell you who Jesus is, so that you believe. This sign says: the water of the old purification is becoming the wine of the new kingdom, and the One who does it is the true Bridegroom, providing for His people beyond all asking. "This, the first of his signs, Jesus did at Cana in Galilee, and manifested his glory. And his disciples believed in him." The servants knew where the wine came from. The disciples saw the glory. Both were right to marvel.

The temple cleansed (2:12–22). After this He goes down to Capernaum with His mother and brothers and disciples, and then up to Jerusalem for the Passover. "In the temple he found those who were selling oxen and sheep and pigeons, and the money-changers sitting there." The court of the Gentiles — the one place where the nations could come and pray — had been turned into a market, noisy with animals and clinking with exchange fees. "And making a whip of cords, he drove them all out of the temple, with the sheep and oxen. And he poured out the coins of the money-changers and overturned their tables. And he told those who sold the pigeons, 'Take these things away; do not make my Father's house a house of trade.'" Note the tenderness inside the fire: the pigeon-sellers, the poorest traders, get words instead of the whip. And notice whose house it is: "my Father's house." The disciples remember what is written: "Zeal for your house will consume me" (Psalm 69:9).

The Jews demand a sign to justify this authority, and Jesus gives them the sign of signs: "Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up." They scoff — the temple has taken forty-six years to build. "But he was speaking about the temple of his body. When therefore he was raised from the dead, his disciples remembered that he had said this, and they believed the Scripture and the word that Jesus had spoken." Jesus Himself is the true temple. The meeting place between God and man is no longer a building in Jerusalem; it is a body — destroyed by men, raised by God in three days. Every approach to God now runs through Him.

He knew what was in man (2:23–25). "Now when he was in Jerusalem at the Passover Feast, many believed in his name when they saw the signs that he was doing. But Jesus on his part did not entrust himself to them, because he knew all people and needed no one to bear witness about man, for he himself knew what was in man." That is a sobering close to a chapter of wonders. Crowd-belief built on signs alone is thin soil, and Jesus knows it — He knows what is in man. He knows it in the crowds, and He knows it in you, and He came anyway. The next chapter opens with one of those men coming to Him by night. Jesus will not entrust Himself to the crowd's enthusiasm. But He will give Himself, wholly, for the crowd's sin. There is a difference, and the cross is where it shows.

Key verses

"This, the first of his signs, Jesus did at Cana in Galilee, and manifested his glory. And his disciples believed in him." — John 2:11 (ESV)

"And he told those who sold the pigeons, 'Take these things away; do not make my Father's house a house of trade.'" — John 2:16 (ESV)

"Jesus answered them, 'Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up.'" — John 2:19 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of John 2 is that our wine always runs out — our joys, our provisions, our best celebrations empty themselves — and that even God's house, handled by men, gets turned into a marketplace that needs cleansing. But the good news is that Jesus fills the lack with better than was lost: He saves the best for last, and He Himself is the true Temple, destroyed and raised in three days, the meeting place with God that can never be turned into a market or torn down again.

A note from the Reformers. The Reformers loved the order of this chapter: glory revealed, then disciples believing — the sign first, God's gift first, and faith as the response, never the purchase price. That is grace alone and faith alone in a wedding story.

Reflection question: The wine ran out at Cana, and Jesus filled the lack with better than was lost. Where has something run dry in your life that you need to bring, plainly and without dressing it up, to Him?
