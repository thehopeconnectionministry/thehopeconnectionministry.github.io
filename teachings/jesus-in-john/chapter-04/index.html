John 4 — A Well That Never Runs Dry: The Woman at the Well and the Official's Son

Jesus in John — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Gospel of John
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

In John 4, Jesus reveals Himself as the giver of living water — the Well that never runs dry — who crosses every fence of nation, religion, and reputation to reach one thirsty woman, and who says plainly to her, of all people, "I who speak to you am he." The chapter ends with Samaritans confessing Him as the Savior of the world, and a dying boy healed by a word spoken miles away.

Commentary

Weary at the well (4:1–6). Jesus leaves Judea for Galilee, and John tells us, "He had to pass through Samaria." Had to — not by geography alone, for a Jew could go around; by appointment. There is a woman in Samaria with a divine appointment she does not know about. He comes to the town of Sychar, near the field Jacob gave to Joseph, and there is Jacob's well. "Jesus, wearied as he was from his journey, was sitting beside the well. It was about the sixth hour" — noon, the hottest hour. Do not skip the weariness. The eternal Word is tired. He is thirsty. He is sitting alone while the disciples go to buy food. This is what "became flesh" really means: a Savior who knows noon heat and empty stomach and sore feet from the inside.

Living water (4:7–15). "A woman from Samaria came to draw water. Jesus said to her, 'Give me a drink.'" One sentence, and three fences fall at once: Jew and Samaritan, who had no dealings with each other; man and woman, teacher and stranger; holy Rabbi and a woman with a past, drawing water alone at noon — the hour when a woman comes alone because the other women come at other hours. She is startled: "How is it that you, a Jew, ask for a drink from me, a woman of Samaria?" Jesus answers with the offer He made to Nicodemus from the other end of society: "If you knew the gift of God, and who it is that is saying to you, 'Give me a drink,' you would have asked him, and he would have given you living water." If you knew the gift, and who it is. Most of our poverty is ignorance — we do not know the gift, and we do not know the Giver. She answers practically: "Sir, you have nothing to draw water with, and the well is deep. Where do you get that living water? Are you greater than our father Jacob?" Jesus does not argue Jacob. He goes to her thirst:

"Everyone who drinks of this water will be thirsty again, but whoever drinks of the water that I will give him will never be thirsty again. The water that I will give him will become in him a spring of water welling up to eternal life." — John 4:13–14 (ESV)

Every earthly well has the same property: you leave thirsty again. Sin's pleasures, men's approval, the next purchase, the next drink — drink, and thirst, and drink, and thirst. Christ's water is different in kind: it becomes in a person a spring, welling up — not a cistern you haul to daily, but a source inside you that rises on its own because He is in it. Her answer is honest and half-blind and wholly human: "Sir, give me this water, so that I will not be thirsty or have to come here to draw water." She wants the gift as convenience. Jesus, in mercy, will not leave her there.

Go, call your husband (4:16–26). "Jesus said to her, 'Go, call your husband, and come here.'" The conversation about water becomes a conversation about her life, because it has to. "I have no husband," she says. "Jesus said to her, 'You are right in saying, "I have no husband"; for you have had five husbands, and the one you now have is not your husband. What you have said is true.'" Notice: He exposes her without shaming her. He names the whole wreckage in one sentence and does not raise His voice. The law does its work — not to crush her, but to show her why she is thirsty. She does what we all do: she changes the subject to a religious argument. "Our fathers worshiped on this mountain, but you say that in Jerusalem is the place where people ought to worship." Jesus answers her argument for real, because sometimes the argument is the door: "The hour is coming when neither on this mountain nor in Jerusalem will you worship the Father... But the hour is coming, and is now here, when the true worshipers will worship the Father in spirit and truth, for the Father is seeking such people to worship him. God is Spirit, and those who worship him must worship in spirit and truth." Worship is not located in a mountain or a building anymore. It is offered through the Spirit, according to the truth — and the Father is seeking such worshipers. Then she offers her last hedge: "I know that Messiah is coming... When he comes, he will tell us all things." And Jesus says to her the words He has not yet said plainly to anyone in this Gospel — not to a priest, not to a rabbi, but to a Samaritan woman with five husbands at a well at noon: "I who speak to you am he."

The jar left behind (4:27–42). The disciples return and marvel — silently — that He is talking with a woman. "So the woman left her water jar and went away into town." That jar is one of the finest details in the Gospel. She came for water; she found the Well; the jar is forgotten. And her witness is beautifully simple: "Come, see a man who told me all that I ever did. Can this be the Christ?" A question, not a sermon — and a town comes out. Many Samaritans believe because of her word; more believe because they hear Him themselves, and their confession becomes the chapter's crown: "We have heard for ourselves, and we know that this is indeed the Savior of the world." Not the Savior of the Jews only. Not the Savior of the respectable. Of the world. Meanwhile the disciples press food on Him and learn that He has food they do not know about: "My food is to do the will of him who sent me and to accomplish his work." And He lifts their eyes to the fields around them — Samaritans streaming out of the town across the fields at that very moment — "Look, I tell you, lift up your eyes, and see that the fields are white for harvest." Sower and reaper rejoice together. The harvest in Samaria was sown by a woman with a past and an abandoned jar.

The official's son (4:43–54). Back in Cana of Galilee, an official — a royal servant of Herod's court — comes from Capernaum because his son is dying. Rank means nothing at a child's bedside. He begs Jesus to come down and heal his son. Jesus first tests the whole crowd's appetite: "Unless you see signs and wonders you will not believe." The official does not debate; he pleads like a father: "Sir, come down before my child dies." And Jesus heals at a distance, with a word: "Go; your son will live." The man believes the word — mark that; he believes before he sees — and goes. On the road his servants meet him: the boy lives, and the fever left at the seventh hour — the very hour Jesus spoke. "And he himself believed, and all his household." This is the second sign, John says. The first was for disciples at a wedding. This one is for a desperate father who took Jesus at His word. That is still how the healing goes: He speaks, we believe the word, and we walk home to find it true.

Key verses

"but whoever drinks of the water that I will give him will never be thirsty again. The water that I will give him will become in him a spring of water welling up to eternal life." — John 4:14 (ESV)

"God is Spirit, and those who worship him must worship in spirit and truth." — John 4:24 (ESV)

"Jesus said to her, 'I who speak to you am he.'" — John 4:26 (ESV)

"They said to the woman, 'It is no longer because of what you said that we believe, for we have heard for ourselves, and we know that this is indeed the Savior of the world.'" — John 4:42 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of John 4 is thirst: everything we drink leaves us thirsty again, and a life of broken wells — five husbands and a man not her husband — cannot quench the soul, nor can religious argument about which mountain to worship on. But the good news is that Jesus sits down at the well of the thirsty, asks for a drink, and then gives living water that becomes a spring inside a person, welling up to eternal life — and He gives it across every fence men build, to a woman the world had written off, who left her jar and brought a town to the Savior of the world.

A note from the Reformers. Worship "in spirit and truth" (verse 24) was a Reformation banner: worship is not located in a mountain or a building but is offered through the Spirit according to the truth of God's Word. Calvin insisted worship be shaped by what God has commanded rather than what men invent — the woman at the well heard it first from Jesus Himself.

Reflection question: The woman left her water jar behind when she went to tell the town. What have you been carrying back to the same old wells that Jesus is offering to replace with living water?
