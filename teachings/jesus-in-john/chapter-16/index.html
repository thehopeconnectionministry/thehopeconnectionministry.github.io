John 16 — I Have Overcome the World: Sorrow Turned to Joy

Jesus in John — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Gospel of John
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

In John 16, the farewell discourse ends with the plainest pastoral honesty in the Bible. Jesus reveals Himself as the departing Christ who promises His Spirit, prayer answered in His name, and sorrow turned into joy like a birth — and who speaks the victory before the battle: "Take heart; I have overcome the world."

Commentary

I have said these things to keep you from falling away (16:1–4). Jesus prepares His friends for the cost with a candor most leaders would soften: "They will put you out of the synagogues. Indeed, the hour is coming when whoever kills you will think he is offering service to God." Religious murder, done as worship — He names it before it happens so that when it happens they will remember He told them, and not fall away. Forewarning is a form of love. "And they will do these things because they have not known the Father, nor me." All persecution finally reduces to that: not knowing God, while using His name. He admits He did not say these things at the beginning, "because I was with you." While the Shepherd was present, the blows fell on Him. Now He prepares the sheep to stand where He stood.

It is to your advantage that I go away (16:5–15). "But now I am going to him who sent me, and none of you asks me, 'Where are you going?' But because I have said these things to you, sorrow has filled your heart." Sorrow has a way of filling the whole heart so there is no room left for questions. Then the astonishing pastoral claim: "Nevertheless, I tell you the truth: it is to your advantage that I go away, for if I do not go away, the Helper will not come to you. But if I go, I will send him to you." His departure is not loss but upgrade — one embodied Presence, limited to one place, exchanged for the Spirit indwelling every believer everywhere. And the Helper's first work is not comfort but conviction: "And when he comes, he will convict the world concerning sin and righteousness and judgment: concerning sin, because they do not believe in me; concerning righteousness, because I go to the Father, and you will see me no longer; concerning judgment, because the ruler of this world is judged." Notice the order of the Spirit's work: He convicts of sin first — the law before the gospel, the wound before the balm. The Spirit's conviction centers on the one great sin — unbelief in Christ — and announces that righteousness is found in the risen, ascended Christ, and that Satan's case is already decided. Then the Spirit's teaching work: "When the Spirit of truth comes, he will guide you into all the truth... He will glorify me, for he will take what is mine and declare it to you." The Spirit is not a rival teacher with new material. He takes Christ's things and shows them to Christ's people. Test every claimed work of the Spirit by that: does it glorify Jesus? "All that the Father has is mine; therefore I said that he will take what is mine and declare it to you." The Trinity conspires, in the best sense, to put Christ into His people's hands.

Sorrow turned into joy (16:16–24). "A little while, and you will see me no longer; and again a little while, and you will see me." The disciples puzzle over the riddle — a little while, because I go to the Father — and Jesus, knowing they want to ask, answers the sorrow underneath the puzzle: "Truly, truly, I say to you, you will weep and lament, but the world will rejoice. You will be sorrowful, but your sorrow will turn into joy." Not sorrow replaced by joy, as though the grief were discarded; sorrow turned into joy, the same event transfigured — like the cross itself, and like His picture: "When a woman is giving birth, she has sorrow because her hour has come, but when she has delivered the baby, she no longer remembers the anguish, for joy that a human being has been born into the world." The pain is not denied; it is outweighed and reinterpreted by what it brings forth. "So also you have sorrow now, but I will see you again, and your hearts will rejoice, and no one will take your joy from you." I will see you again — the resurrection is promised here as a reunion, and the joy of it is theft-proof: no one will take it. Then prayer is enlarged for the new era: "In that day you will ask nothing of me. Truly, truly, I say to you, whatever you ask of the Father in my name, he will give it to you. Until now you have asked nothing in my name. Ask, and you will receive, that your joy may be full." The departed Christ is not an absent Christ; He is a mediating Christ, and prayer in His name is the church's daily proof that the farewell was not a goodbye.

I have overcome the world (16:25–33). Jesus acknowledges He has spoken in figures of speech, and promises an hour of plain speech about the Father. And the plain speech, when it comes, is mostly about the Father's love: "In that day you will ask in my name, and I do not say to you that I will ask the Father on your behalf; for the Father himself loves you, because you have loved me and have believed that I came from God." Read that twice. The Father himself loves you. Prayer is not persuading a reluctant Father through a willing Son. The Son's mediation is the Father's own provision, because the Father loves His people. The disciples, relieved, confess their faith: "Now we know that you know all things... By this we believe that you came from God." Jesus receives their confession and immediately steadies it with reality: "Do you now believe? Behold, the hour is coming, indeed it has come, when you will be scattered, each to his own home, and will leave me alone. Yet I am not alone, for the Father is with me." He knows they will scatter within hours. He tells them so without bitterness, because His confidence was never in their grip on Him. Then the last word of the discourse, spoken before a single blow has fallen:

"I have said these things to you, that in me you may have peace. In the world you will have tribulation. But take heart; I have overcome the world." — John 16:33 (ESV)

Peace is located: in me. Tribulation is located too: in the world — you will have it; it is promised as surely as the peace. And the ground of courage is a victory announced in the past tense before the battle: I have overcome. He does not say "I will overcome if you hold on." The outcome is not in doubt, and it does not rest on the scattered disciples. It rests on Him. That is the sentence to carry into every hard week: the world gets its tribulation; Christ's people get His peace; and the scoreboard was settled before the game began.

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of John 16 is that trouble in this world is not a malfunction of the Christian life — it is promised: expulsion, hatred, scattering, sorrow while the world rejoices. But the good news is that sorrow turns into joy at His return, the Father Himself loves His people, the Helper convicts and then comforts, and the victory is already spoken in the past tense: take heart — He has overcome the world. Your peace is not the absence of tribulation. It is a Person who has beaten it.

A note from the Reformers. Notice the Spirit's order of work in this chapter: He convicts of sin first (verse 8) — the law before the gospel, the wound before the balm. The Reformers called this the law's chief use, driving sinners to Christ, and they distrusted any preaching that offered comfort to people who had never been convicted. Bad news first, honestly — then the good news lands like rain.

Reflection question: Jesus promised trouble and peace in the same breath. Where do you need to stop expecting a trouble-free life and start taking the peace He actually promised?
