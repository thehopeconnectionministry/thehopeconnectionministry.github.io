John 5 — Passed Out of Death into Life: Healing at Bethesda and the Son's Authority

Jesus in John — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Gospel of John
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

In John 5, Jesus reveals Himself as the Son who does what the Father does — who speaks and a man sick for thirty-eight years walks, and whose voice will one day be heard in every grave. The chapter's great dividing line is His own: some will rise to life and some to judgment, and the one who hears His word and believes has already passed from death to life.

Commentary

The pool of Bethesda (5:1–9). Jesus goes up to Jerusalem for a feast, and John takes us to a place of concentrated misery: "Now there is in Jerusalem by the Sheep Gate a pool, in Aramaic called Bethesda, which has five roofed colonnades. In these lay a multitude of invalids — blind, lame, and paralyzed." A multitude of suffering people, lying in porches, waiting on a superstition about stirred water. Among them is a man who has been an invalid for thirty-eight years — longer than Jesus has been alive in the flesh. Thirty-eight years of lying beside water he believes could heal him if only someone would help him in. When Jesus sees him lying there and knows that he has already been there a long time, He asks him the searching question: "Do you want to be healed?" It sounds almost unkind until you sit with it. A man can build a whole identity around a thirty-eight-year sickness. He can stop expecting. He can settle. The question searches all of us: do you actually want to be made well, or have you made peace with the mat?

The man answers with his hopelessness: "Sir, I have no one to put me into the pool when the water is stirred up, and while I am going another steps down before me." No one. That is the saddest word in the chapter. Jesus does not put him in the pool. He does something better — He makes the pool unnecessary: "Get up, take up your bed, and walk." And at once the man is healed, and he takes up his bed and walks. The voice that made the world speaks a sentence, and thirty-eight years end. But John adds a loaded note: "Now that day was the Sabbath." The healed man's healing becomes a controversy before the chapter is half done, because the religious system would rather have a man on a mat than a rule about mats broken.

Sin no more (5:10–18). The authorities stop the man: "It is the Sabbath, and it is not lawful for you to take up your bed." His defense is unanswerable: "The man who healed me, that man said to me, 'Take up your bed, and walk.'" Later Jesus finds him in the temple — the healed man has gone to the right place — and says to him, "See, you are well! Sin no more, that nothing worse may happen to you." There is something worse than thirty-eight years of paralysis, and Jesus cares more about the man's soul than his legs. Grace received must become holiness pursued; the Lord who heals the body warns the heart. The man reports Jesus to the authorities, "and this was why the Jews were persecuting Jesus, because he was doing these things on the Sabbath." Jesus' answer raises the stakes to the sky: "My Father is working until now, and I am working." God does not stop upholding and healing the world on the Sabbath; the Son claims the Father's own prerogative as His own pattern. John states the result without blinking: "This was why the Jews were seeking all the more to kill him, because not only was he breaking the Sabbath, but he was even calling God his own Father, making himself equal with God." They understood Him perfectly. That is why they wanted Him dead. Equality with God is either blasphemy or the truth, and John 5 is Jesus' great defense of the truth.

The Son does what the Father does (5:19–24). Jesus does not retreat an inch. "Truly, truly, I say to you, the Son can do nothing of his own accord, but only what he sees the Father doing. For whatever the Father does, that the Son does likewise." This is not weakness; it is the eternal life of the Trinity — the Son, perfectly one with the Father, works the Father's works. "For the Father loves the Son and shows him all that he himself is doing. And greater works than these will he show him, so that you may marvel. For as the Father raises the dead and gives them life, so also the Son gives life to whom he will." Raising the dead belongs to God alone in Israel's faith — and Jesus claims it for the Son. "For the Father judges no one, but has given all judgment to the Son, that all may honor the Son, just as they honor the Father. Whoever does not honor the Son does not honor the Father who sent him." There is no honoring God while refusing His Son. The Father will not accept worship that sidesteps Christ. Then comes the chapter's golden sentence, present tense and glorious:

"Truly, truly, I say to you, whoever hears my word and believes him who sent me has eternal life. He does not come into judgment, but has passed from death to life." — John 5:24 (ESV)

Not will pass — has passed. The verdict of the last day is announced ahead of time to everyone who hears and believes. The believer's judgment is behind him, because it fell on Somebody else.

The hour when the dead hear His voice (5:25–29). "Truly, truly, I say to you, an hour is coming, and is now here, when the dead will hear the voice of the Son of God, and those who hear will live." Even now, the spiritually dead hear His voice in the gospel and live — that is the "now here." And beyond it stands the greater hour: "Do not marvel at this, for an hour is coming when all who are in the tombs will hear his voice and come out, those who have done good to the resurrection of life, and those who have done evil to the resurrection of judgment." The voice that raised a sick man off a mat will one day be heard in every grave on earth. Every tomb will empty at that voice — some to life, some to judgment. Jesus speaks of His own return and the general resurrection with the calm of a man describing sunrise. He can, because the voice is His.

The witnesses (5:30–47). Jesus closes His defense by calling His witnesses, and the list is a lesson in how God proves His Son. John the Baptist bore witness — "He was a burning and shining lamp." The works the Father gave Him bear witness. The Father Himself has borne witness. And the Scriptures bear witness: "You search the Scriptures because you think that in them you have eternal life; and it is they that bear witness about me, yet you refuse to come to me that you may have life." That is one of the saddest sentences in the Bible — men searching the Book of life and refusing the Life the Book is about. Jesus will not receive glory from people, and He names the disease under the unbelief: "How can you believe, when you receive glory from one another and do not seek the glory that comes from the only God?" The fear of man is an unbelief factory. And the final witness is the one they trust most: "For if you believed Moses, you would believe me; for he wrote of me. But if you do not believe his writings, how will you believe my words?" Moses himself — the lawgiver — becomes the accuser of those who use Moses to refuse Christ. The whole Old Testament is a witness stand, and every witness points the same direction.

Key verses

"Jesus said to him, 'Get up, take up your bed, and walk.'" — John 5:8 (ESV)

"Truly, truly, I say to you, whoever hears my word and believes him who sent me has eternal life. He does not come into judgment, but has passed from death to life." — John 5:24 (ESV)

"You search the Scriptures because you think that in them you have eternal life; and it is they that bear witness about me." — John 5:39 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of John 5 is that a man can lie helpless for thirty-eight years with no one to help him, that there is something worse than sickness — sin, and the judgment beyond it — and that men can search the Scriptures themselves and still refuse to come to Christ for life. But the good news is present tense and glorious: whoever hears Jesus' word and believes the Father who sent Him has eternal life, does not come into judgment, but has passed from death to life. The voice that raised a sick man off a mat will one day empty every grave — and for the believer, its verdict is already in.

A note from the Reformers. Scripture Alone stands in Jesus' own sentence: "You search the Scriptures, because you think that in them you have eternal life; and it is they that bear witness about me" (verse 39). Scripture's center is Christ; read it to find Him, and it gives life. Read it to replace Him, and even the Bible becomes a locked door.

Reflection question: Jesus asked a man who had been sick for thirty-eight years if he wanted to be made well. Is there a long-standing brokenness in your life where you have stopped expecting Him to act — and what would rising at His word look like?
