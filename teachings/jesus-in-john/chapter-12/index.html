John 12 — Unless a Grain of Wheat Falls: Mary's Oil, Palm Branches, and the Hour

Jesus in John — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Gospel of John
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

In John 12, the last public week opens and the hour arrives. Jesus reveals Himself as the grain of wheat that falls into the earth and dies to bear much fruit, and as the lifted-up Son who draws all people to Himself. A woman's poured-out love fills a house in Bethany; palm branches fill a road in Jerusalem; and Greeks come seeking — and Jesus answers their seeking with a cross.

Commentary

Mary's poured-out love (12:1–8). Six days before the Passover, Jesus comes to Bethany — to the house of the family He raised from grief — and they give a dinner for Him. Martha serves (of course she does; it is her gift, and the resurrection has not retired it), and Lazarus reclines at table — the dead man eating dinner, a quiet wonder John lets us notice ourselves. "Mary therefore took a pound of expensive ointment made from pure nard, and anointed the feet of Jesus and wiped his feet with her hair. The house was filled with the fragrance of the perfume." A pound of pure nard was worth three hundred denarii — a year of a laborer's wages, likely her security, her future in a jar. She breaks it open and pours out all of it, on His feet, and wipes them with her hair — her glory, let down in public, used as a towel. Devotion like this cannot be done halfway or at a discount. The fragrance fills the house; poured-out love always perfumes more than the feet it touches.

Judas Iscariot — John identifies him with cold clarity, "who was about to betray him" — does the accounting: "Why was this ointment not sold for three hundred denarii and given to the poor?" It sounds like stewardship. John, who watched the money box for years, tells us the truth: "He said this, not because he cared about the poor, but because he was a thief, and having charge of the moneybag he used to help himself to what was put into it." The bad news is that a heart can do accounting on worship while it is preparing to sell the Lord for silver — and can sound righteous doing it. Jesus defends her: "Leave her alone, so that she may keep it for the day of my burial. For the poor you always have with you, but you do not always have me." Mary, alone among them, seems to have understood that He was going to die. Her anointing is a burial preparation done in love ahead of time, and Jesus receives it as such. There are acts of devotion that look like waste to everyone but God. Do them anyway.

The plot widens (12:9–11). The crowd comes to Bethany not only for Jesus but to see Lazarus — a walking sermon is hard to ignore — and the chief priests make plans to put Lazarus to death as well, "because on account of him many of the Jews were going away and believing in Jesus." A resurrection becomes a capital offense. When a man's testimony is so alive that the only answer his enemies can find is to kill the evidence, the testimony is doing its work. Pray for that kind of evidence in your own life.

Hosanna (12:12–19). The next day the great crowd at the feast takes branches of palm trees and goes out to meet Him, crying, "Hosanna! Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord, even the King of Israel!" — Psalm 118's words, the pilgrims' psalm, now aimed at one Man. Jesus finds a young donkey and sits on it, exactly as Zechariah wrote: "Fear not, daughter of Zion; behold, your king is coming, sitting on a donkey's colt!" A war-king comes on a horse; this King comes on a colt, in humility, to die. John notes the disciples did not understand at the time — "when Jesus was glorified, then they remembered" — a pattern in this Gospel: understanding often arrives after resurrection, looking back. The crowd that had seen Lazarus raised bears witness, and the Pharisees say to one another in despair, "You see that you are gaining nothing. Look, the world has gone after him." They exaggerate, and they prophesy. The world is indeed about to come after Him — the next verses bring the first installment.

The Greeks and the grain (12:20–26). Some Greeks — God-fearing Gentiles who have come up to worship at the feast — approach Philip with the request that opens the world's door: "Sir, we wish to see Jesus." The nations are coming, just as chapter 10's other sheep promised. And Jesus answers their seeking with the announcement the whole Gospel has been tolling toward: "The hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified." The hour — not of coronation in the temple courts, but of the cross. And He explains it with a seed:

"Truly, truly, I say to you, unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains alone; but if it dies, it bears much fruit." — John 12:24 (ESV)

A grain kept safe in the barn remains one grain, forever alone. A grain buried multiplies beyond counting. Jesus is the grain. His death is not the end of His fruitfulness but the means of it — every believer who has ever lived is fruit of that one buried seed. And the law of the grain governs His followers too: "Whoever loves his life loses it, and whoever hates his life in this world will keep it for eternal life. If anyone serves me, he must follow me; and where I am, there will my servant be also. The Father will honor the one who serves me." Following Jesus means walking the grain's road — down, into the ground, in a thousand daily dyings — with the Father's honor at the end of it.

Troubled, and glorified (12:27–36). Then we are allowed to hear the cost in His own voice: "Now is my soul troubled. And what shall I say? 'Father, save me from this hour'? But for this purpose I have come to this hour. Father, glorify your name." John's Gethsemane is compressed into a breath: a troubled soul, a refused escape, a settled purpose. Heaven answers audibly: "Then a voice came from heaven: 'I have glorified it, and I will glorify it again.'" The crowd hears thunder; some say an angel. Jesus interprets: "Now is the judgment of this world; now will the ruler of this world be cast out. And I, when I am lifted up from the earth, will draw all people to myself." At the cross, the world is judged, Satan is cast out, and the lifted-up Son becomes a magnet for all peoples — the Greeks at the door are only the first. "He said this to show by what kind of death he was going to die." The crowd objects that the Christ remains forever — how can the Son of Man be lifted up (to die)? Jesus answers with an urgent walk-while-you-have-the-light appeal: "The light is among you for a little while longer. Walk while you have the light, lest darkness overtake you... While you have the light, believe in the light, that you may become sons of light." Then He departs and hides Himself from them. The public ministry is closing. The light withdraws from those who would not walk in it — the most solemn judgment in the chapter is not thunder but absence.

Isaiah saw His glory (12:37–50). John closes the public ministry with a theological epitaph. "Though he had done so many signs before them, they still did not believe in him" — and John reaches back seven centuries for the explanation: Isaiah's words, "Lord, who has believed what he heard from us?" and "He has blinded their eyes and hardened their heart." Unbelief, persisted in, becomes a judicial hardening — God giving men over to the darkness they chose in chapter 8. But John adds the wonder: Isaiah wrote those words "because he saw his glory and spoke of him" — Isaiah, in the temple vision of Isaiah 6, saw Christ's glory. The prophet's vision was of this Man. Yet even among the authorities, many believed — but secretly, "for fear of the Pharisees," lest they be put out of the synagogue; "for they loved the glory that comes from man more than the glory that comes from God." Secret discipleship, starved by the love of human glory — John names its root exactly, and it is the same disease as chapter 5: receiving glory from one another. Jesus' last public cry gathers His whole message: "Whoever believes in me, believes not in me but in him who sent me. And whoever sees me sees him who sent me. I have come into the world as light, so that whoever believes in me may not remain in darkness." And His words will judge on the last day the very people who heard them as mere words. Hear them now as life, and they will not meet you then as judgment.

Key verses

"Truly, truly, I say to you, unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains alone; but if it dies, it bears much fruit." — John 12:24 (ESV)

"Now is my soul troubled. And what shall I say? 'Father, save me from this hour'? But for this purpose I have come to this hour. Father, glorify your name." — John 12:27 (ESV)

"And I, when I am lifted up from the earth, will draw all people to myself." — John 12:32 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of John 12 is that a heart can price devotion and call it waste while preparing to sell the Lord, that many can see signs and still not believe, and that some who believe will hide it because they love the glory of man more than the glory of God. But the good news is that the grain has fallen: the Son of Man has been lifted up, the ruler of this world cast out, and the lifted-up Christ draws all people to Himself — Greeks, Samaritans, and every scattered child of God — into a harvest no single grain could imagine.

A note from the Reformers. Calvin and Luther both seized on the grain of wheat as the pattern of the Christian life under the cross: fruitfulness never comes by self-preservation. The life that is kept is lost; the life laid down in Christ is the only one that multiplies.

Reflection question: Jesus says fruit comes through a grain that falls and dies. What in your life are you holding back from the ground that God may be asking you to let die — so that it can bear fruit you cannot yet see?
