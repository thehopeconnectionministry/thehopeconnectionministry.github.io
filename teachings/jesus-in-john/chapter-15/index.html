John 15 — Remain in Me: The True Vine and the World's Hatred

Jesus in John — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Gospel of John
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Walking from the upper room toward the garden, Jesus reveals Himself as the true Vine, and His people as branches whose whole life flows from remaining in Him. Fruit comes from abiding, not striving. And He prepares them for the other side of the union: the world that hated the Vine will hate the branches — but the Helper will testify, and so will they.

Commentary

The true Vine and the Vinedresser (15:1–8). "I am the true vine, and my Father is the vinedresser." The last of the seven I AM sayings gathers up Israel's whole story. In the Old Testament, Israel was God's vine — planted from Egypt (Psalm 80), tended with love, and judged for yielding wild grapes (Isaiah 5). Jesus takes the title and adds true: He is the genuine Vine, the true Israel, in whom the people's calling is fulfilled. And the Father walks His vineyard with pruning shears — which is good news before it is anything else, because pruning is attention, not abandonment. "Every branch in me that does not bear fruit he takes away, and every branch that does bear fruit he prunes, that it may bear more fruit." Both kinds of branch meet the knife: the fruitless is removed; the fruitful is cut so that it bears more. The pruning of a fruitful life is not punishment. It is the Vinedresser's investment in a bigger harvest. When God's shears are on your life, the question is not whether He cares. It is what fruit He is after.

Then the chapter's heartbeat word, spoken again and again: abide. The Greek is menō (μένω) — remain, abide, stay, make your home. It runs through this chapter like a pulse. "Abide in me, and I in you. As the branch cannot bear fruit by itself, unless it abides in the vine, neither can you, unless you abide in me."

"I am the vine; you are the branches. Whoever abides in me and I in him, he it is that bears much fruit, for apart from me you can do nothing." — John 15:5 (ESV)

The bad news is stated without cushion: apart from Me you can do nothing — not "less," not "little that lasts," but nothing of the fruit the Father counts. A severed branch does not merely struggle; it withers, and Jesus does not hide the end of the severed branch: gathered, thrown into the fire, burned — a solemn warning against a merely external attachment to Him, Judas' kind, that never draws life. But the promise side is lavish: "If you abide in me, and my words abide in you, ask whatever you wish, and it will be done for you." Prayer becomes powerful not by technique but by saturation — His words abiding in us until our wishes are His wishes, and then the asking is almost a formality. "By this my Father is glorified, that you bear much fruit and so prove to be my disciples." Fruit is the family proof, and the Father's glory is the harvest's purpose. And note the standing of the branches before any fruit is discussed: "Already you are clean because of the word that I have spoken to you." Clean first — by His word, as in chapter 13's washing — then fruitful. The order never reverses.

Abide in my love (15:9–17). "As the Father has loved me, so have I loved you. Abide in my love." The measure is staggering: the Father's love for the Son is the measure of the Son's love for you. Abiding is not a technique for getting loved; it is staying in a love already given. And the staying has a shape: "If you keep my commandments, you will abide in my love, just as I have kept my Father's commandments and abide in his love." Obedience is not the rent we pay for the room; it is the way we stay in the room and enjoy it. "These things I have spoken to you, that my joy may be in you, and that your joy may be full." The destination of all this abiding and obeying is joy — His joy, full-sized, in us. Then the commandment of chapter 13 returns, now with its full measure named: "This is my commandment, that you love one another as I have loved you. Greater love has no one than this, that someone lay down his life for his friends." Within hours He will define the sentence by doing it. And He re-names His people: "No longer do I call you servants, for the servant does not know what his master is doing; but I have called you friends, for all that I have heard from my Father I have made known to you." Servants get orders; friends get the reasons. The initiative, as always, is His: "You did not choose me, but I chose you and appointed you that you should go and bear fruit and that your fruit should abide." Chosen, appointed, fruitful, lasting — and prayer again: "so that whatever you ask the Father in my name, he may give it to you." The whole section breathes one air: union with Christ is the fountain, and everything else — fruit, joy, answered prayer, love — is the stream.

The world's hatred (15:18–25). Then the weather changes, and He will not let them be surprised: "If the world hates you, know that it has hated me before it hated you. If you were of the world, the world would love you as its own; but because you are not of the world, but I chose you out of the world, therefore the world hates you." Hatred from the world is not a sign that something has gone wrong. It is a sign of whose you are — the branch shares the Vine's treatment as surely as it shares the Vine's life. "Remember the word that I said to you: 'A servant is not greater than his master.' If they persecuted me, they will also persecute you. If they kept my word, they will also keep yours." And the hatred is finally theological, not personal: "Whoever hates me hates my Father also." Jesus exposes the root of it — His works and words leave the world without excuse: "If I had not done among them the works that no one else did, they would not be guilty of sin, but now they have seen and hated both me and my Father." Light rejected becomes guilt compounded; that is the solemn side of chapter 1's promise. "But the word that is written in their Law must be fulfilled: 'They hated me without a cause.'" Even the hatred was foretold (Psalm 69:4). Nothing in the coming night is improvisation.

The Helper will testify (15:26–27). The chapter ends with reinforcement promised for the hated: "But when the Helper comes, whom I will send to you from the Father, the Spirit of truth, who proceeds from the Father, he will bear witness about me. And you also will bear witness, because you have been with me from the beginning." Two witnesses will stand in the hostile courtroom of the world: the Spirit, proceeding from the Father, sent by the Son — the whole Trinity in one verse — and the apostles, whose qualification is simply that they have been with Him from the beginning. The church's witness has never had another engine: the Spirit testifies, and people who have been with Jesus testify, and the two testimonies are one. You do not need to be impressive to be a witness. You need to have been with Him, and to say what you know — like the beggar of chapter 9, now armed with a theology.

Key verses

"I am the vine; you are the branches. Whoever abides in me and I in him, he it is that bears much fruit, for apart from me you can do nothing." — John 15:5 (ESV)

"Greater love has no one than this, that someone lay down his life for his friends." — John 15:13 (ESV)

"You did not choose me, but I chose you and appointed you that you should go and bear fruit and that your fruit should abide." — John 15:16 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of John 15 is that apart from Christ we can do nothing — a severed branch withers — and that the world which hated Him will hate those who are His. But the good news is that the Christian life is not production but connection: the Father prunes the fruitful because He intends more fruit, the Son loves us as the Father loved Him, calls us friends, chose us before we chose Him, and sends the Helper to testify with us. Stay in the Vine, and the life does the rest.

A note from the Reformers. Calvin called union with Christ the fountain from which every benefit flows: we have Christ's righteousness, life, and Spirit not at a distance but because we are in Him, as the branch is in the vine. Take away union, and justification becomes a legal fiction; keep it, and the whole Christian life is Christ living in His people.

Reflection question: Jesus' one command in this chapter is to remain. What pulls you away from abiding in Him, and what would staying close look like this week — practically, one day at a time?
