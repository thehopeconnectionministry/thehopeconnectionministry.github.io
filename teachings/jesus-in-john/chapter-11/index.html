John 11 — I Am the Resurrection and the Life: Lazarus Raised

Jesus in John — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Gospel of John
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

In John 11, Jesus reveals Himself as the Resurrection and the Life in person. At a grave four days sealed, He weeps with the sisters He loves, and then shouts their brother back to life — the greatest of the seven signs, a preview of the morning His own tomb stands empty, and the act that seals the council's decision to kill Him.

Commentary

Lord, he whom you love is ill (11:1–6). "Now a certain man was ill, Lazarus of Bethany, the village of Mary and her sister Martha. It was Mary who anointed the Lord with ointment and wiped his feet with her hair, whose brother Lazarus was ill." John introduces this family by its love, and the sisters' message to Jesus is a model prayer — no demands, no instructions, just the need laid before love: "Lord, he whom you love is ill." Not "he who loves you" — he whom you love. Their appeal rests on His love, not theirs. That is how to pray in a crisis. Jesus' first answer is a promise that frames the whole chapter: "This illness does not lead to death. It is for the glory of God, so that the Son of God may be glorified through it." John then writes the sentence that makes the next verse so hard: "Now Jesus loved Martha and her sister and Lazarus. So, when he heard that Lazarus was ill, he stayed two days longer in the place where he was." So — because He loved them, He stayed. The delay is not indifference; it is love with a purpose they cannot yet see. The bad news of this chapter is not unbelief; it is grief — real grief, in a family Jesus loves, while Jesus waits. If you have ever prayed "Lord, the one You love is ill" and heaven seemed to wait two days, you are in this chapter, and so is His love, in the very same sentence as the waiting.

Going back to Judea (11:7–16). After two days Jesus says, "Let us go to Judea again" — Judea, where they recently tried to stone Him. The disciples protest; Jesus answers with walking-in-the-light wisdom and then says it plainly, in a figure: "Our friend Lazarus has fallen asleep, but I go to awaken him." Our friend. The disciples take Him literally — sleep is good for a sick man — so Jesus tells them plainly: "Lazarus has died, and for your sake I am glad that I was not there, so that you may believe. But let us go to him." Glad — not at the death, but at the believing that is about to be born at a grave. Thomas, so often remembered for chapter 20's doubt, speaks here with grim loyalty: "Let us also go, that we may die with him." It is courage of a sort, and Jesus accepts the company. There are seasons when following Him feels like walking toward death with Him. Thomas went. So can you.

Lord, if you had been here (11:17–27). Jesus arrives to find Lazarus four days in the tomb — four days, in that world, past all hope of revival, the fact stated twice so no one can call it a swoon. Bethany is full of mourners. Martha, the serving sister, hears He is coming and goes out to meet Him — Mary stays in the house — and her first words are the honest grief every praying family has prayed in some form: "Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have died." It is not unbelief. It is faith with a wound in it — she believes in His power and cannot yet see His purpose. "But even now I know that whatever you ask from God, God will give you." Even now. Faith that says "even now" at a four-days grave is faith indeed. Jesus says, "Your brother will rise again." Martha answers with correct doctrine and a distant hope: "I know that he will rise again in the resurrection on the last day." Her theology is right and her comfort is far away — a resurrection at the end of the world does not dry today's tears. So Jesus brings the doctrine home into a Person standing in front of her:

"Jesus said to her, 'I am the resurrection and the life. Whoever believes in me, though he die, yet shall he live, and everyone who lives and believes in me shall never die. Do you believe this?'" — John 11:25–26 (ESV)

The Resurrection is not only a day on a calendar. It is a Man on a road in Bethany. Death, for the believer in Him, is so undone that Jesus can say "though he die, yet shall he live" and "shall never die" in the same breath — the body dies; the person, in Him, does not. And Martha's answer is one of the great confessions of the Gospels, equal to Peter's: "Yes, Lord; I believe that you are the Christ, the Son of God, who is coming into the world." She confesses the Person before she sees the miracle. That order matters.

Jesus wept (11:28–37). Martha calls Mary — "The Teacher is here and is calling for you" — and Mary comes quickly and falls at His feet with the same wounded sentence: "Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have died." When Jesus sees her weeping, and the Jews weeping with her, "he was deeply moved in his spirit and greatly troubled." The Greek carries an edge — a groan, even a shudder of anger at death itself. "Where have you laid him?" "Lord, come and see." And then the shortest verse in the Bible, and one of the fullest:

"Jesus wept." — John 11:35 (ESV)

He weeps at a grave He is about to empty. He knows Lazarus will walk out in minutes, and He weeps anyway — because grief is not a failure of faith, and the tears of His friends are not an interruption of His plan but a place He enters fully. The Son of God does not stand above our sorrow explaining it. He stands in it, wet-faced. Some watching say, "See how he loved him!" Others ask the question grief always asks: "Could not he who opened the eyes of the blind man also have kept this man from dying?" He could have. That is the ache. And the answer is not that He couldn't or didn't care — it is that He had something greater than prevention in mind: resurrection.

Lazarus, come out (11:38–44). Deeply moved again, Jesus comes to the tomb — a cave, with a stone against it. "Take away the stone." Martha, practical even in faith, objects: "Lord, by this time there will be an odor, for he has been dead four days." Jesus answers her with her own lesson returned: "Did I not tell you that if you believed you would see the glory of God?" They take away the stone. Jesus lifts His eyes and prays aloud — for the crowd's sake, He says, "that they may believe that you sent me" — and then cries with a loud voice: "Lazarus, come out." It has often been noted that He had to say the name; a general "come out" from that voice might have emptied every tomb in Bethany. "The man who had died came out, his hands and feet bound with linen strips, and his face wrapped with a cloth. Jesus said to them, 'Unbind him, and let him go.'" The seventh sign, the greatest: a four-days corpse hears, lives, and shuffles out still wearing his grave clothes. Jesus raises him; the community unbinds him. That division of labor still stands — Christ gives the life, and His people help the newly alive out of the old wrappings.

The council decides (11:45–57). Many of the Jews believe in Him. Some carry the report to the Pharisees, and the council meets in fear — not of Rome's theology but of Rome's army: "If we let him go on like this, everyone will believe in him, and the Romans will come and take away both our place and our nation." Caiaphas, the high priest that year, speaks more truth than he knows: "It is better for you that one man should die for the people, not that the whole nation should perish." John pauses to interpret: Caiaphas prophesied, despite himself, "that Jesus would die for the nation, and not for the nation only, but also to gather into one the children of God who are scattered abroad" — the other sheep of chapter 10, gathered by a death. "So from that day on they made plans to put him to death." The raising of Lazarus is the sign that seals the cross. Life for Lazarus means death for Jesus; the exchange is the gospel in miniature — He dies that the dead might live. Jesus withdraws to Ephraim until the Passover approaches, and the chapter ends with the whole city asking whether He will come to the feast, and the chief priests giving orders that anyone who knows where He is must report it. The hunt is on. The hour is near.

Key verses

"Jesus said to her, 'I am the resurrection and the life. Whoever believes in me, though he die, yet shall he live.'" — John 11:25 (ESV)

"Jesus wept." — John 11:35 (ESV)

"When he had said these things, he cried out with a loud voice, 'Lazarus, come out.'" — John 11:43 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of John 11 is grief itself — a brother in a tomb four days, two sisters saying "if you had been here," and a Savior who loved them and still waited two days. But the good news is that the Resurrection is not only a future day; it is a Person who stands at our graves, weeps with us, and then speaks — and the dead hear and live. The raising of Lazarus cost Jesus His own life by the council's decree, and He walked toward that cost with His eyes open. He dies that the dead might live.

A note from the Reformers. The Reformers lingered on Martha: her confession comes before the miracle, resting on Christ's word alone. That is faith alone in a Bethany road — believing the Person while the stone is still against the tomb.

Reflection question: Martha believed in a resurrection at the last day, and Jesus told her the Resurrection was standing in front of her. Where do you need to know that He is not only your future hope but your present Lord — at the grave, in the waiting, in the "if only"?
