John 9 — One Thing I Do Know: The Man Born Blind

Jesus in John — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Gospel of John
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

In John 9, Jesus reveals Himself as the Light of the world in action — opening a beggar's eyes twice: once to see, and once to worship. The man born blind grows bolder under interrogation until his whole theology fits in one sentence: "One thing I do know, that though I was blind, now I see." Meanwhile the men who claim to see choose the dark and call it sight.

Commentary

Neither this man nor his parents (9:1–5). "As he passed by, he saw a man blind from birth." Before the disciples see a man, they see a theology problem: "Rabbi, who sinned, this man or his parents, that he was born blind?" It is the oldest reflex in religion — suffering must be somebody's specific fault, and if we can name the fault we can file the sufferer away. Jesus refuses the whole frame: "It was not that this man sinned, or his parents, but that the works of God might be displayed in him." Not that God made him blind for a show — but that his blindness, met by Christ, would become a display case for the works of God. Some suffering in your life is not a verdict to decode. It is a place where God intends to work, in the open. Jesus adds His working orders: "We must work the works of him who sent me while it is day; night is coming, when no one can work. As long as I am in the world, I am the light of the world." The claim of chapter 8 now gets a demonstration. The Light is about to make light reach a man who has never seen any.

Mud, Siloam, and sight (9:6–12). "Having said these things, he spit on the ground and made mud with the saliva. Then he anointed the man's eyes with the mud and said to him, 'Go, wash in the pool of Siloam' (which means Sent)." The God who formed man from the dust of the ground in Genesis works here with dust and spit like a potter remaking a vessel — the Maker's hands on the broken part. And John translates the pool's name himself, and the translation is the sermon: the blind man washes in the pool called Sent, sent there by the Sent One of God, and comes back seeing. "So he went and washed and came back seeing." Faith in this chapter is a blind man groping his way across a city on the strength of a sentence. He cannot see the pool. He cannot see the way. He can only go — and going, he sees. The neighbors are stunned: is this the beggar who used to sit here? Some say it is he; others say he only looks like him. The man settles it with the first line of his growing testimony: "I am the man." How were your eyes opened? "The man called Jesus made mud and anointed my eyes and said to me, 'Go to Siloam and wash.' So I went and washed and received my sight." Notice his theology at this stage: Jesus is "the man called Jesus." By the end of the chapter he will call Him Lord and worship Him. Testimony grows as a man stands on it.

The interrogation begins (9:13–23). They bring the man to the Pharisees — and John drops the loaded detail: "Now it was a Sabbath day when Jesus made the mud and opened his eyes." Again the Sabbath, again a healing turned into a charge. The Pharisees divide on the spot: "This man is not from God, for he does not keep the Sabbath." Others ask the unanswerable question: "How can a man who is a sinner do such signs?" The healed man, asked his opinion, advances one step: "He is a prophet." The authorities, unwilling to believe the miracle at all, summon the parents: is this your son, was he born blind, how does he see? The parents confirm the son and the blindness and then step back in fear: "Ask him; he is of age; he will speak for himself." John explains: the Jews had already agreed that anyone confessing Jesus as the Christ would be put out of the synagogue — excommunication, the loss of community, worship, and livelihood in one stroke. The parents choose safety. It is a sad, human moment, and John reports it without cruelty: fear of expulsion makes cowards of people who know the truth. Their son, thank God, is made of different stuff — or rather, is being made different by the stuff of grace.

One thing I do know (9:24–34). They summon the man a second time and open with a pious threat: "Give glory to God. We know that this man is a sinner." It is a demand to recant dressed as a call to worship. The beggar's answer has silenced more arguments than a library:

"Whether he is a sinner I do not know. One thing I do know, that though I was blind, now I see." — John 9:25 (ESV)

He does not claim to settle their Christology. He testifies to his experience of Christ's power — and experience anchored in fact is unanswerable. They press for the details again; he grows bold, even wry: "I have told you already, and you would not listen. Why do you want to hear it again? Do you also want to become his disciples?" That stings, and they revile him: "You are his disciple, but we are disciples of Moses. We know that God has spoken to Moses, but as for this man, we do not know where he comes from." And the beggar — unlettered, expelled-in-waiting — proceeds to teach the teachers a flawless piece of theology: "Why, this is an amazing thing! You do not know where he comes from, and yet he opened my eyes. We know that God does not listen to sinners, but if anyone is a worshiper of God and does his will, God listens to him. Never since the world began has it been heard that anyone opened the eyes of a man born blind. If this man were not from God, he could do nothing." Every premise is biblical. The conclusion is inescapable. Isaiah had promised that in Messiah's day the eyes of the blind would be opened (Isaiah 35:5) — and the men who knew Isaiah best could not see Isaiah happening in front of them. Their answer is not refutation but expulsion: "You were born in utter sin, and would you teach us?" And they cast him out. The bad news of this chapter is on display in the investigators: the more evidence they gather, the blinder they choose to be. Evidence is never the problem. The will is the problem.

Jesus finds him (9:35–41). "Jesus heard that they had cast him out, and having found him..." Stop there. They cast him out; Jesus finds him. That is the whole gospel in a sequence. The man loses his synagogue and gains his Savior in the same day, and the trade is not close. Jesus asks him the question of the Gospel: "Do you believe in the Son of Man?" "And who is he, sir, that I may believe in him?" "You have seen him, and it is he who is speaking to you." The blind man's eyes are opened the second time: "Lord, I believe," and he worshiped him. From "the man called Jesus," to "a prophet," to Lord, worshiped on the ground — that is the road of faith, walked in one chapter by a beggar. Then Jesus speaks the chapter's verdict, and it is double-edged: "For judgment I came into this world, that those who do not see may see, and those who see may become blind." The Pharisees standing by catch the edge: "Are we also blind?" "If you were blind, you would have no guilt; but now that you say, 'We see,' your guilt remains." The man who admits his blindness gets sight. The men who insist on their sight keep their guilt. It is the same door as always: the Physician is for those who know they are sick.

Key verses

"Jesus answered, 'It was not that this man sinned, or his parents, but that the works of God might be displayed in him.'" — John 9:3 (ESV)

"He answered, 'Whether he is a sinner I do not know. One thing I do know, that though I was blind, now I see.'" — John 9:25 (ESV)

"He said, 'Lord, I believe,' and he worshiped him." — John 9:38 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of John 9 is that men who claim to see can interrogate a miracle all day and choose the dark — that claimed sight, defended proudly, keeps guilt in place. But the good news is that Jesus seeks out the man the synagogue cast out, finds him, reveals Himself to him, and opens his eyes the second time — to worship. The one who admits he was blind goes home seeing, twice over, and his testimony needs no degree: I was blind, now I see.

A note from the Reformers. Spurgeon urged ordinary believers to learn from this beggar: you may not be able to answer the scholars' questions, but you can always tell what you know. "I was blind, now I see" requires no degree, no training, and no permission. A testimony like that has silenced more arguments than a library.

Reflection question: The healed man's whole theology fit in one sentence about what Jesus had done for him. What is your "one thing I do know" — and have you said it out loud to anyone?
