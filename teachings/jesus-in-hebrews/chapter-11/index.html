Hebrews 11 — By Faith: The Cloud of Witnesses

Jesus in Hebrews — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Hebrews
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

The cloud of witnesses runs on one fuel — faith in a promise they could not see — and the God who was not ashamed of them has built them a city, and a place in it for us, together. Jesus stands at this chapter's edges and its center: He is the unseen reality faith lays hold of, the One the witnesses desired from far off without receiving, the better resurrection they refused release to obtain, and the One in whom — together with us — they are finally made perfect. Chapter 12 will name Him the founder and perfecter of this very faith; chapter 11 shows us the road He perfected it on, walked by His people for four thousand years.

Commentary

What faith is (11:1–3). The most famous definition in the Bible opens the chapter: "Now faith is the assurance of things hoped for, the conviction of things not seen" (11:1). The Greek word is hypostasis — substance, assurance, the reality standing under a thing. It is the same word used in chapter 1 for the Son as the exact imprint of God's substance. Faith, then, is not pretending the hoped-for things exist. It is their very substance in advance — the title deed held in the hand while the city is still being prepared. "For by it the people of old received their commendation" (11:2) — faith is how God's people have always been approved by God; there was never another way. And faith begins at the beginning: "By faith we understand that the universe was created by the word of God, so that what is seen was not made out of things that are visible" (11:3). None of us watched creation happen. We believe the Maker on His word — which is the pattern of everything that follows.

From Abel to Abraham: faith before sight (11:4–12). Then the preacher marches the whole Old Testament past us in review, and every life turns on the same two words: by faith. Abel offered a more acceptable sacrifice than Cain, and through his faith, "though he died, he still speaks" — the first witness still testifying. Enoch walked with God and was taken up, not seeing death; "before he was taken he had this commendation, that he pleased God." And here the preacher pauses for the chapter's rule: "And without faith it is impossible to please him, for whoever would draw near to God must believe that he exists and that he rewards those who seek him" (11:6). Noah, warned of things not yet seen, built an ark in reverent fear and became an heir of the righteousness that comes by faith. Abraham obeyed and went out "not knowing where he was going," lived in tents in the promised land as in a foreign country, and looked forward "to the city that has foundations, whose designer and builder is God" (11:8–10). Sarah herself received power to conceive "since she considered him faithful who had promised" — faithfulness is faith's whole argument: not the size of the promise, but the character of the Promiser. From one man, "and him as good as dead," came descendants as many as the stars.

They died in faith — strangers, and unashamed of God (11:13–16). Now the preacher reads the roll honestly, and the honesty is the chapter's backbone: "These all died in faith, not having received the things promised, but having seen them and greeted them from afar, and having acknowledged that they were strangers and exiles on the earth" (11:13). Died — without receiving. The bad news must be read plainly here, because this chapter refuses the cheap version of faith: faith is not a guarantee that we will see the promise kept in our lifetimes, and any gospel that sells it as one is lying. They greeted the promises from afar, the way sailors greet a coastline they will not reach — and they were content to be strangers here because they desired "a better country, that is, a heavenly one." And God's verdict on such people is one of the tenderest sentences in Scripture: "Therefore God is not ashamed to be called their God, for he has prepared for them a city" (11:16). Not ashamed. The God of heaven is proud to be named the God of people who trusted Him in the dark.

From the altar to the sea: faith that obeys and endures (11:17–29). Abraham, tested, offered up Isaac — reasoning "that God was able even to raise him from the dead, from which, figuratively speaking, he did receive him back" (11:19). Faith did arithmetic with the resurrection before there was a word for it. Isaac blessed, Jacob blessed dying, Joseph gave directions about his bones — faith reaching past its own deathbed into God's future. Moses' parents hid him by faith, unafraid of the king's edict. Moses himself, grown up, "refused to be called the son of Pharaoh's daughter, choosing rather to be mistreated with the people of God than to enjoy the fleeting pleasures of sin. He considered the reproach of Christ greater wealth than the treasures of Egypt, for he was looking to the reward" (11:24–26). Notice: the reproach of Christ — Moses' faith, centuries before Bethlehem, was already Christ-shaped; he weighed Christ's reproach against Egypt's treasures and chose the reproach, because he was looking to the reward. By faith Israel passed through the Red Sea; by faith the walls of Jericho fell; and by faith Rahab the prostitute — the roll's most surprising name — "did not perish with those who were disobedient, because she had given a friendly welcome to the spies" (11:31). A Canaanite outsider, saved by faith and a scarlet cord, written into the ancestry of Jesus Himself.

Victories, sufferings — and waiting for us (11:32–40). "And what more shall I say? For time would fail me to tell of Gideon, Barak, Samson, Jephthah, of David and Samuel and the prophets" (11:32) — the preacher is running out of breath, not material. Through faith they conquered kingdoms, stopped the mouths of lions, quenched fire, escaped the sword, were made strong out of weakness. And then the chapter turns, and does not flinch: "Some were tortured, refusing to accept release, so that they might rise again to a better life. Others suffered mocking and flogging, and even chains and imprisonment. They were stoned, they were sawn in two, they were killed with the sword. They went about in skins of sheep and goats, destitute, afflicted, mistreated — of whom the world was not worthy — wandering about in deserts and mountains, and in dens and caves of the earth" (11:35–38). Of whom the world was not worthy — God's assessment of people the world threw away. Faith carried some through the fire and carried others in it; it was the same faith. "And all these, though commended through their faith, did not receive what was promised, since God had provided something better for us, that apart from us they should not be made perfect" (11:39–40). Wonder of wonders: they are waiting for us. The perfection of Abel and Abraham and Moses waits on the completion of the whole company — on us. The cloud is not finished gathering. They are in the stands, and the race is still being run, and the finish is together.

The reward and the reproach (11:6, 26). Two verses in this chapter correct the modern suspicion that faith is embarrassed by reward. Verse 6 says the one who draws near to God "must believe that he exists and that he rewards those who seek him." Verse 26 says Moses chose Christ's reproach over Egypt's treasures precisely because "he was looking to the reward." Faith is not mercenary for looking to the reward, because the reward is God Himself — the city He prepared, the country He promised, His own "well done." The witnesses were not stoics enduring for nothing; they were heirs expecting an inheritance, and the expectation was part of the fuel. That matters for tired believers now. Hebrews never asks us to pretend the race is its own reward or that heaven is a small thing. It asks us to want the reward so much — the city, the resurrection, the God who is not ashamed of us — that present reproach and present loss are counted, honestly, as the smaller sum. Moses did the arithmetic in Pharaoh's palace. The cloud of witnesses has been doing it ever since.

The bad news and the good news

The bad news must be read honestly in Hebrews 11, because the chapter refuses the cheap version of faith: these all died without receiving the things promised; some were tortured, mocked, stoned, sawn in two, and lived in caves and holes in the ground, destitute and mistreated. Faith is not a guarantee of an easy life, and any gospel that sells it as one is lying. But the good news is that faith was never about the ease — it is the assurance of what is hoped for, because of who promised it. The witnesses were carried by the same promise that carries us; God is not ashamed to be called their God, He has prepared a city for them, and — wonder of wonders — apart from us they should not be made perfect. They are waiting for us, and the God who was not ashamed of them is not ashamed of us.

A note from the Reformers. This chapter is the Reformers' anthem because it proves their doctrine is as old as Abel. Every hero named was justified, sustained, and carried home by faith in the promise — centuries before the cross, centuries before the law was even given in Abraham's case. Faith is not the Reformation's invention. It is the Bible's one road, from the first family to the last saint.

Reflection question: The witnesses in this chapter believed without receiving in their lifetimes — what promise are you holding onto right now that you may not see fulfilled this side of heaven?
