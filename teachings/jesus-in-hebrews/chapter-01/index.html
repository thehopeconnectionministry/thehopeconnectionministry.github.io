Hebrews 1 — God Has Spoken by His Son: Greater Than Angels

Jesus in Hebrews — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Hebrews
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

The final Word is a Person — the Son who made the worlds, purified our sins by Himself, and sat down at the right hand of the Majesty on high. Jesus is revealed here as the radiance of God's glory and the exact imprint of His nature, the heir of all things, the Creator who upholds the universe by the word of His power, and the finished Redeemer whose seated posture announces that purification is done. He is greater than the angels — not a messenger of God's word, but the Word Himself, worshiped by angels and addressed by the Father as God.

Commentary

God has spoken — finally, in a Son (1:1–2a). The sermon opens with a contrast between two eras of God's speech. "Long ago, at many times and in many ways, God spoke to our fathers by the prophets." God's old speech was real and glorious, but it came in pieces — a burning bush here, a dream there, a psalm, a vision, a enacted parable — many times, many ways, never the whole at once. "But in these last days he has spoken to us by his Son." The last days are not a future age we are still waiting for; they began when the Son came. And the difference is not only in the amount of revelation. It is in the kind. The prophets were servants carrying messages. The Son is the heir carrying His own inheritance: "whom he appointed the heir of all things." Everything belongs to Him by right. And He is more than heir: "through whom also he created the world." The One who speaks for God is the One through whom God made all things. To settle for the fragments when the fullness has come — to take the messengers and miss the heir — is the danger this whole letter exists to prevent.

The radiance and the seated Redeemer (1:2b–4). Now the preacher stacks up the glories of the Son in the most concentrated Christology in the New Testament:

"He is the radiance of the glory of God and the exact imprint of his nature, and he upholds the universe by the word of his power. After making purification for sins, he sat down at the right hand of the Majesty on high," — Hebrews 1:3 (ESV)

Take it piece by piece. The Greek word is apaugasma — radiance, the shining-out of glory, as sunlight is the shining-out of the sun. Jesus does not reflect God's glory the way a mirror borrows light. He radiates it, because it is His own by nature. He is "the exact imprint of his nature" — as a seal stamps its very image into wax, the Son is the precise expression of what God is. He "upholds the universe by the word of his power" — present tense, right now: the worlds do not run on their own; the Son carries them. And then, in one breath, the cross and the throne: "After making purification for sins, he sat down." Priests in the tabernacle never sat down on duty — there was no chair in the holy place, because the work was never finished. This Priest finished, and sat. The sermon's first page already contains its whole gospel: purification made, by Himself, and a seated Christ. "Having become as much superior to angels as the name he has inherited is more excellent than theirs" (1:4) — inherited, because the Son's exaltation to the right hand is the Father's public installation of Him in the office He earned through the cross.

"You are my Son" — the catena of Scripture (1:5–13). The preacher now proves the Son's superiority the Reformation way: from Scripture alone. Verse after verse, drawn mostly from the Psalms, he lets God the Father do the talking. "For to which of the angels did God ever say, 'You are my Son, today I have begotten you'?" (1:5, quoting Psalm 2:7). To none of them. Angels are never called God's son in this singular, royal sense. "And again, when he brings the firstborn into the world, he says, 'Let all God's angels worship him'" (1:6) — the angels, whom some of the readers were tempted to exalt, are commanded to worship the Son. Of the angels God says, "He makes his angels winds, and his ministers a flame of fire" (1:7) — glorious, but they are weather and flame: created, changeable, serving. "But of the Son he says, 'Your throne, O God, is forever and ever'" (1:8, quoting Psalm 45:6). Notice what the Father calls the Son: O God. The Trinity is on the sermon's first page — the Father addressing the Son as God, and as the Lord who "in the beginning… laid the foundation of the earth" (1:10, quoting Psalm 102). The heavens will wear out like a garment; "but you are the same, and your years will have no end" (1:12). And the section lands where the whole letter will keep landing — on Psalm 110:1: "Sit at my right hand until I make your enemies a footstool for your feet" (1:13). Said to the Son. Said to no angel, ever.

What angels are — and what the Son is (1:14). The chapter closes by putting angels in their true, honored, lesser place: "Are they not all ministering spirits sent out to serve for the sake of those who are to inherit salvation?" Angels are servants sent to serve the heirs. The Son is the heir Himself, and the author of the salvation the angels serve. To drift back toward a religion of angels and messengers — however glorious — is to trade the King for His court attendants. The preacher will not allow it. God has spoken by His Son. Hear Him.

Loved, anointed, and upheld (1:8–9, 13). Two details in the catena deserve a slower look. The Father says of the Son, "You have loved righteousness and hated wickedness; therefore God, your God, has anointed you with the oil of gladness beyond your companions" (1:9, quoting Psalm 45:7). The Son's throne is not bare power; it is a righteous throne, held by One who loves righteousness in His own character, and His anointing is gladness — the joy of the King exceeds that of all His companions. And the chapter's last word about angels puts the whole created order of heaven in its place: the Son sits; the angels serve. Some of these readers, formed in a Judaism that rightly honored angels as God's messengers at Sinai, needed to hear that the court is not the King. The preacher is not diminishing angels. He is enthroning the Son — and a church that has the Son seated at the right hand has no need to go shopping among the servants for a word from God.

Why "greater than angels" mattered then — and now (1:4–6). To a modern reader, a whole chapter proving Jesus is greater than angels can seem like winning an argument nobody is having. The first readers were having it. In Jewish thought, angels stood at the summit of creation: they had delivered the law at Sinai (see 2:2), they ministered in heaven's court, and some in that world were tempted to give them a devotion that belonged to God. The preacher's answer is not to scorn angels but to rank them: they are "ministering spirits sent out to serve for the sake of those who are to inherit salvation" (1:14). Servants — glorious servants — sent to serve us. And the temptation has a modern form. Whenever a believer goes looking for a fresher word — a new revelation, a spiritual experience, a messenger more exciting than the Son delivered in Scripture — he is repeating the old drift in new clothes. God's last word is not coming. It came. It is a Person, seated, and the angels themselves worship Him.

Key verses

"Long ago, at many times and in many ways, God spoke to our fathers by the prophets, but in these last days he has spoken to us by his Son, whom he appointed the heir of all things, through whom also he created the world." — Hebrews 1:1–2 (ESV)

"He is the radiance of the glory of God and the exact imprint of his nature, and he upholds the universe by the word of his power. After making purification for sins, he sat down at the right hand of the Majesty on high," — Hebrews 1:3 (ESV)

"But of the Son he says, 'Your throne, O God, is forever and ever, the scepter of uprightness is the scepter of your kingdom.'" — Hebrews 1:8 (ESV)

"But you are the same, and your years will have no end." — Hebrews 1:12 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Hebrews 1 is that God has spoken His final word, and there is no other word coming to compete with it — which means that to neglect the Son, to settle for fragments and messengers and shadows when the heir Himself has come, is to refuse God's own last and best speech to us. We cannot plead that God has been silent. But the good news is the loudest opening in the New Testament: God has spoken to us by His Son — the radiance of His glory, the upholder of the universe — and that Son, before He ever sat down, first made purification for our sins. The God who made the worlds has spoken, and what He said, in the end, was a Person who came to purify sinners and then took His seat because the work was done.

A note from the Reformers. Hebrews opens the Reformers' favorite argument: God has spoken finally, fully, and unsurpassably in His Son, and that speech is preserved for us in the written Word. There is no further revelation coming to compete with Christ — no new word beyond the Son, and no authority above the Scripture that bears Him.

Reflection question: God's final word is not a principle but a Person — where have you been looking for God to speak to you in ways that bypass the Son He has already spoken by?
