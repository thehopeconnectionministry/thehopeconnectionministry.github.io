Hebrews 13 — Outside the Camp: The Same Jesus, Yesterday, Today, Forever

Jesus in Hebrews — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Hebrews
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

The same Jesus — yesterday, today, and forever — so love stays warm, hearts rest content on an unbreakable promise, and the God of peace equips His flock through the great Shepherd of the sheep. Jesus is revealed here as the unchanging One in a changing world, the great Shepherd of the sheep brought again from the dead by the blood of the eternal covenant, the One who suffered outside the gate to sanctify the people — so that His people go to Him outside the camp, bearing His reproach — and as the eternal object of the church's praise, offered continually through Him.

Commentary

Love on the ground (13:1–6). The sermon ends the way sermons should — on the ground, in daily life. "Let brotherly love continue" (13:1). Continue — it was already there; keep it warm. "Do not neglect to show hospitality to strangers, for thereby some have entertained angels unawares" (13:2) — Abraham at the oaks of Mamre stands behind that line, and the door of the Christian home stays open. "Remember those who are in prison, as though in prison with them, and those who are mistreated, since you also are in the body" (13:3) — remember them as if your body were in their cell, because in Christ's body, it is. "Let marriage be held in honor among all, and let the marriage bed be undefiled, for God will judge the sexually immoral and adulterous" (13:4). Then the preacher aims at the itch underneath half our sins: "Keep your life free from love of money, and be content with what you have, for he has said, 'I will never leave you nor forsake you'" (13:5, quoting Deuteronomy 31:6 and Joshua 1:5). Contentment is not a mood; it is a theology. The ground of it is a promise spoken first to Joshua, now handed by the preacher to every believer as his own: "So we can confidently say, 'The Lord is my helper; I will not fear; what can man do to me?'" (13:6, quoting Psalm 118:6). A God who will never leave makes money's promises small and man's threats smaller.

Remember your leaders — Jesus Christ the same (13:7–9). "Remember your leaders, those who spoke to you the word of God. Consider the outcome of their way of life, and imitate their faith" (13:7). Leaders die; their faith is to be imitated, their outcome considered — did it land them in the city? Then, between the leaders who were and the teachers who come and go, stands the chapter's anchor line, and one of the most loved sentences in the New Testament:

"Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever." — Hebrews 13:8 (ESV)

Leaders die, times change, feelings swing — He does not. The same Jesus who saved the leaders saved us; the same Jesus who saves us today will be the same forever. "Do not be led away by diverse and strange teachings, for it is good for the heart to be strengthened by grace, not by foods, which have not benefited those devoted to them" (13:9). Strange teachings drift in during every generation, usually dressed as depth. The heart is not strengthened by rules about food, or by any ceremony — it is strengthened by grace. After thirteen chapters of the finished priesthood of Christ, that is the note the preacher wants ringing in the ears.

Outside the camp (13:10–16). The sermon's last great picture gathers the whole book into one scene. "We have an altar from which those who serve the tent have no right to eat" (13:10). Under the old system, the bodies of the animals whose blood was brought into the holy places for sin were burned outside the camp — the sin offering ended in ashes beyond the boundary. "So Jesus also suffered outside the gate in order to sanctify the people through his own blood" (13:12). Outside the gate of Jerusalem, on the refuse side of the wall, the true sin offering was made — the Sanctifier sanctifying His people by His own blood in the place of shame. And the conclusion follows the way a command follows a cross: "Therefore let us go to him outside the camp and bear the reproach he endured. For here we have no lasting city, but we seek the city that is to come" (13:13–14). Go to Him — where He is, outside, in the reproach — because our city is not here anyway. The readers tempted to slip back inside the respectable walls are called out to a crucified Messiah in the open. And the sacrifices of this new order are named: "Through him then let us continually offer up a sacrifice of praise to God, that is, the fruit of lips that acknowledge his name. Do not neglect to do good and to share what you have, for such sacrifices are pleasing to God" (13:15–16). Praise and generosity — lips and hands — are the offerings of the finished altar.

Submit, pray — and the benediction (13:17–25). "Obey your leaders and submit to them, for they are keeping watch over your souls, as those who will have to give an account. Let them do this with joy and not with groaning, for that would be of no advantage to you" (13:17). Watchmen who must give account; a flock that can make the watching a joy. The preacher asks for prayer with a clear conscience (13:18–19), and then pronounces the benediction that closes the sermon — one of the richest in Scripture: "Now may the God of peace who brought again from the dead our Lord Jesus, the great shepherd of the sheep, by the blood of the eternal covenant, equip you with everything good that you may do his will, working in us that which is pleasing in his sight, through Jesus Christ, to whom be glory forever and ever. Amen" (13:20–21). The God of peace. The resurrection. The great Shepherd — the Priest of the whole letter is also the Shepherd who was raised and who leads the flock home. The eternal covenant, sealed in blood. And the equipping: God working in us what pleases Him — grace to the last line, glory to God alone. A final appeal — "bear with my word of exhortation, for I have written to you briefly" (13:22) — only a preacher could call thirteen chapters of this density brief — news of Timothy, greetings from Italy, and the last word of the letter is the word the whole letter has been proving: "Grace be with all of you" (13:25).

Fruit of lips, and the watchmen's joy (13:15–17). The chapter's two sacrifices deserve naming together, because they keep each other honest. The first is "the fruit of lips that acknowledge his name" — praise, confession, the public naming of Jesus as Lord. The second is doing good and sharing what we have — the wallet and the hands. Lips without hands become noise; hands without lips become philanthropy that hides its Lord. Hebrews joins them as the twin offerings of the new covenant, offered continually — the praise sacrifice is not a Sunday event but a life's fruit, growing in all seasons. And beside the sacrifices stands the flock's duty to its shepherds: leaders who "keep watch over your souls, as those who will have to give an account." A congregation can make that watching a joy or a groaning, and the preacher says plainly that joyless shepherding is "of no advantage to you." The letter that began with God speaking ends with a speaking, sharing, watched-over, mutually glad people. That is what the finished priesthood of Christ builds on earth.

The city that is to come (13:14). One sentence carries the whole chapter's pilgrim logic: "For here we have no lasting city, but we seek the city that is to come." Abraham looked for this city in chapter 11; the witnesses died greeting it from afar; here the preacher tells settled, tempted, property-owning believers that the search is still their identity. Here — in this arrangement, this economy, this generation — no lasting city. Every earthly security the readers were tempted to protect by drifting back is, by heaven's audit, temporary. And the seeking is not passive: it is the engine of the chapter's ethics. Because the city is coming, brotherly love continues now. Because the city is coming, strangers are welcomed and prisoners remembered now. Because the city is coming, money is held loosely and marriage is held honorably now. Eternity, in Hebrews, never floats a man out of his duties; it sets his duties on fire. The people with no lasting city here turn out to be the best neighbors this present city has.

Grace be with all of you (13:25). The sermon's final words are worth weighing. After the warnings, the oath, the anchor, the uttermost, the hall of faith, the discipline, and the consuming fire, the preacher does not end with a command. He ends with a blessing of grace — the free, unmerited favor of God — wished upon all of them, the strong and the drifting alike. Grace opened the letter ("God has spoken"), and grace closes it. That is the frame of the Christian life as Hebrews sees it: everything between is held inside the favor of God, spoken first in His Son and still being spoken, by this same preacher's heirs, to tired people tempted to drift. If you take one sentence from this chapter into the week, take the blessing with you: grace be with you — all of it, from the God of peace, through the great Shepherd, forever.

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Hebrews 13 is that contentment never comes naturally to us: the love of money itches, strange teachings drift in to strengthen hearts by rules instead of by grace, and the city we are tempted to settle into here is not lasting — here we have no lasting city. And the call of the chapter is costly: out of the respectable walls, outside the camp, bearing reproach with a crucified Lord. But the good news is the ground the preacher gives every command — a promise God spoke to Joshua and now hands to every believer: "I will never leave you nor forsake you. So that with good courage we say, 'The Lord is my helper. I will not fear.'" Over it all stands the chapter's anchor line: "Jesus Christ is the same yesterday, today, and forever." Leaders die, times change, feelings swing — He does not. And the God of peace, who raised the great Shepherd of the sheep, is equipping His flock even now, working in us that which is pleasing in His sight.

A note from the Reformers. The letter's last doctrinal sentence before its benediction is this: "It is good for the heart to be strengthened by grace" (verse 9). Not by ceremonies, not by food laws, not by striving — by grace. After thirteen chapters of the finished priesthood of Christ, that is the note the preacher wants ringing in the ears. It is the whole Reformation in one line, written fifteen centuries early.

Reflection question: "I will never leave you nor forsake you" is God's own answer to discontent — what are you reaching for right now that you would not need if you believed He is enough?
