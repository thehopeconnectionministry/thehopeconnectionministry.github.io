Hebrews 10 — By One Offering, Perfected Forever: Draw Near, Hold Fast

Jesus in Hebrews — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Hebrews
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

One offering has perfected forever the people it is still sanctifying — so we enter boldly by the new and living way, hold fast without wavering, and refuse to shrink back. Jesus is revealed here as the One whose single offering did what ten thousand sacrifices could not, whose body prepared by the Father became the willing offering of Psalm 40, who sat down at God's right hand waiting until His enemies are made His footstool, and whose flesh is the new and living way through the curtain into the Holy Place.

Commentary

The shadow and the endless repetition (10:1–4). "For since the law has but a shadow of the good things to come instead of the true form of these realities, it can never, by the same sacrifices that are continually offered every year, make perfect those who draw near" (10:1). A shadow can never take away sins — it has the outline of the reality and none of its substance. And the repetition itself was the confession: "Otherwise, would they not have ceased to be offered, since the worshipers, having once been cleansed, would no longer have any consciousness of sins?" (10:2). A sacrifice that has to be repeated every year is announcing, every year, that it did not finish. Instead, "in these sacrifices there is a reminder of sins every year. For it is impossible for the blood of bulls and goats to take away sins" (10:3–4). Impossible — the preacher's blunt verdict on the whole animal system, spoken not in contempt but in honesty: it was a reminder, a tutor, a shadow. It was never the removal.

"Behold, I have come to do your will" (10:5–10). Then the Son Himself speaks, in the words of Psalm 40, and the incarnation and the cross meet in one speech: "Consequently, when Christ came into the world, he said, 'Sacrifices and offerings you have not desired, but a body have you prepared for me; in burnt offerings and sin offerings you have taken no pleasure. Then I said, Behold, I have come to do your will, O God, as it is written of me in the scroll of the book'" (10:5–7). A body prepared — that is why He took flesh: to have something to offer. God's pleasure was never in the animals; it was in the obedient Son. "He does away with the first in order to establish the second. And by that will we have been sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all" (10:9–10). Once for all — ephapax again — and the result stated in the past tense: we have been sanctified. The old order is done away, not in disgrace but in fulfillment, and the second stands on the will of God and the body of Jesus.

Every priest stands; this Priest sat (10:11–18). "And every priest stands daily at his service, offering repeatedly the same sacrifices, which can never take away sins. But when Christ had offered for all time a single sacrifice for sins, he sat down at the right hand of God, waiting from that time until his enemies should be made a footstool for his feet" (10:11–13). Standing priests, seated Christ — the whole argument in one posture. Then the verse the whole letter leans on: "For by a single offering he has perfected for all time those who are being sanctified" (10:14). The Greek word is teleioō — to perfect, to bring to the goal, to complete. Hold the two halves together, because the verse holds them together: perfected for all time — finished, complete, done — and being sanctified — in process, still being formed, still growing. The finishing is done; the forming goes on. Your sanctification is slow because you are being sanctified — not because your perfection is in doubt. The Holy Spirit then seals it with Jeremiah's promise once more: "I will put my laws on their hearts, and write them on their minds… I will remember their sins and their lawless deeds no more." And the conclusion needs no improvement: "Where there is forgiveness of these, there is no longer any offering for sin" (10:18). Where forgiveness is complete, offerings stop. All of them. Forever.

Draw near, hold fast, stir up (10:19–25). Doctrine now becomes exhortation — three great "let us" commands, the sermon's heartbeat. "Therefore, brothers, since we have confidence to enter the holy places by the blood of Jesus, by the new and living way that he opened for us through the curtain, that is, through his flesh, and since we have a great priest over the house of God, let us draw near with a true heart in full assurance of faith" (10:19–22). The curtain is His flesh — torn, as the temple curtain tore at His death — and the way through is new and living: not a worn path of ritual but a living Person. Draw near, with hearts sprinkled clean and bodies washed with pure water. "Let us hold fast the confession of our hope without wavering, for he who promised is faithful" (10:23). Our grip is commanded; His faithfulness is the ground. "And let us consider how to stir up one another to love and good works, not neglecting to meet together, as is the habit of some, but encouraging one another, and all the more as you see the Day drawing near" (10:24–25). Note it plainly: neglecting the gathering was already "the habit of some" in the first century, and the preacher names it as a danger, not a preference. The assembly is one of God's means of keeping His people, and the nearer the Day, the more we need it, not the less.

The fourth warning — and the better possession (10:26–39). Now the warning, at full strength: "For if we go on sinning deliberately after receiving the knowledge of the truth, there no longer remains a sacrifice for sins, but a fearful expectation of judgment, and a fury of fire that will consume the adversaries" (10:26–27). To go on sinning deliberately — settled, contemptuous persistence — after knowing the truth is to treat the one sacrifice as nothing; and there is no other sacrifice left to run to. The argument from the lesser: a man who set aside Moses' law died without mercy; "how much worse punishment, do you think, will be deserved by the one who has trampled underfoot the Son of God, and has profaned the blood of the covenant by which he was sanctified, and has outraged the Spirit of grace?" (10:29). Trampled the Son. Profaned the blood. Outraged the Spirit. The preacher piles up the horror because the sin is horror. "It is a fearful thing to fall into the hands of the living God" (10:31). This is not a passage to soften, and we do not soften it: deliberate, defiant apostasy from a known Christ is the most dangerous ground a soul can stand on. But hear how the preacher preaches it — he turns immediately to his hearers' own history of faithfulness: "But recall the former days when, after you were enlightened, you endured a hard struggle with sufferings… For you had compassion on those in prison, and you joyfully accepted the plundering of your property, since you knew that you yourselves had a better possession and an abiding one" (10:32–34). You knew — you proved you knew, at real cost. "Therefore do not throw away your confidence, which has a great reward. For you have need of endurance, so that when you have done the will of God you may receive what is promised" (10:35–36). And the chapter closes with Habakkuk's word and the preacher's own verdict on his people: "my righteous one shall live by faith, and if he shrinks back, my soul has no pleasure in him. But we are not of those who shrink back and are destroyed, but of those who have faith and preserve their souls" (10:38–39). We are not of those who shrink back. Warning and confidence, in the same breath, aimed at the same people. That is how Hebrews handles the trembling — it warns them off the cliff and then takes their arm.

Key verses

"For by a single offering he has perfected for all time those who are being sanctified." — Hebrews 10:14 (ESV)

"let us draw near with a true heart in full assurance of faith, with our hearts sprinkled clean from an evil conscience and our bodies washed with pure water." — Hebrews 10:22 (ESV)

"Let us hold fast the confession of our hope without wavering, for he who promised is faithful." — Hebrews 10:23 (ESV)

"But we are not of those who shrink back and are destroyed, but of those who have faith and preserve their souls." — Hebrews 10:39 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Hebrews 10 is present, and it is fierce: the law was a shadow, and a shadow can never take away sins — and to go on sinning deliberately after receiving the knowledge of the truth, trampling the Son of God underfoot and treating His blood as a common thing, leaves no sacrifice remaining, only a fearful expectation of judgment. The preacher will not soften that, and neither do we. But the chapter's commands run the other direction — toward confidence, not despair. By one offering He has perfected forever those who are being sanctified. So draw near with a true heart in full assurance of faith; hold fast the confession of our hope without wavering, for He who promised is faithful; stir one another up to love and good works; and do not throw away your confidence. We are not of those who shrink back. We are of those who have faith and preserve their souls.

A note from the Reformers. "Full assurance of faith" (verse 22) was a Reformers' watchword. Assurance is not pride, they taught, because it rests not on the strength of our believing but on the finishedness of the offering believed in. A perfected-forever people can afford to be honest about being sanctified slowly.

Reflection question: "Perfected forever" and "being sanctified" describe you in the same verse — how does holding both together change the way you see your slow days and setbacks?
