Hebrews 2 — Do Not Drift Away: The Brother Who Tasted Death

Jesus in Hebrews — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Hebrews
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

The crowned Man who tasted death for everyone breaks the devil's death-grip and stands beside the tempted as a merciful Brother who has been there. Jesus is revealed here as truly man — made lower than the angels for a little while, perfected through suffering, not ashamed to call us brothers — and as the destroyer of the devil's power of death, the merciful and faithful High Priest who made propitiation for our sins and who helps the tempted because He Himself suffered when tempted.

Commentary

The first warning: the danger of drifting (2:1–4). The sermon's first exhortation grows straight out of chapter 1. "Therefore we must pay much closer attention to what we have heard, lest we drift away from it." The picture is nautical: a boat slipping its moorings, a ship floating slowly past the harbor in the night. Nobody decides in one moment to abandon Christ. They neglect, and they float. The argument is from the lesser to the greater: if the message declared by angels — the law given at Sinai — proved to be reliable, and every transgression received a just retribution, "how shall we escape if we neglect such a great salvation?" (2:3). Note the word: neglect, not reject. The first warning of Hebrews is aimed not at rebels but at drifters — at tired believers who stop paying attention. And this salvation is not a rumor: it "was declared at first by the Lord, and it was attested to us by those who heard, while God also bore witness by signs and wonders and various miracles and by gifts of the Holy Spirit distributed according to his will" (2:3–4). The preacher counts himself among those who received the word from the hearers of the Lord — a handed-down, witnessed, God-confirmed gospel. Drifting from it is not a small thing, because it is not a small salvation.

The crowned Man of Psalm 8 (2:5–9). The preacher now turns to mankind's lost glory. "For it was not to angels that God subjected the world to come, of which we are speaking." The world to come belongs not to angels but to man — to a Man. Quoting Psalm 8, he marvels: "What is man, that you are mindful of him, or the son of man, that you care for him? You made him for a little while lower than the angels; you have crowned him with glory and honor, putting everything in subjection under his feet" (2:6–8). That was God's design for Adam and his race — dominion, glory, honor. But honestly: "At present, we do not yet see everything in subjection to him" (2:8). We see death reigning, not man. "But we see him who for a little while was made lower than the angels, namely Jesus, crowned with glory and honor because of the suffering of death, so that by the grace of God he might taste death for everyone" (2:9).

"But we see him who for a little while was made lower than the angels, namely Jesus, crowned with glory and honor because of the suffering of death, so that by the grace of God he might taste death for everyone." — Hebrews 2:9 (ESV)

Taste death for everyone — He did not sip it; He drained the cup, experiencing death in the place of every one of His people. What Adam lost, Jesus — the last Adam, the true Man — has won back, and He wears the crown now as the first of a restored humanity.

Perfect through suffering, not ashamed (2:10–13). "For it was fitting that he, for whom and by whom all things exist, in bringing many sons to glory, should make the founder of their salvation perfect through suffering" (2:10). The Greek word is archēgos — pioneer, author, the captain who goes first and opens the way for others. Jesus does not call us down a road He has not walked. He blazed it, through suffering and death, and brings many sons behind Him. "Perfect through suffering" does not mean He was morally defective and improved; it means He was fully equipped, completed as our Savior, by walking the whole road of human pain. And then the sentence to carry out of this chapter: "That is why he is not ashamed to call them brothers" (2:11). The holy Son of God, and sinners like us — and He is not ashamed of the relationship. He says it in Scripture's own words, quoted from Psalm 22 — the cross psalm: "I will tell of your name to my brothers; in the midst of the congregation I will sing your praise" (2:12). And again: "Behold, I and the children God has given me" (2:13). The Brother presents His brothers and sisters to the Father like a man proud of his family.

He destroyed the death-holder and helps the tempted (2:14–18). "Since therefore the children share in flesh and blood, he himself likewise partook of the same things, that through death he might destroy the one who has the power of death, that is, the devil, and deliver all those who through fear of death were subject to lifelong slavery" (2:14–15). Here is the fear that enslaves the whole human race — the fear of death, held over us by the one who holds death's power. Jesus broke that leverage the only way it could be broken: by dying. He took flesh and blood precisely so that He could die, and by dying He destroyed the devil's grip and freed the slaves of fear. "For surely it is not angels that he helps, but he helps the offspring of Abraham" (2:16). And so the chapter lands on the first full statement of His priesthood: "Therefore he had to be made like his brothers in every respect, so that he might become a merciful and faithful high priest in the service of God, to make propitiation for the sins of the people" (2:17). Propitiation — the turning away of wrath by a sacrifice. The Priest is merciful because He is one of us; He is faithful because He is God's appointed; and He made propitiation because our sins required it. "For because he himself has suffered when tempted, he is able to help those who are being tempted" (2:18). He has been where you are. The help He gives is not the help of a spectator. It is the help of a Brother who has carried the full weight Himself.

A salvation too great to neglect (2:3–4, revisited). It is worth pausing on how the preacher describes this salvation: "such a great salvation." Its greatness is measured by its herald — declared at first by the Lord Himself, not by an angel or a prophet — and by its witnesses, the men who heard Him and handed it on, and by God's own co-witness in signs and gifts of the Spirit. Drift is dangerous precisely because what we drift from is not small. A man does not shrug at a great salvation the way he shrugs at a small one; he neglects it the way a tired sailor neglects the tiller — a little inattention, over a long, quiet stretch of water. The preacher's remedy is in the same verse: pay much closer attention. Not panic. Attention — the deliberate, repeated fastening of the mind on what we have heard, in the Word, in the assembly, at the table. Great salvations are kept by attended-to Christs.

"Not yet — but we see Jesus" (2:8–9). The preacher's honesty in this passage is itself a pastoral gift. He does not pretend the world looks subjected to man. "At present, we do not yet see everything in subjection to him." We see hospitals and graves; we see thrones occupied by the proud; we see our own hearts not yet fully subdued. Hebrews gives us the two words for living in that gap: not yet — and but we see Jesus. The Christian life is lived between those two clauses. We do not see everything subjected; we see Him — crowned, at the right hand, the first Man installed in the glory the race was made for. Faith, in this chapter, is not optimism about circumstances. It is eyesight aimed at a Person. The crowned Jesus is the down payment and the proof of concept: what is true of the Head will become true of the body, because the Head is "bringing many sons to glory" (2:10) — present tense, on the way, not finished but underway.

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Hebrews 2 is quiet and deadly: nobody has to rebel against Christ to lose their way — they only have to neglect so great a salvation and drift, the way a boat slips its moorings in the night. And underneath our drifting stands the fear that enslaves the whole human race, the fear of death, held over us by the devil. But the good news answers both. Jesus was made lower than the angels for a little while, crowned with glory because of the suffering of death, so that by the grace of God He might taste death for everyone. He destroyed the devil's leverage, freed those who lived in slavery to fear, made propitiation for our sins — and He is not ashamed to call us brothers. Having suffered temptation Himself, this Brother is able to help the tempted. He has been where you are.

A note from the Reformers. Calvin, in his commentary on this letter, pressed the pastoral gold of this chapter: the Son's willingness to call us brothers is not a figure of speech but a standing invitation to come to Him as family. The Reformers taught believers to answer the fear of death not with their own record but with the Brother who tasted death for everyone — death has lost its holder, and its sting with him.

Reflection question: Drifting, not rebellion, is this chapter's warning — in what area of your walk have you been slowly floating, and what would tying back up to Christ look like this week?
