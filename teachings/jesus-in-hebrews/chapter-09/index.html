Hebrews 9 — Once for All: Into the Holy Places by His Own Blood

Jesus in Hebrews — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Hebrews
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Behind a better curtain, with better blood, once for all — the Priest who obtained eternal redemption will appear a second time for the people who wait for Him. Jesus is revealed here as the High Priest of the good things that have come, who entered not an earthly copy but heaven itself, not with the blood of goats and calves but with His own blood, securing an eternal redemption; the mediator of the new covenant whose death redeems transgressions under the first; and the Christ who, having been offered once to bear the sins of many, will appear a second time to save those eagerly waiting for Him.

Commentary

The earthly tent — a sermon illustration (9:1–5). The preacher walks us through the tabernacle the way a man walks through a house he grew up in. "Now even the first covenant had regulations for worship and an earthly place of holiness" (9:1). The first section — the Holy Place — held the lampstand, the table, and the Bread of the Presence. Behind the second curtain stood the Most Holy Place: the golden altar of incense, the ark of the covenant covered on all sides with gold, holding the golden urn of manna, Aaron's staff that budded, and the tablets of the covenant; above it, the cherubim of glory overshadowing the mercy seat. "Of these things we cannot now speak in detail" (9:5) — the preacher is not giving a tour for its own sake. Every piece of furniture was God's own sermon illustration, preached in gold and cloth for fifteen centuries. The question is what the illustration was saying.

The bad news built into the furniture (9:6–10). The arrangement itself preached the bad news. The priests went regularly into the first section, performing their duties — but "into the second only the high priest goes, and he but once a year, and not without taking blood, which he offers for himself and for the unintentional sins of the people" (9:7). Once a year. One man. With blood not his own. And veils everywhere else. "By this the Holy Spirit indicates that the way into the holy places is not yet opened as long as the first section is still standing" (9:8). The Holy Spirit Himself was the illustrator, and His message was restriction: the way in was not yet open. The gifts and sacrifices offered there "cannot perfect the conscience of the worshiper, but deal only with food and drink and various washings, regulations for the body imposed until the time of reformation" (9:9–10). They could order a man's outside. They could not cleanse his conscience — the inner court where guilt actually lives. The whole system, by God's own design, was a beautiful, God-given dead end, holding the place until the time of reformation came.

"But Christ" — once for all, eternal redemption (9:11–14). Then the chapter's great turning: "But Christ, having arrived as a high priest of the coming good things" — in the ESV, "of the good things that have come" — "then through the greater and more perfect tent (not made with hands, that is, not of this creation) he entered once for all into the holy places, not by means of the blood of goats and calves but by means of his own blood, thus securing an eternal redemption" (9:11–12, ESV).

"he entered once for all into the holy places, not by means of the blood of goats and calves but by means of his own blood, thus securing an eternal redemption." — Hebrews 9:12 (ESV)

The Greek word is ephapax — once for all, once and never again. It is the drumbeat of this letter (7:27; 9:12; 10:10). Christianity's center is an event that does not need, and cannot receive, a repeat. The word is small; the comfort is enormous. And the redemption secured is eternal — not annual, not renewable, eternal. The argument deepens: if the blood of goats and bulls and the ashes of a heifer could sanctify the flesh outwardly, "how much more will the blood of Christ, who through the eternal Spirit offered himself without blemish to God, purify our conscience from dead works to serve the living God" (9:14). The old sacrifices reached the skin. Christ's blood reaches the conscience — the very place the old system could not touch — and purifies it not merely for relief but for service: from dead works, to serve the living God.

Mediator of a new covenant — and a will (9:15–22). "Therefore he is the mediator of a new covenant, so that those who are called may receive the promised eternal inheritance, since a death has occurred that redeems them from the transgressions committed under the first covenant" (9:15). Even the sins of the old covenant centuries — the sins of Abraham, Moses, and David — were carried by this one death. Then the preacher explains covenants through wills: a will takes effect only at death; it is never in force while the one who made it lives (9:16–17). That is why the first covenant itself was inaugurated with blood — Moses sprinkling the book, the people, the tent, and the vessels, saying, "This is the blood of the covenant that God commanded for you" (9:20, quoting Exodus 24:8). "Indeed, under the law almost everything is purified with blood, and without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness of sins" (9:22). Blood was never decoration in God's house. Forgiveness was never cheap. The only question the gospel answers is whose blood — the animals', endlessly, or the Son's, once.

Heaven itself — once, at the end of the ages (9:23–28). The copies needed cleansing with animal blood; "the heavenly things themselves" needed "better sacrifices than these" (9:23). "For Christ has entered, not into holy places made with hands, which are copies of the true things, but into heaven itself, now to appear in the presence of God on our behalf" (9:24). On our behalf — He is there, in the presence of God, for us, now. And His entrance was not to offer Himself repeatedly, "as the high priest enters the holy places every year with blood not his own, for then he would have had to suffer repeatedly since the foundation of the world" (9:25–26). No. "But as it is, he has appeared once for all at the end of the ages to put away sin by the sacrifice of himself" (9:26). Once, at the end of the ages — the cross stands at history's hinge. Then the solemn parallel: "And just as it is appointed for man to die once, and after that comes judgment, so Christ, having been offered once to bear the sins of many, will appear a second time, not to deal with sin but to save those who are eagerly waiting for him" (9:27–28). Die once; offered once. And the second appearing will not be about sin at all — sin was dealt with the first time, finished. He comes to save, fully and finally, the people who are eagerly waiting for Him. The eagerness is the mark of the people: they wait for a Savior, not a prosecutor.

The conscience — the battleground the blood reaches (9:9, 13–14). Follow one word through this chapter and the whole argument glows: conscience. The old gifts "cannot perfect the conscience of the worshiper" (9:9). The old blood sanctified "for the purification of the flesh" (9:13). Christ's blood will "purify our conscience from dead works to serve the living God" (9:14). The conscience is the inner witness that accuses or excuses us — the courtroom inside every man, where guilt is actually felt. Religion can wash the outside, regulate the diet, schedule the washings, and leave that courtroom in session all night. Every reader who has lain awake under an accusing conscience knows the difference between a cleansed record on paper and a cleansed conscience in fact. The preacher's claim is that Christ's blood reaches the second one. Not by making us forget what we did, but by settling what it cost — once, in full, by better blood. A purified conscience is not a seared one; it still warns against new sin. But it no longer prosecutes the old, because the old has been answered by blood it cannot argue with.

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Hebrews 9 was built into the furniture of God's own house: the way into the Holy Place was not yet disclosed, the gifts and sacrifices offered there could not perfect the conscience of the worshiper, and it is appointed for man to die once, and after that, judgment. The old system, by God's design, could order a man's outside and never cleanse the court where his guilt actually lives. But the good news is the chapter's great turning word: But Christ. He entered once for all into the holy places — heaven itself — not with the blood of goats and calves but with His own blood, having obtained eternal redemption. Not annual. Eternal. And the Christ who appeared once to bear the sins of many will appear a second time, not to deal with sin, but to save those who are eagerly waiting for Him.

A note from the Reformers. The Reformers' protest against the Mass as a repeated sacrifice of Christ rests squarely on this chapter. Hebrews will not allow the cross to be treated as an installment: Christ offered Himself once for all, obtained eternal redemption, and sat down. What Rome called unbloody repetition, this preacher calls impossible — and the conscience set free by "once for all" has never wanted to go back.

Reflection question: Christ entered once for all and obtained eternal redemption — do you ever find yourself trying to re-enter, re-pay, or re-qualify, as if His entrance were not enough?
