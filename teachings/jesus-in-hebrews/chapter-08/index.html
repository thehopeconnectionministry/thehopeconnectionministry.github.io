Hebrews 8 — A Better Covenant: Sins Remembered No More

Jesus in Hebrews — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Hebrews
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

The seated Priest ministers a better covenant with better promises — God writes His law on the heart and remembers sins no more, and the old shadows honorably retire. Jesus is revealed here as the High Priest seated at the right hand of the throne of the Majesty in heaven, minister of the true tent pitched by the Lord and not by man, and mediator of a covenant in which every promise runs on God's own verbs: I will put, I will write, I will be their God, I will be merciful, I will remember no more.

Commentary

The main point: a seated Priest in a true tent (8:1–5). The preacher now does something a preacher rarely does — he announces his main point outright: "Now the point in what we are saying is this: we have such a high priest, one who is seated at the right hand of the throne of the Majesty in heaven, a minister in the holy places, in the true tent that the Lord set up, not man" (8:1–2). A Person, in a place. Seated — the posture of finished work — at the right hand of the Majesty. And ministering not in the earthly copy but in the true tent, the heavenly original, "that the Lord set up, not man." The earthly tabernacle was real and God-given, but it was always a copy: the priests on earth "serve a copy and shadow of the heavenly things. For when Moses was about to erect the tent, he was instructed by God, saying, 'See that you make everything according to the pattern that was shown you on the mountain'" (8:5, quoting Exodus 25:40). A shadow is not a lie — a shadow proves a substance is standing in the light somewhere. The old sanctuary honored its Maker by pointing past itself. If Jesus had been on earth, the preacher adds, He would not have been a priest at all under the old arrangement, since priests of Aaron's line already served there (8:4). His priesthood belongs to heaven's tent, and to a better covenant — which is where the sermon now turns.

A better covenant on better promises (8:6–7). "But as it is, Christ has obtained a ministry that is as much more excellent than the old as the covenant he mediates is better, since it is enacted on better promises" (8:6). Better covenant, better promises, better ministry — the comparative runs through the whole book like a drumbeat. "For if that first covenant had been faultless, there would have been no occasion to look for a second" (8:7). Note where the fault lay — the next verse is careful: "For he finds fault with them" — with the people, not first with the covenant's Maker. The first covenant was faulted by the people who broke it almost before the book of the covenant was dry; a law written on stone outside us cannot change the heart inside us, and Israel proved it for fifteen centuries. The old covenant could command. It could not convert. Something better had to come — and God had promised it, through Jeremiah, in the longest Old Testament quotation in the New Testament.

Jeremiah's promise, quoted in full (8:8–12). The preacher quotes Jeremiah 31:31–34 at length, and we should read it the way the Reformers taught the church to read it — by counting the verbs, because every one of them belongs to God. "Behold, the days are coming, declares the Lord, when I will establish a new covenant with the house of Israel and with the house of Judah, not like the covenant that I made with their fathers on the day when I took them by the hand to bring them out of the land of Egypt. For they did not continue in my covenant, and so I showed no concern for them, declares the Lord" (8:8–9). Then the new covenant's own terms:

"For this is the covenant that I will make with the house of Israel after those days, declares the Lord: I will put my laws into their minds, and write them on their hearts, and I will be their God, and they shall be my people." — Hebrews 8:10 (ESV)

I will put. I will write. I will be their God. The law moves from stone to heart — from an external code pressing in to an internal love written by God's own hand. "And they shall not teach, each one his neighbor and each one his brother, saying, 'Know the Lord,' for they shall all know me, from the least of them to the greatest" (8:11) — knowing God is no longer the privilege of a priestly class; it belongs to the least. And then the covenant's foundation stone, the verse on which the whole promise rests: "For I will be merciful toward their iniquities, and I will remember their sins no more" (8:12). Forgiveness is not one blessing among many in the new covenant. It is the ground of all the rest. God writes, God forgives, God forgets — by His own sworn decision, not by pretending the sins were small.

The old, honorably obsolete (8:13). "In speaking of a new covenant, he makes the first one obsolete. And what is becoming obsolete and growing old is ready to vanish away" (8:13). The old is not insulted. It is honorably declared obsolete, having done its work of pointing here — the way a scaffold is honored by the building it served, and then taken down. For the readers of this letter, with the temple still standing and the old sacrifices still smoking a few streets away, this sentence was the whole battle: the shadows had served their purpose. The substance had come. To go back now would not be humility. It would be trading the building for the scaffold.

From the least to the greatest — and the copy that taught (8:5, 11). Two comforts hide in this chapter's details. The first is in Jeremiah's promise: "they shall all know me, from the least of them to the greatest." The new covenant levels the knowledge of God. Under the old, a man approached God through a priestly caste that knew the holy things on his behalf. Under the new, the least believer — the newest convert, the simplest saint — knows the Lord Himself, directly, by the writing of the law on his own heart. No Christian is a second-class citizen of this covenant. The second comfort is the shadow itself. The earthly priests "serve a copy and shadow of the heavenly things" — and a copy, faithfully made from a pattern shown on a mountain, is a teacher. For fifteen centuries the tabernacle taught Israel, in furniture and blood, exactly what sin costs and exactly how God must be approached. The shadow was a school. Christians who honor the old covenant's types — who study the tent, the altar, and the Day of Atonement — are not going backward. They are reading the textbook the substance was illustrated in, and they will love the substance more for it.

"Ready to vanish" — reading the old with honor (8:13). The preacher's word for the first covenant is obsolete — and the tense matters: it is "becoming obsolete and growing old" and "ready to vanish away." Writing before AD 70, he describes a system still standing but already sentenced, like a building condemned but not yet demolished. Within a few years the Romans would level the temple, and the sacrificial system the readers were tempted to return to would cease to exist at all — God's own providence removing the scaffold the preacher said was ready to fall. There is a lesson in the tone. The preacher never mocks the old covenant. He calls its promises real, its pattern heaven-shown, its priests God-appointed. Fulfillment is not contempt. The Christian reads the Old Testament the way an heir reads the family letters of the house he now owns: with gratitude, with honor, and with no thought of moving back into the old rooms. The shadows were God's. The substance is better. Both sentences are true, and Hebrews never lets us drop either one.

Key verses

"Now the point in what we are saying is this: we have such a high priest, one who is seated at the right hand of the throne of the Majesty in heaven," — Hebrews 8:1 (ESV)

"But as it is, Christ has obtained a ministry that is as much more excellent than the old as the covenant he mediates is better, since it is enacted on better promises." — Hebrews 8:6 (ESV)

"For I will be merciful toward their iniquities, and I will remember their sins no more." — Hebrews 8:12 (ESV)

"In speaking of a new covenant, he makes the first one obsolete. And what is becoming obsolete and growing old is ready to vanish away." — Hebrews 8:13 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Hebrews 8 is why a new covenant was needed at all. The first covenant was broken by the people almost before the ink was dry; a law written on stone outside us cannot change the heart inside us, and we proved it for fifteen centuries. Commanded, we could be. Converted, the old covenant could not make us. But the good news is whose verbs carry the new covenant: "I will put my laws into their minds. I will write them on their hearts. I will be their God… For I will be merciful toward their iniquities. I will remember their sins no more." God writes, God forgives, God forgets. The seated Priest ministers this covenant from the true tent in heaven, and the old shadows are not scorned — they are honorably retired, having done their work of pointing here.

A note from the Reformers. Grace alone in God's own grammar: read Jeremiah's promise as the preacher quotes it and count the verbs. I will put, I will write, I will be their God, I will be merciful, I will remember no more. The new covenant does not ask the sinner to climb; it announces what God will do. The Reformers built their doctrine of grace on sentences exactly like these.

Reflection question: God promises to remember your sins no more — is there a forgiven sin you keep remembering against yourself that God has told you, in writing, He has forgotten?
