The Hope Connection
Lewis Fuller

"looking to Jesus, the founder and perfecter of our faith, who for the joy that was set before him endured the cross, despising the shame, and is seated at the right hand of the throne of God." — Hebrews 12:2 (ESV)

Introduction — Hebrews at a Glance

Hebrews is a sermon that arrived by mail. It does not read like any other letter in the New Testament. There is no greeting naming the writer, no list of churches, no travel plans at the start. Instead it opens like a preacher standing up, clearing his throat, and delivering the most majestic first sentence in the New Testament: "Long ago, at many times and in many ways, God spoke to our fathers by the prophets, but in these last days he has spoken to us by his Son." Everything in this book flows from that sentence. God has spoken. His final Word is not a principle, not a ritual, not a system. His final Word is a Person — His Son. And the whole sermon that follows is one long, careful, urgent argument: this Son is greater than everything you are tempted to go back to. Greater than angels. Greater than Moses. Greater than Aaron and every priest who ever wore the breastplate. His covenant is better, His sanctuary is better, His sacrifice is better — and His sacrifice, unlike all the others, is finished. He offered Himself once for all, and then He did what no priest under the old covenant ever did. He sat down.

Who wrote it. Here we have to be honest, because the church has been honest about it for two thousand years: we do not know who wrote Hebrews. The letter itself never names its author. Some have said Paul — and the letter does end with greetings from Timothy and a note about "our brother Timothy" being released (13:23), which puts it in Paul's circle — but the style is more polished, more oratorical, than anything else Paul wrote, and Paul usually names himself at the start. Others have proposed Apollos, the eloquent Alexandrian who was "mighty in the Scriptures" (Acts 18:24) — a man who fits the book's learning and rhetoric well. Barnabas has been proposed; so has Luke; so, in modern times, has Priscilla. The early church father Origen, writing in the third century, looked at all the evidence and concluded, in words the church has quoted ever since, that who wrote the epistle, "God knows." That is where the matter still rests, and it does not trouble us. What matters is not the name we cannot recover but the authority we can: the church received this book as Scripture — apostolic in its teaching, in full agreement with the rest of the New Testament, and bearing the marks of the Holy Spirit's own authorship. The preacher hid his name, perhaps on purpose. The sermon is about the Son, not about the man who preached it. As John the Baptist said of himself, the friend of the bridegroom is content to decrease so that Christ increases.

Who it was written for. The readers were Jewish Christians — believers who had come to faith in Jesus out of Judaism, who knew the Old Testament Scriptures deeply, and who were now under pressure. Persecution had already cost some of them their property (10:32–34). The old way — the temple, the priests, the sacrifices, the feasts — was still standing, still visible, still familiar, and it was calling them back. Not back to paganism. Back to the shadows: to a respectable, ancestral, God-given religion that had done its work and was now fulfilled. Their danger was not dramatic apostasy in a single decision. It was drift — the slow, quiet floating past the harbor that this book warns against in its very first exhortation (2:1). Hebrews was written to tired, pressured believers who were tempted to trade the substance for the shadow because the shadow felt safer. If you have ever been tempted to go back to an old life that at least felt familiar, this book was written for exactly that moment.

When it was written. The letter was most likely written before AD 70, when the Roman armies destroyed Jerusalem and its temple. The argument of chapters 8–10 depends on the tabernacle and temple system still operating: the writer speaks of priests offering gifts and sacrifices in the present tense, and he never mentions the temple's destruction — an event that would have been the strongest possible proof of his point that the old order was passing away. So we are somewhere in the 60s of the first century, roughly AD 60–68, while the old system still stood and still beckoned.

The shape of the book. Hebrews is a sermon-letter, and it moves in great waves of doctrine and warning. Chapters 1–2: the Son greater than angels — God's final Word, and the crowned Man who tasted death for everyone. Chapters 3–4: Jesus greater than Moses, and the rest of God still open to those who hear His voice today. Chapters 5–7: Jesus our High Priest, appointed by the Father, after the order of Melchizedek — a priesthood that never ends. Chapters 8–10: the heart of the book — a better covenant on better promises, a heavenly sanctuary, and one sacrifice, once for all, that perfects forever those who are being sanctified. Chapter 11: the hall of faith, the great roll of witnesses who lived and died by faith in the promise. Chapters 12–13: the race set before us, the Father's discipline, the unshakable kingdom, and the life lived outside the camp with Jesus — ending with benediction. Woven through the whole sermon are five great warning passages — 2:1–4; 3:7–4:13; 5:11–6:20; 10:26–39; and 12:25–29 — and they must be read at full strength, the way the preacher preached them. They are not decorations. They are one of God's own means of keeping His people, and this commentary treats them that way: real warnings, aimed at real drift, that drive us to hold fast to Christ — and always, in this book, paired with the anchor of God's promise. Warning and anchor together. That is how Hebrews handles its hearers, and it is how we will handle Hebrews.

The key verse. The whole book gathers itself into one sentence, spoken as the preacher calls his tired people to run:

"looking to Jesus, the founder and perfecter of our faith, who for the joy that was set before him endured the cross, despising the shame, and is seated at the right hand of the throne of God." — Hebrews 12:2 (ESV)

That is Hebrews in miniature. Jesus began the faith and Jesus will finish it. He endured the cross. He is seated — the work done — at the right hand of the throne of God. And the theme running through every chapter is this: Jesus the Son greater than angels, greater than Moses, and greater than every priest — our eternal High Priest and once-for-all sacrifice, the pioneer and perfecter of faith.

Read in the Reformation way. This commentary reads Hebrews the way the Reformers taught the church to read every book of the Bible, and Hebrews may be the most Reformation book in the New Testament — the Reformers leaned on it constantly. Sola scriptura — Scripture alone: God has spoken finally and fully in His Son, and that speech comes to us preserved in the written Word; there is no further revelation competing with Christ and no authority above the Scripture that bears Him. Sola gratia — grace alone: the new covenant runs on God's verbs — "I will put my laws into their minds… I will be their God… I will remember their sins no more" (8:10–12) — a better covenant enacted on better promises, in which God does what sinners cannot. Sola fide — faith alone: chapter 11 is the great proof that God's people have always lived by faith in the promise, from Abel onward, and 10:38 sets the terms: "my righteous one shall live by faith." Solus Christus — Christ alone: the once-for-all sacrifice is the drumbeat of this book — the Greek word is ephapax, once and never again (7:27; 9:12; 10:10) — and when the offering was complete, the Priest sat down, leaving nothing for any priest, ritual, or merit of ours to add. And soli Deo gloria — glory to God alone: the book ends in doxology, ascribing all working in us to "the God of peace… through Jesus Christ, to whom be glory forever and ever" (13:20–21). Along the way you will meet brief nods to Calvin — who wrote a commentary on this very letter — to Luther, who lectured on Hebrews early in the Reformation, and to Spurgeon, who preached its warnings and its anchor with equal force. A line or two, never a lecture. And in every chapter the law and the gospel are kept distinct: the law shows us our sin and our need (the bad news), and the gospel shows us Christ and His finished work (the good news). Hebrews itself preaches that way — warning and promise, thunder and anchor — and so does this commentary.

How to use this commentary. Each chapter section begins with "Jesus in this chapter" — a short look at how Christ is revealed there. Then the commentary walks the chapter's movements in order, explaining the text plainly and fully. Key verses are quoted in full from the English Standard Version. Each chapter closes with "The bad news and the good news" — the law-then-gospel pattern, the weight of the chapter and the hope in Christ — and one reflection question to carry into prayer. Read it slowly. Read it with your Bible open. Hebrews is dense — the richest meat in the New Testament — so chew it slowly. And remember, as you read, that the Priest this book describes is not a memory. He lives forever, and He is interceding for His people right now.

How to Use This Commentary

This commentary was built to be used, not just read. Here is how to get the most out of it.

The order of every session. Work through each chapter the same way, in this order:

  1. Read the chapter in your own Bible first. Open your Bible to the chapter of Hebrews and read it all the way through before you read a word of commentary. The commentary serves the Scripture; it never replaces it. Read it out loud if you can — Hebrews was a sermon before it was a letter, and it was written to be heard. You will catch things with your ears that your eyes slide past.
  2. Then read the commentary section for that chapter. Keep your Bible open beside it and look up the verses as they come. Start with "Jesus in this chapter" — before anything else, fix your eyes on how Christ is revealed there. That is not a slogan for this book; it is the book's own command: "Consider Jesus" (3:1); "looking to Jesus" (12:2).
  3. Then sit with the reflection question. Each chapter ends with one reflection question and a "bad news and good news" paragraph. Do not rush past them. Read the bad news honestly — let the law do its searching work, especially in the warning passages — and then read the good news slowly, out loud if you can. Answer the reflection question in prayer, out loud to God, not just in your head.
  4. Write in a notebook. Keep a simple notebook with this study. Write down your answer to the reflection question, your answer to the study guide question for that chapter (see the Hebrews Study Guide below), and the memory verse for the chapter. A truth is not really yours until you have written it, prayed it, and lived it. You do not need anything fancy — a dollar notebook and a pen will do the whole letter.

The rhythm: three sessions a week. This fits the study rhythm used across The Hope Connection: three sessions a week, one book at a time, until the book is done. At one chapter per session, three sessions a week, you will walk through all thirteen chapters of Hebrews in about four and a half weeks. If you want to go slower — and with Hebrews, slower is often better — spread one chapter across the whole week: session one, read the chapter in your Bible and the "Jesus in this chapter" opening; session two, read the commentary; session three, return to the chapter, work through the questions, and pray it back to God. Either pace is fine. What matters is the steadiness, not the speed.

No catch-up piles. This rule is not negotiable, because it protects the whole habit: if you miss a day, you do not owe anybody two chapters. You simply pick up at the next chapter at the next session. Life happens — sickness, family, work, hard weeks. A study rhythm that piles up guilt every time life interrupts will not survive a month. This one bends and keeps going. Missed a week? Start again at the next chapter. The Lord is not keeping a tally, and neither are we.

Using it alone. Everything above is written for one reader, one Bible, one notebook, and the Lord. Pray before you open the book — a short, real prayer: "Lord, open my eyes, and let this Word search me." Hebrews 4 promises the Word will. Pray again when you finish, turning the chapter's good news into thanksgiving and its bad news into confession.

Using it with a spouse or a friend. Read the chapter out loud together at the start of the session — take turns reading. Then read the commentary, or take turns reading it aloud too. Talk through the reflection question and the study guide question together, honestly. Pray for each other out of what the chapter showed you. Hebrews is a book about holding fast together — "exhort one another every day" (3:13) is one of its commands — and two people studying it together will see things one reader alone will miss, and will hold each other up the way this letter commands.

Using it in a small group. One chapter per meeting. Have someone read the chapter aloud from the Bible to open. Walk through the commentary section together, stopping wherever the text raises a question. Save real time at the end for the reflection question and the study guide question — that is where study becomes life. Close in prayer, and let the chapter's memory verse be the group's verse for the week: read it together at the start of the next meeting and see who can say it from memory. Keep the group small enough that everybody talks, and keep the door open — Hebrews was written for tired believers, and every chapter here is written plain enough for a struggling Christian to follow and be anchored by.

The memory verses. The study guide below gives one memory verse for each chapter — thirteen verses, the spine of Hebrews. If you take one verse a week alongside the chapter rhythm, by the time you finish the book you will carry its whole argument in your heart: the radiance of God's glory, the throne of grace, the anchor of the soul, the uttermost salvation, the sins remembered no more, and the Jesus who is the same yesterday and today and forever. That is treasure no one can take from you.

Study Questions — One per Chapter

Work each question in your notebook after the chapter's reflection question. Many of them follow the Bible's own pattern of putting off the old and putting on the new (Ephesians 4:22–24; Colossians 3:8–10).

  1. Hebrews 1 — Hear the Son. God's final word is a Person, not a principle. Put off looking for God to speak in ways that bypass the Son He has already spoken by; put on listening to Jesus in the written Word. Where will you hear Him this week — name the passage and the time?
  2. Hebrews 2 — Tie up against the drift. Drifting, not rebellion, is this chapter's warning. Put off one neglect that is loosening your hold — skipped prayer, an unread Bible, absence from God's people. Put on one concrete act of tying back up to Christ this week. What is it, and when?
  3. Hebrews 3 — Answer Today. The wilderness generation heard God's voice for forty years and hardened anyway. Put off one "tomorrow" you have been giving God; put on obedience today, before the day is over. What is He saying to you, and what will holding fast look like in one specific act?
  4. Hebrews 4 — Draw near. The throne you are invited to approach is a throne of grace, and the Priest on it sympathizes with your weakness. Put off hiding one need — from God in prayer, and if needed from a trusted brother or sister. Put on bold prayer: bring that need to the throne today, in time of need. Name the need.
  5. Hebrews 5 — Take solid food. The preacher grieved over believers still on milk when they should have been teachers. Put off one spiritual baby-food habit — only ever being fed, never feeding on the Word yourself. Put on one solid-food step this week: a chapter studied slowly, a doctrine learned, a younger believer encouraged. Which step?
  6. Hebrews 6 — Check the anchor, not the waves. Your hope is anchored inside the veil, where Jesus already is. Put off measuring God's hold on you by your feelings and circumstances; put on remembering His promise and His oath. When the storm pulls this week, what truth about God's grip will you say out loud?
  7. Hebrews 7 — Pray like you are prayed for. Jesus is interceding for you right now, and He never leaves office. Put off prayerlessness and prayer that apologizes for existing; put on drawing near through Him with confidence. How will your praying change this week knowing your case is being spoken for in heaven at this moment?
  8. Hebrews 8 — Let God forget what He has forgiven. God promises, in writing, to remember your sins no more. Put off remembering one forgiven sin against yourself — rehearsing it, re-confessing it as if the blood had not covered it. Put on receiving the new covenant's promise as God's own word to you. Which sin will you hand over to His forgetfulness?
  9. Hebrews 9 — Stop trying to re-enter. Christ entered once for all and obtained eternal redemption. Put off trying to re-pay, re-qualify, or re-earn a standing He already secured; put on resting in the once-for-all offering and eagerly waiting for Him. Where have you been trying to add an installment to a finished payment?
  10. Hebrews 10 — Hold fast together. Perfected forever, and being sanctified now — both at once. Put off shrinking back and skipping the gathering of God's people; put on holding fast and stirring someone up to love and good works this week. Who will you encourage, and when will you do it?
  11. Hebrews 11 — Hold the promise you cannot see. The witnesses believed without receiving in their lifetimes, and God was not ashamed to be called their God. Put off demanding to see before you trust; put on one act of obedience to a promise you may not see fulfilled this side of heaven. What promise are you holding, and what is the act?
  12. Hebrews 12 — Lay aside the weight. Races are run by looking — away from the weights, up to Jesus. Put off one weight and one clinging sin that are slowing your stride; put on fixing your eyes on Him in a set daily practice. Name the weight, the sin, and the practice.
  13. Hebrews 13 — Be content in the Same Jesus. "I will never leave you nor forsake you" is God's own answer to discontent. Put off reaching for one thing you think you need in order to be okay; put on contentment and brotherly love — one act of hospitality, remembrance, or generosity this week. What are you reaching for, and what will you do instead?
