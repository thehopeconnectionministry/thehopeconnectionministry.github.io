Hebrews 7 — The Deathless Priest: He Saves to the Uttermost

Jesus in Hebrews — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Hebrews
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Dying priests give way to the deathless Priest — Melchizedek's greater Son saves to the uttermost, because He never stops interceding and never leaves office. Jesus is revealed here as the High Priest after the order of Melchizedek: indestructible in life, permanent in office, holy and unstained, who offered Himself once and needs no sacrifice for His own sins — and who consequently saves completely and forever all who draw near to God through Him, since He always lives to make intercession for them.

Commentary

Melchizedek, king of righteousness (7:1–3). The preacher returns to the mysterious figure he had to postpone in chapter 5. Melchizedek appears in Genesis 14 for a few verses: king of Salem, priest of the Most High God, who met Abraham returning from the rescue of Lot, brought out bread and wine, blessed him, and received a tenth of everything from him — and then vanishes from the record. The preacher reads him the way Scripture presents him: "He is first, by translation of his name, king of righteousness, and then he is also king of Salem, that is, king of peace" (7:2). Righteousness first, then peace — the order matters; peace is righteousness's fruit. "He is without father, without mother, without genealogy, having neither beginning of days nor end of life, but resembling the Son of God he continues a priest forever" (7:3). This is not a claim that Melchizedek was eternal; it is a reading of the record. Genesis gives every other major figure a genealogy and a death notice. For this priest: neither. As Scripture presents him, he is a living preview — a type — of a priesthood that does not end, resembling the Son of God, who is a priest forever in reality and not only in silhouette.

The lesser is blessed by the greater (7:4–10). The preacher's argument is from the tithe. Abraham — the patriarch, the father of the nation, the receiver of the promises — gave Melchizedek a tenth of the spoils. And "it is beyond dispute that the inferior is blessed by the superior" (7:7): Melchizedek blessed Abraham, not the other way around. Even Levi — the tribe of the old priesthood — "who receives tithes, paid tithes through Abraham, so to speak, for he was still in the loins of his ancestor when Melchizedek met him" (7:9–10). In Abraham, Levi bowed to Melchizedek's priesthood before Levi existed. The Levitical priests, moreover, are "mortal men" who receive tithes and die; of Melchizedek "it is testified that he lives" (7:8). The old priesthood itself, in its own founding father, once acknowledged a greater priesthood above it. The readers tempted to go back to Aaron's line are being shown that Aaron's line was never the top of the mountain.

Perfection was not through Levi — a change of law (7:11–19). "Now if perfection had been attainable through the Levitical priesthood (for under it the people received the law), what further need would there have been for another priest to arise after the order of Melchizedek, rather than one named after the order of Aaron?" (7:11). The very existence of Psalm 110:4 — God's oath appointing a different kind of priest, spoken centuries after Aaron — proves the Aaronic system could not finish the job. The bad news is structural: a priesthood of dying men can never finish; the sacrifices repeat because the sacrificers keep being carried out to their own graves. And a change of priesthood means a change of law: Jesus comes from Judah, a tribe Moses never connected with priests — His priesthood rests not on "a legal requirement concerning bodily descent, but by the power of an indestructible life" (7:16). Indestructible life. The old law made nothing perfect, "but on the other hand, a better hope is introduced, through which we draw near to God" (7:19). Draw near — the thing the old system, for all its beauty, could never finally let sinners do.

Sworn with an oath — a better covenant's guarantee (7:20–22). The old priests were made priests "without an oath." Jesus was made Priest with one, by the God who said, "The Lord has sworn and will not change his mind, 'You are a priest forever'" (7:21, quoting Psalm 110:4). God does not swear carelessly, and He does not repent of His oaths. "This makes Jesus the guarantor of a better covenant" (7:22). A guarantor is a surety — one who pledges himself for another's debt and another's performance. Jesus does not merely mediate the better covenant from a distance. He personally guarantees it, with His own indestructible life as the pledge.

He continues forever — the uttermost (7:23–25). Now the chapter's summit: "The former priests were many in number, because they were prevented by death from continuing in office, but he holds his priesthood permanently, because he continues forever" (7:23–24). Many priests, because death kept interrupting. One Priest now, permanently, because He continues forever. And the conclusion is the most complete salvation sentence in the Bible:

"Consequently, he is able to save to the uttermost those who draw near to God through him, since he always lives to make intercession for them." — Hebrews 7:25 (ESV)

The Greek phrase is eis to panteles — "to the uttermost": completely and forever at the same time, to the very end of the worst case. The salvation Jesus gives is total in its reach — no sinner too far gone — and endless in its duration, because the Savior doing the saving lives forever. And the mechanism is intercession, present tense, always: He always lives to make intercession for them. Your case is being spoken for in heaven at this moment, by a Priest who never sleeps, never retires, and never dies.

Such a High Priest (7:26–28). "For it was indeed fitting that we should have such a high priest, holy, innocent, unstained, separated from sinners, and exalted above the heavens" (7:26). Unlike the old priests, "he has no need, like those high priests, to offer sacrifices daily, first for his own sins and then for those of the people, since he did this once for all when he offered up himself" (7:27). Once for all — ephapax — when He offered up Himself. The Priest and the offering are one. "For the law appoints men in their weakness as high priests, but the word of the oath, which came later than the law, appoints a Son who has been made perfect forever" (7:28). Weak men, rotating in and out by death — or a perfected Son, forever. The preacher rests his case.

The word of the oath, later than the law (7:28). The chapter's final contrast is one of chronology with a point. "The law appoints men in their weakness as high priests, but the word of the oath, which came later than the law, appoints a Son who has been made perfect forever." Later — Psalm 110 was sung centuries after Sinai, which means God always intended the Aaronic priesthood to be succeeded. The oath-priest was not God's emergency repair after the first system failed; He was the announced successor, published in Israel's own hymnbook while the first system was still running. And the two appointments differ in everything that matters: men in weakness, many, dying — a Son made perfect forever, one, indestructible. "Made perfect" is the letter's language for Jesus completed through suffering for His office (2:10; 5:9): the road through Gethsemane and the cross was the making of this Priest. A priesthood that required the Priest's own suffering to establish it will never be too proud, or too distant, to stoop to the suffering of the people it serves.

Draw near — the priesthood's whole purpose (7:19, 25). Twice this chapter states the end for which the deathless Priest exists: "a better hope is introduced, through which we draw near to God" (7:19); He saves "those who draw near to God through him" (7:25). The old priesthood, for all its God-given beauty, was a system of distances: courts within courts, a veil, one man once a year, and the people standing far off. The new priesthood is a system of nearness. The Priest is permanent so that the nearness can be permanent; He intercedes always so that the welcome never lapses. Draw near, then, is not a suggestion for the especially spiritual. It is the ordinary traffic of the Christian life, the thing the whole apparatus of heaven exists to make possible. A believer who stays at a distance — praying rarely, confessing seldom, treating God as a last resort — is living as though Aaron still held the office. He does not. The office is held by a deathless Priest whose entire work is to bring people near and keep them near.

Key verses

"For this Melchizedek, king of Salem, priest of the Most High God, met Abraham returning from the slaughter of the kings and blessed him," — Hebrews 7:1 (ESV)

"but he holds his priesthood permanently, because he continues forever." — Hebrews 7:24 (ESV)

"Consequently, he is able to save to the uttermost those who draw near to God through him, since he always lives to make intercession for them." — Hebrews 7:25 (ESV)

"For it was indeed fitting that we should have such a high priest, holy, innocent, unstained, separated from sinners, and exalted above the heavens." — Hebrews 7:26 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Hebrews 7 is structural, and it is written on every page of Israel's history: the priests die. A priesthood of mortal, weak men — men who must offer first for their own sins — can never finish the job; perfection was never attainable through Levi, and the sacrifices repeated because the sacrificers kept being carried out to their own graves. But the good news is the permanent Priest. Jesus holds His priesthood forever, because He continues forever, sworn in by God's own oath and standing as guarantor of a better covenant. He needed no sacrifice for His own sins; He offered up Himself, once. And consequently — consequently — He is able to save to the uttermost those who draw near to God through Him, since He always lives to make intercession for them. To the uttermost: completely, and forever.

A note from the Reformers. One living Priest means no other mediator is needed or permitted to stand between the sinner and God. The Reformers' protest against a whole economy of lesser mediators rests on this chapter: the intercession that saves us is not distributed among the departed. It is concentrated in a living Christ, and it never pauses.

Reflection question: Jesus is interceding for you right now, and He never leaves office — how would your praying change if you remembered that your case is being spoken for in heaven at this moment?
