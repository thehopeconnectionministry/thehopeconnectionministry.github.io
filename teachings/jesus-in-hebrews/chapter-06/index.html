Hebrews 6 — Hold Fast to the Anchor: Promise, Oath, and the Veil

Jesus in Hebrews — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Hebrews
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Our hope is an anchor, not a wish — fixed inside the veil where the Forerunner has already gone, held fast by a God who swore an oath He cannot break. Jesus is revealed here as the Forerunner who has entered the inner place behind the curtain on our behalf, the High Priest forever after the order of Melchizedek, and as the ground of the strongest assurance in Scripture: God's promise confirmed by God's oath, two unchangeable things in which it is impossible for God to lie.

Commentary

Press on to maturity (6:1–3). The preacher will not stay at the foundation. "Therefore let us leave the elementary doctrine of Christ and go on to maturity, not laying again a foundation of repentance from dead works and of faith toward God, and of instruction about washings, the laying on of hands, the resurrection of the dead, and eternal judgment" (6:1–2). These foundations are good — repentance, faith, resurrection, judgment are the ABCs of the faith — but a foundation exists to be built on, not to be re-poured every year. "And this we will do if God permits" (6:3) — even the pressing on is in God's hands. What follows is the severest passage in the letter, and we take it at full strength, the way the preacher preached it.

The severe warning (6:4–8). "For it is impossible, in the case of those who have once been enlightened, who have tasted the heavenly gift, and have shared in the Holy Spirit, and have tasted the goodness of the word of God and the powers of the age to come, and then have fallen away, to restore them again to repentance, since they are crucifying once again the Son of God to their own harm and holding him up to contempt" (6:4–6). Read it honestly. The preacher describes people who have stood in the full light of the church's life — enlightened, tasting, sharing — and then have fallen away in settled, contemptuous rejection, treating the crucified Son as a thing of shame. For such, while they hold Christ up to contempt, restoration is impossible — not because God's mercy is small, but because they are refusing the only remedy there is, and refusing it with open eyes. There is no second cross to run to. The illustration follows: land that drinks the rain and bears fruit receives a blessing; land that bears thorns and thistles "is worthless and near to being cursed, and its end is to be burned" (6:7–8). This warning is real, and it is one of God's own means of keeping His people: it is meant to make us hold fast rather than drift, to frighten us off the road to apostasy before our feet are on it. Never soften it into a hypothetical. Preach it the way the book does — at full strength — and then keep reading, because the preacher himself does not leave his hearers standing in the ashes of it.

"We are sure of better things in your case" (6:9–12). Hear the turn, in the preacher's own words: "Though we speak in this way, yet in your case, beloved, we feel sure of better things — things that belong to salvation" (6:9). In your case. The warning was not his verdict on them; it was his guardrail for them. And his confidence rests on evidence and on God's character: "For God is not unjust so as to overlook your work and the love that you have shown for his name in serving the saints, as you still do" (6:10). God sees the service. God does not forget. "And we desire each one of you to show the same earnestness to have the full assurance of hope until the end, so that you may not be sluggish, but imitators of those who through faith and patience inherit the promises" (6:11–12). Full assurance of hope — held to the end — is this chapter's goal, and the road to it is faith and patience, imitating the promise-inheritors rather than the sluggish.

Promise and oath — two unchangeable things (6:13–18). Now the preacher grounds the whole matter where it belongs: not in our grip on God, but in God's grip on us. His example is Abraham, who, "having patiently waited, obtained the promise" (6:15). When God made the promise, "since he had no one greater by whom to swear, he swore by himself" (6:13). An oath, among men, ends dispute by appealing to something greater. God, having nothing greater, appealed to Himself — stooping to give His people the strongest possible confirmation: "So when God desired to show more convincingly to the heirs of the promise the unchangeable character of his purpose, he guaranteed it with an oath, so that by two unchangeable things, in which it is impossible for God to lie, we who have fled for refuge might have strong encouragement to hold fast to the hope set before us" (6:17–18). Two unchangeable things: His promise, and His oath. In both, it is impossible for God to lie. And notice who this assurance is for: "we who have fled for refuge" — fugitives, people who ran to God the way a manslayer ran to a city of refuge. Strong encouragement is the fugitive's portion.

The anchor inside the veil (6:19–20). And then, the verse this ministry is built on:

"We have this as a sure and steadfast anchor of the soul, a hope that enters into the inner place behind the curtain, where Jesus has gone as a forerunner on our behalf, having become a high priest forever after the order of Melchizedek." — Hebrews 6:19–20 (ESV)

The Greek word is ankyra — an anchor. It is the only anchor named in the New Testament, and its quality is in its location. An ordinary anchor drags along the seabed, down among the shifting circumstances. This one goes up — through the curtain, into heaven itself, into the inner place — and holds there, because of who is there: Jesus, the Forerunner, who has already entered on our behalf. A forerunner is not the whole company; he is the first of it, the scout who has reached the destination the rest are traveling toward. The ship may toss. The anchor does not drag, because the anchor is not in the sea. It is in the veil, fixed to the throne, held by the Priest who is already home. As Lewis has put it in his own words: "I am not held up by my grip on God. I am held by His grip on me." That is chapter 6 in one sentence. The security is not the strength of the sailor's hand on the rope. It is the anchor, the oath, and the Forerunner — all God's doing, all unchangeable.

Imitators of the promise-inheritors (6:11–15). Between the warning and the oath stands a pastoral bridge that is easy to miss: the call to imitate "those who through faith and patience inherit the promises." Abraham is the pattern — and Abraham's patience was not a short wait. The promise of a son came when he was seventy-five; Isaac was born when he was a hundred. Twenty-five years of waiting, with failures inside the waiting — the Hagar detour was Abraham's too. And still the preacher says he "patiently waited" and "obtained the promise." That is the company we are told to imitate: not the instantly rewarded, but the long-waiting, whose faith outlasted their own mistakes because it rested on the Promiser rather than on their performance. Sluggishness is the chapter's named enemy — the same dullness rebuked in chapter 5 — and its opposite is not frantic activity but earnest, patient, promise-holding endurance. The anchor holds during the wait. That is what anchors are for: not the harbor, but the storm and the long night before the harbor.

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Hebrews 6 is the severest in the letter, and we do not soften it: for those who have stood in the full light — enlightened, tasting the heavenly gift, sharing in the Holy Spirit — and then fall away into settled contempt of the Son, crucifying Him afresh and holding Him up to shame, there is no second remedy to restore them while they refuse the only one there is. Land that drinks the rain and bears only thorns is near to being cursed. That warning is real, and God uses it to keep His people holding fast. But the good news is the preacher's own next breath: "in your case, beloved, we feel sure of better things — things that belong to salvation." And the ground of that sureness is not our grip but God's: His promise and His oath, two unchangeable things in which it is impossible for God to lie. Our hope is an anchor of the soul, sure and steadfast, fixed inside the veil — where Jesus, our Forerunner, has already gone on our behalf.

A note from the Reformers. The Reformers located assurance exactly where this chapter puts it: not in the strength of our feelings but in the promise and oath of God, who cannot lie. Calvin directed trembling believers to God's sworn word as the one firm ground — He who needed to swear by no one greater swore by Himself, so that we might be sure. Feelings rise and fall. The anchor is not in the boat; it is in the veil.

Reflection question: Your hope is anchored inside the veil, where Jesus already is — when the storm pulls at you, do you check the anchor, or do you check the waves?
