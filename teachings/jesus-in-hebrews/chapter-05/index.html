Hebrews 5 — Appointed by the Father: The Author of Eternal Salvation

Jesus in Hebrews — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Hebrews
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

The appointed Priest who prayed with loud cries and tears was heard, and learned obedience through suffering — and He is the author of an eternal salvation, not a temporary one. Jesus is revealed here as the High Priest chosen not by Himself but by the Father's own words in the Psalms, a priest forever after the order of Melchizedek, who in the days of His flesh offered up prayers with loud cries and tears, was heard because of His reverence, and — having been made perfect — became the source of eternal salvation to all who obey Him.

Commentary

What a high priest is (5:1–4). The preacher now explains the office Jesus fills, and he begins with the old priests, honorably. "For every high priest chosen from among men is appointed to act on behalf of men in relation to God, to offer gifts and sacrifices for sins" (5:1). Two qualifications mark the office. First, the priest must be one of the people — chosen from among men, to act for men. Second, he must be gentle with people like himself: "He can deal gently with the ignorant and wayward, since he himself is beset with weakness. Because of this he is obligated to offer sacrifice for his own sins just as he does for those of the people" (5:2–3). The Aaronic priest carried the people's sins with hands that had just offered for his own. And third, the office is not seized: "And no one takes this honor for himself, but only when called by God, just as Aaron was" (5:4). Priesthood is an appointment, not an ambition. Hold those three marks — solidarity, weakness, appointment — because the preacher is about to show us a Priest who fulfills the first and third beyond all measure, and transforms the second entirely.

Appointed by two Psalms (5:5–6). "So also Christ did not exalt himself to be made a high priest, but was appointed by him who said to him, 'You are my Son, today I have begotten you'" (5:5, quoting Psalm 2:7); "as he says also in another place, 'You are a priest forever, after the order of Melchizedek'" (5:6, quoting Psalm 110:4). Jesus did not grasp at the priesthood. The Father appointed Him, in Scripture's own words, with two decrees: Son by eternal right, and Priest by sworn office — a priest forever, and not after Aaron's order, which was already dying, but after Melchizedek's, the mysterious king-priest of Genesis 14 who appears in the record without beginning or end. Chapter 7 will unfold Melchizedek fully. Here the point is the appointment: the priesthood of Jesus is not a human arrangement. It is the Father's own ordaining word, spoken in the Psalms centuries before Bethlehem.

Loud cries and tears (5:7–10). Now the preacher shows us the Priest at prayer, and it is the most human picture of Jesus in the letter: "In the days of his flesh, Jesus offered up prayers and supplications, with loud cries and tears, to him who was able to save him from death, and he was heard because of his reverence" (5:7). Gethsemane is in this verse — the sweat, the agony, the "let this cup pass" prayed by a true man facing a real death. And He was heard — not by being spared the cross, but by being raised through it and seated above it; the Father answered the prayer at the resurrection, not instead of the cross but on the other side of it. "Although he was a son, he learned obedience through what he suffered" (5:8). Learned — not from disobedience to obedience, for He never disobeyed; He learned obedience the way a man learns a road, by walking it, experiencing its cost step by step until the lesson was in His bones. "And being made perfect, he became the source of eternal salvation to all who obey him, being designated by God a high priest after the order of Melchizedek" (5:9–10).

"And being made perfect, he became the source of eternal salvation to all who obey him," — Hebrews 5:9 (ESV)

Eternal salvation — not probationary, not provisional, not renewed annually like the old sacrifices. The source of it is a perfected Priest: perfected through suffering, designated by God. And it flows "to all who obey him" — obedience here is the obedience of faith, the pattern of a life that hears the Son and follows, the fruit of the salvation He authors.

Dull of hearing (5:11–14). Then the preacher pauses, and the pause stings. "About this we have much to say, and it is hard to explain, since you have become dull of hearing" (5:11). The Melchizedek teaching — the deep meat of this letter — is hard to serve to hearers who have gone dull. "For though by this time you ought to be teachers, you need someone to teach you again the basic principles of the oracles of God. You need milk, not solid food" (5:12). By now — after all this time in the faith — they should be teaching others. Instead they still need the ABCs. "For everyone who lives on milk is unskilled in the word of righteousness, since he is a child. But solid food is for the mature, for those who have their powers of discernment trained by constant practice to distinguish good from evil" (5:13–14). Maturity, in this book, is not a feeling or an age. It is trained discernment — senses exercised by constant practice, the way an athlete's body is trained by repetition. Spiritual laziness is a genuine danger for genuine believers, and this rebuke is the doorway into the severest warning in the letter, which comes next. The preacher loves his hearers too much to keep serving milk to grown people.

Solid food and trained senses (5:13–14). The preacher's picture of maturity deserves a closer look, because it is not what most people picture. The mature are not those who can repeat the most doctrine — though doctrine is the food — but those who have "their powers of discernment trained by constant practice to distinguish good from evil." The language is athletic: senses trained the way a body is trained, by repetition under load. A child lives on milk because milk is pre-digested; someone else did the work. Solid food must be chewed by the eater himself. So it is in the faith: the believer who only ever receives preaching, and never opens the Word to chew on it himself, never trains the senses — and untrained senses cannot tell good from evil when it matters, in the gray moment, under pressure. The remedy is not guilt but practice: steady portions of solid food, honestly chewed, in the chapter you are reading now. That is what this whole letter is — a preacher refusing to let grown people stay on the bottle.

He was heard — and what His hearing means for ours (5:7). Linger on one phrase: Jesus prayed to the One "who was able to save him from death, and he was heard because of his reverence." God was able to save Him from death. He did not — not in the way Gethsemane's first petition asked. He saved Him through death, out the other side, by resurrection. The hearing was real; the form of the answer was the Father's. This is the pattern that steadies believers whose own loud cries and tears seem unanswered: the Priest who intercedes for us knows, from the inside, what it is to pray in agony and receive the answer in God's form rather than our own. His reverence — His godly fear, His submission of the petition to the Father's will — is held up as the reason for His hearing. And now this same Priest, heard Himself, presents our prayers. No prayer offered through Him falls on inexperienced ears in heaven. The Man who wept in the garden is the Priest at the throne.

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Hebrews 5 comes as the preacher pauses: his hearers have become dull of hearing. By now they ought to be teachers, and they still need milk. Spiritual laziness is a genuine danger for genuine believers, and a diet of baby food will never train the discernment that distinguishes good from evil. But the good news stands taller than the rebuke: this appointed Priest — who did not exalt Himself, who prayed with loud cries and tears and was heard, who learned obedience through what He suffered — having been made perfect, became to all who obey Him the author of eternal salvation. Eternal. Not probationary, not provisional. The Priest is perfect, and the salvation He authors lasts as long as He does: forever.

A note from the Reformers. Luther's early lectures on Hebrews (1517–18) — among his first works as a reformer — lingered over this chapter's suffering, praying Christ: the Savior who was heard through tears is the Savior trembling believers may come to without fear. Calvin likewise stressed the appointment: a priesthood God Himself ordained in the Psalms cannot be set aside by man's religion.

Reflection question: The preacher grieves over believers who stayed on milk when they should have been teachers — where have you settled for spiritual baby food, and what is one solid-food step you can take?
