Hebrews 4 — A Rest Remains: The Sword and the Throne of Grace

Jesus in Hebrews — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Hebrews
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

The word that lays us bare belongs to the Priest who covers us — the throne we approach is a throne of grace, and the help arrives in time of need. Jesus is revealed here as the great High Priest who has passed through the heavens, the Son of God who sympathizes with our weaknesses because He was tempted in every respect as we are, yet without sin — and as the One in whom God's Sabbath rest is entered, not by our working, but by our ceasing from our works as God did from His.

Commentary

The promise still stands — fear the right fear (4:1–5). Chapter 3's warning flows straight into chapter 4's promise: "Therefore, while the promise of entering his rest still stands, let us fear lest any of you should seem to have failed to reach it" (4:1). This is the one fear the preacher commands — not fear of God the Father's welcome, but fear of missing the rest through unbelief, the way the wilderness generation did. "For good news came to us just as to them, but the message they heard did not benefit them, because they were not united by faith with those who listened" (4:2). Same message, two hearings. Faith is the difference between a sermon that saves and a sermon that hardens. "For we who have believed enter that rest" (4:3) — present tense; the entering has already begun for believers. And this rest is ancient: God's own rest dates from the foundation of the world, when "on the seventh day God finished his work that he had done" (Genesis 2:2). The rest God promises is His own rest — the rest of finished work. Joshua gave Israel a land, but centuries later David was still singing "Today" in Psalm 95, which proves a fuller rest remained. "So then, there remains a Sabbath rest for the people of God, for whoever has entered God's rest has also rested from his works as God did from his" (4:9–10). The rest is entered the way God entered His: by ceasing from our works. Not by ceasing from effort in the Christian life — the next verse commands striving — but by ceasing from the work of self-justification, the endless labor of trying to be our own saviors.

Strive to enter — and the sword (4:11–13). "Let us therefore strive to enter that rest, so that no one may fall by the same sort of disobedience" (4:11). The striving is the striving of faith — hearing, believing, holding fast, refusing to drift. And then comes the most searching paragraph in the letter, the reason no one can hide from this preacher's God:

"For the word of God is living and active, sharper than any two-edged sword, piercing to the division of soul and of spirit, of joints and of marrow, and discerning the thoughts and intentions of the heart." — Hebrews 4:12 (ESV)

The word is not ink that sits still. It is alive, and it cuts — deeper than any surgeon, down to the division of soul and spirit, down to thoughts and intentions, the things we hide even from ourselves. "And no creature is hidden from his sight, but all are naked and exposed to the eyes of him to whom we must give account" (4:13). There is no hiding place from this God. That is the bad news at full strength — and the preacher sets it directly before the greatest comfort in the letter, on purpose. The God from whom nothing is hidden is the God whose Priest covers us. The sword and the throne stand side by side in this chapter, and you cannot have the second honestly without passing the first.

The sympathizing Priest and the throne of grace (4:14–16). "Since then we have a great high priest who has passed through the heavens, Jesus, the Son of God, let us hold fast our confession" (4:14). Passed through the heavens — His ascension carried Him not into a better tabernacle on earth but into heaven itself, into the true Holy Place, carrying our confession with Him. "For we do not have a high priest who is unable to sympathize with our weaknesses, but one who in every respect has been tempted as we are, yet without sin" (4:15). The Greek word is sympatheō — to sympathize, literally to suffer-with. Our High Priest is not a distant official processing requests. He feels our weakness with us, having carried the full weight of temptation Himself — and unlike us, He carried it without ever giving in, which means He felt its full force to the end, the way only a man who never yields feels the whole weight of the load. And so the chapter ends with the invitation the whole sermon has been building toward:

"Let us then with confidence draw near to the throne of grace, that we may receive mercy and find grace to help in time of need." — Hebrews 4:16 (ESV)

Confidence — boldness — at a throne. And notice what kind of throne it is: not a throne of judgment for those who come through this Priest, but a throne of grace. Mercy for what is past; grace to help in time of need — help timed, precisely, to the need. Luther taught believers to pray boldly precisely because of this chapter: we come not on our own worthiness but on Christ's command and Christ's priesthood. Boldness in prayer is not arrogance; it is faith taking the Priest at His word.

"Let us fear" — the fear that guards the rest (4:1, 11). This chapter holds two commands that look like opposites and are not: "let us fear" (4:1) and "draw near with confidence" (4:16). The fear is aimed at unbelief — fear lest any of us should seem to have failed to reach the rest, the way a traveler fears missing the last train home. The confidence is aimed at the throne — boldness to come, through the Priest, for mercy and help. Fear of drifting, confidence in drawing near: the two belong together, and each protects the other. Fear without confidence becomes despair and stays away from the throne. Confidence without fear becomes presumption and stops striving to enter. The preacher holds both in one chapter because a healthy believer holds both in one heart. And note the striving of verse 11 once more: we strive to enter rest — the one labor of the Christian life that consists in ceasing from our own works and trusting Another's finished one. It is the hardest work the flesh ever does: stopping.

The rest is God's before it is ours (4:3–5, 9). One more depth in the promise: the rest we are invited into is not a rest God invented for us; it is the rest God Himself entered at creation. "His works were finished from the foundation of the world," and on the seventh day God rested — not from exhaustion, for the Creator does not tire, but in the satisfaction of completed work. To enter God's rest, then, is to be brought into God's own settled enjoyment of a finished work — and in the gospel, the finished work is Christ's. This is why self-justification is the exact opposite of rest: the man trying to justify himself is doing, at his own bench, work that can never be finished, because every day adds new sin to the ledger. The believer rests because the Priest's work holds the same quality as the Creator's: done, pronounced good, and sat down upon. The Sabbath rest that "remains" is not an appointment at the end of history only; it begins the day a sinner stops working for his acceptance and starts working from it.

Key verses

"So then, there remains a Sabbath rest for the people of God, for whoever has entered God's rest has also rested from his works as God did from his." — Hebrews 4:9–10 (ESV)

"For the word of God is living and active, sharper than any two-edged sword, piercing to the division of soul and of spirit, of joints and of marrow, and discerning the thoughts and intentions of the heart." — Hebrews 4:12 (ESV)

"For we do not have a high priest who is unable to sympathize with our weaknesses, but one who in every respect has been tempted as we are, yet without sin." — Hebrews 4:15 (ESV)

"Let us then with confidence draw near to the throne of grace, that we may receive mercy and find grace to help in time of need." — Hebrews 4:16 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Hebrews 4 is that the word of God is living and active, sharper than any two-edged sword, discerning the thoughts and intentions of the heart — and there is no creature hidden from His sight. There is no hiding place from this Priest, and the generation that heard and did not believe fell in the wilderness with the promise still standing. But the good news is that this is exactly why He is our hiding place. We have a great High Priest who has passed through the heavens, who is not unable to sympathize with our weaknesses, but was in all points tempted as we are, yet without sin. The throne is called grace. The help is timed to the need. A Sabbath rest remains for the people of God — entered by resting from our own works — and the way to it stands open: draw near.

A note from the Reformers. Luther taught believers to pray boldly precisely because of this chapter: we come not on our own worthiness but on Christ's command and Christ's priesthood. The timid conscience that stays away insults the throne it fears — and the throne, for those in Christ, is grace.

Reflection question: The throne you are invited to approach is a throne of grace, and the Priest on it sympathizes with your weakness — what need have you been hiding that you should bring boldly, today, in time of need?
