Broken Vessels — A History Lesson

Counted back — not one written off.

  • Broken Vessels
  • Ezra 1
  • Jeremiah 18
  • Jars of Clay
  • Assurance & Identity

"All the vessels of gold and of silver were 5,400. All these did Sheshbazzar bring up, when the exiles were brought up from Babylonia to Jerusalem."

Ezra 1:11 (ESV)

The Word Before the History

Before there is a single potter in the Bible, there is a Potter's verb. When Genesis says God made man, the Hebrew word is yatsar — "then the LORD God formed the man of dust from the ground" (Genesis 2:7). Yatsar is the potter's word: to fashion clay on a wheel. When Jeremiah walks into the potter's house centuries later, the potter himself is called a yotser — from the same root. You were not manufactured. You were formed, by hand, like clay — and everything the Bible ever says about vessels is, in the end, saying something about you. In the New Testament the Greek word is skeuos — a vessel, a container, a jar. Keep those two words in hand. Here is the history of what God has always done with vessels — including the broken ones.

1. Counted Out — The Vessels Go to Babylon

The temple in Jerusalem held vessels made to God's own pattern: dishes, flagons, and bowls of pure gold for the table of the bread of the Presence (Exodus 25:29), and under Solomon a whole inventory — the golden altar, the golden table, the lampstands of pure gold, five on the south side and five on the north, with their flowers, lamps, and tongs of gold; the basins, snuffers, bowls, dishes for incense, and cups, all of gold (1 Kings 7:48-50). Around them stood Huram's bronze work, and the Bible gives us its specs to the finger: the two pillars were eighteen cubits high (about 27 feet), twelve cubits around, hollow bronze four fingers thick, with capitals five cubits high (1 Kings 7:15-16; Jeremiah 52:21). The bronze Sea was ten cubits across, five cubits deep, thirty cubits around, a handbreadth thick, holding 2,000 baths — roughly 12,000 gallons — standing on twelve bronze oxen (1 Kings 7:23-26).

In 586 BC Nebuchadnezzar's army cut the pillars in pieces and carried the bronze and the vessels to Babylon (2 Kings 25:13-17). And Jeremiah records something remarkable: God did not call it an accident. Of the vessels that remained, God said, "They shall be carried to Babylon, and there they shall be until the day when I visit them, declares the LORD. Then I will bring them up and restore them to this place" (Jeremiah 27:22). The vessels were counted out — but they were already promised back. God keeps His inventory even in exile.

2. Weighed and Found Wanting — Belshazzar's Feast

A generation later, Belshazzar, king of Babylon, threw a feast for a thousand of his lords and called for the holy vessels — the gold and silver vessels Nebuchadnezzar had taken from the temple in Jerusalem — and he, his princes, his wives, and his concubines drank wine from them and "praised the gods of gold and silver, bronze, iron, wood, and stone" (Daniel 5:2-4). The same hour, fingers appeared and wrote on the wall: MENE, MENE, TEKEL, PARSIN. Daniel read it plain: "TEKEL: you have been weighed in the balances and found wanting" (Daniel 5:27). That night Belshazzar was slain (Daniel 5:30).

Here is the bad news in the history, and it must not be softened: what God set apart as holy, man treated as common — and God counted it. The vessels were not the problem. The hands that profaned them were. A vessel's worth is set by whose table it stands on.

Counted out, and counted back — by number. What God counts, He keeps.

3. Counted Back — Not One Written Off

Seventy years ran out, exactly as Jeremiah had promised, and Cyrus king of Persia ordered the vessels home. Ezra gives us the count like a man signing for treasure — because he was: "30 basins of gold, 1,000 basins of silver, 29 censers, 30 bowls of gold, 410 bowls of silver, and 1,000 other vessels; all the vessels of gold and of silver were 5,400. All these did Sheshbazzar bring up, when the exiles were brought up from Babylonia to Jerusalem" (Ezra 1:9-11).

Read that again slowly. The vessels were counted out, and they were counted back — by number. Captivity did not change their identity, and exile did not reduce their count. What God counts, He keeps. If you feel like a vessel that got carried off a long way from where you belonged, Ezra 1 is God's answer: He has not lost count of you.

4. The Potter's House — Marred in the Hand, Remade in the Hand

Then God sent Jeremiah down to watch a potter work. "And the vessel he was making of clay was spoiled in the potter's hand, and he reworked it into another vessel, as it seemed good to the potter to do" (Jeremiah 18:4). And God applied it Himself: "O house of Israel, can I not do with you as this potter has done? declares the LORD. Behold, like the clay in the potter's hand, so are you in my hand, O house of Israel" (Jeremiah 18:6). Isaiah prayed the same truth back to God: "But now, O LORD, you are our Father; we are the clay, and you are our potter; we are all the work of your hand" (Isaiah 64:8).

Notice where the vessel was spoiled: in the potter's hand — and notice what the potter did not do. He did not sweep the clay off the wheel. He did not throw it in the scrap pile. He reworked it, in his hand, into another vessel. The clay never left his hands between the marring and the making.

5. The Honest Warning — The Flask and the Cisterns

A history lesson has to tell the whole history. In Jeremiah 19, God sent the prophet to buy "a potter's earthenware flask," gather the elders at the Potsherd Gate, and break it in their sight: "So will I break this people and this city, as one breaks a potter's vessel, so that it can never be mended" (Jeremiah 19:11). And in Jeremiah 2:13 God names the root sin: "My people have committed two evils: they have forsaken me, the fountain of living waters, and hewed out cisterns for themselves, broken cisterns that can hold no water."

The difference between Jeremiah 18 and Jeremiah 19 is not God's mood. It is the clay's posture. The marred vessel of chapter 18 stayed on the wheel, in the hand, and was remade. The flask of chapter 19 is the picture of a people who refused the hand altogether. Brokenness brought to the Potter is the beginning of remaking. Brokenness that refuses Him leaks until it is empty.

6. The King Who Felt Like One — Psalm 31

Even David — king, psalmist, man after God's own heart — prayed it: "I have been forgotten like one who is dead; I have become like a broken vessel" (Psalm 31:12). Lamentations says the same of the whole nation: "The precious sons of Zion, worth their weight in fine gold, how they are regarded as earthen pots, the work of a potter's hands!" (Lamentations 4:2).

Hold the two ends of Psalm 31 together. The man who called himself a broken vessel ends the same psalm like this: "Be strong, and let your heart take courage, all you who wait for the LORD!" (Psalm 31:24). The broken vessel was still praying. Still hoping. Still in the hand.

7. Treasure in Jars of Clay — The New Testament Turn

Then Paul takes the whole history and lands it: "But we have this treasure in jars of clay, to show that the surpassing power belongs to God and not to us" (2 Corinthians 4:7). The Greek is ostrakina skeuē — unglazed, everyday, cheap clay pots. Not the temple's gold. The kind of pot you could buy anywhere and break any day. And God put the treasure in those — on purpose — so that when the power shows, nobody credits the pot. Paul describes the vessels' condition without any varnish: "We are afflicted in every way, but not crushed; perplexed, but not driven to despair; persecuted, but not forsaken; struck down, but not destroyed" (2 Corinthians 4:8-9). Cracked on every side — and still carrying the treasure.

Paul returns to it in his last letter: "Now in a great house there are not only vessels of gold and silver but also of wood and clay, some for honorable use, some for dishonorable. Therefore, if anyone cleanses himself from what is dishonorable, he will be a vessel for honorable use, set apart as holy, useful to the master of the house, ready for every good work" (2 Timothy 2:20-21). And in Romans he reminds every clay pot who holds the wheel: "Has the potter no right over the clay, to make out of the same lump one vessel for honorable use and another for dishonorable use?" (Romans 9:21).

8. The Chosen Vessel — A Persecutor, Remade

Finally, the history lands on a man. Saul of Tarsus was breaking vessels — ravaging the church — when the risen Jesus stopped him on the Damascus road and told Ananias: "he is a chosen instrument of mine to carry my name before the Gentiles and kings and the children of Israel" (Acts 9:15). The word rendered "instrument" there is skeuos — vessel. The King James says it plainly: "he is a chosen vessel unto me." The man who broke the church became the vessel who carried the gospel to the world — and the apostle who taught us about treasure in jars of clay knew exactly what he was talking about.

The Cross at the Center of It

The Reformers had a name for this pattern. In the Heidelberg Disputation of 1518, Martin Luther taught that God does His work under its opposite — strength hidden in weakness, life hidden in death, glory hidden in shame — and called it the theology of the cross. That is the whole history of the vessels in one sentence. God carried His holy things in gold, let them be carried into exile, counted them back one by one, and then, in the fullness of time, chose to carry the greatest treasure of all — the gospel itself — in clay. A crucified Savior. A persecutor apostle. A church of jars of clay. The container was never the point. The hands are the point.

Sit With This

  1. Observe: In Jeremiah 18, where exactly was the vessel when it was spoiled — and where was it when it was remade? What does that tell you about where you have to be for God to rework your life?
  2. Interpret: Why would God deliberately put His treasure in ostrakina — cheap, breakable clay (2 Corinthians 4:7)? What would be lost if He had used gold?
  3. Apply: The vessels came home counted — 5,400, not one written off (Ezra 1:11). Where have you been treating yourself like a vessel God has lost count of? Bring that place back to the Potter's hand today.

Your life is not a mistake; it is part of God's sovereign purpose and is held in His hands. The clay that stays in the Potter's hand has never once been thrown away.
