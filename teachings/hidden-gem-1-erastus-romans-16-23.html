Erastus: The Name Carved in Stone

Romans 16:23 — the sign-off is the sermon.

  • Hidden Gems
  • Romans 16
  • Erastus
  • All Scripture

Gaius, who is host to me and to the whole church, greets you. Erastus, the city treasurer, and our brother Quartus, greet you.

Romans 16:23 (ESV)

The chapter everybody skims

Be honest: when your reading plan lands on Romans 16, your eyes speed up. After fifteen chapters of the heaviest theology ever written — justification, sanctification, the sovereignty of God — the last chapter reads like a list of names you can't pronounce, attached to people you never heard of, in a church you've never visited. Most of us skim the greetings the way we skim the credits after a movie. We're glad somebody's name is up there. We just don't stop to read them.

But Paul didn't write filler. The man who gave us “for all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God” (Romans 3:23) ended his greatest letter with a roll call — and he ended it on purpose.

Buried in that roll call, three verses from the end, sits one quiet line: “Erastus, the city treasurer, greets you” (Romans 16:23). Erastus. No story, no speech, no miracle. Just a name, a job title, and a greeting. If any verse in Romans ever looked skippable, it's that one.

Then, in 1929, archaeologists digging in ancient Corinth — the city where Paul was living when he wrote Romans — uncovered a limestone pavement with a Latin inscription carved into it. The inscription says an Erastus laid that pavement at his own expense, in return for his appointment as aedile, a senior city official. Same name. Same city. Same era. Same level of city office. The name most of us skim right past was, all along, carved in stone.

Three men, three kinds of faithfulness

That inscription is why this collection exists — and why Erastus is Gem #1. (Honesty label, because this series will always give you one: scholars judge the pavement's Erastus to be very likely the same man Paul names. The evidence is strong — name, city, date, office — but Scripture doesn't say it outright, so we hold it as likely, not proven. The gem doesn't need the inscription to be true. The inscription just proves the verse was never decoration.)

Now read the whole verse slowly, because there's more in it than Erastus: “Gaius, who is host to me and to the whole church, greets you. Erastus, the city treasurer, and our brother Quartus, greet you” (Romans 16:23). Three men, three kinds of faithfulness. Gaius opened his house — Paul had baptized him (1 Corinthians 1:14), and the entire Corinthian church met under his roof; his treasure was hospitality. Erastus served in public office — a believer with a city job, faithful in the open, in a pagan city's government. And Quartus gets no title at all but “the brother” — standing one verse after Tertius, the scribe who actually wrote the letter down (v. 22). Tertius means “third.” Quartus means “fourth.” Two men remembered by numbers, one by his job, one by his open door — and the Holy Spirit breathed out every name on purpose.

Even Erastus's job title preaches. Paul calls him ho oikonomos tēs poleōs — oikonomos, say it oy-kon-OM-os — “the steward of the city.” An oikonomos was a manager of someone else's household and money: trusted, accountable, handling what wasn't his for the good of the house. That's what a city treasurer was — and it's the same picture Scripture gives every believer. We are stewards of a household that isn't ours, trusted with a treasure that isn't ours, in a city where we're seen. Erastus didn't have a pulpit. He had a ledger and a pavement — and God put his name in the Book.

No filler verses — only un-dug ones

Here's the meaning, and it's the rule of this whole collection: there are no filler verses, only un-dug ones. “All Scripture is breathed out by God and profitable” (2 Timothy 3:16) — all of it, including the greetings, the genealogies, the lists. The Reformers staked everything on that word “all”: sola Scriptura means the whole counsel of God, not just the underlined parts. The verses we skim are not God's leftovers. Some of them are His signatures — proof, pressed into the record like a name into wet cement, that these were real people, in real places, and that He remembers every one of them.

So here's the application for the believer nobody notices. God keeps the roster. He wrote down a host, a city official, a scribe, and “the brother” — and He has not lost a name since. Your faithfulness may never get a chapter; it may look, even to you, like a greeting-list life: showing up, opening your home, doing honest work, staying when it would be easier to drift. Heaven is not skimming your life. The same God who recorded Quartus — a man with no title but “brother” — has recorded you.

I know what it is to have your name on the wrong lists. For over thirty years my name lived on court dockets, jail rosters, and rehab sign-in sheets — lists nobody frames. For years after Jesus saved me, the only rooms that heard my story were AA and NA rooms. But here's what a verse like Romans 16:23 tells a man like me: God keeps a better roll call. He writes names in a book no court can subpoena and no past can edit (Revelation 20:15; Luke 10:20). Erastus the steward, Quartus the brother — and, by grace alone, Lewis the redeemed. If your name is in Christ, it's in the list that lasts.

The sign-off is the sermon

A host, a city official, a scribe, and “the brother” — the sign-off is the sermon. Don't skim the end of Romans anymore. Somebody you know is living in the verses everybody skips, faithfully, quietly, unnoticed by everyone but God. It might be you.

There are no filler verses, only un-dug ones.

Reflect — Questions & Answers

Q: Is the Erastus in the Corinth pavement inscription really Paul's Erastus?
A: Very likely, held honestly. The inscription (found 1929) names an Erastus who funded a pavement in Corinth in connection with holding the aedileship — same name, city, era, and civic rank as the “city treasurer” (steward) of Romans 16:23. Most scholars judge them the same man. Since Scripture doesn't state it, this series labels it a strong likelihood, not a proven fact.

Q: Why would God put a list of greetings in the Bible at all?
A: Because the church is people, not just ideas. Romans 16 shows the gospel's fruit: a household church, believers in public office, co-workers known and loved by name. It also anchors the letter in real history — real names in a real city — and it teaches that God records ordinary faithfulness (2 Timothy 3:16: ALL Scripture is God-breathed and profitable).

Q: What do I do with the “boring” parts of the Bible in my own reading?
A: Slow down instead of speeding up. Ask who these people were, why the Spirit kept their names, and what their one line shows about God. A greeting list became a sermon on God's memory. The genealogies and rosters will preach too — if you'll dig instead of skim. That's what this series is for, one gem a week.
