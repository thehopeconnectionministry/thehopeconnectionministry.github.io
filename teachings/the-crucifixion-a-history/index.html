The Crucifixion — A History

What Happened at the Cross — and What It Accomplished

  • Teachings
  • Church History
  • The Cross

The Hope Connection — Lewis Fuller

1. The Cross at the Center

Of all the ways a man could die in the ancient world, none was lower, none was more shameful, and none was more feared than the cross. And yet that is the death at the very center of the Christian faith. The church does not hide it or soften it. She sings about it. She puts it on her buildings. She carries it into prisons and hospital rooms and whispers it at gravesides. The apostle Paul, writing to a church in Corinth that was tempted to be embarrassed by a crucified Savior, said it plainly:

"For the word of the cross is folly to those who are perishing, but to us who are being saved it is the power of God." — 1 Corinthians 1:18 (ESV)

"For I decided to know nothing among you except Jesus Christ and him crucified." — 1 Corinthians 2:2 (ESV)

Paul did not decide to know a little about the cross among other things. He decided to know nothing else in comparison. That tells us something: if we are going to understand what God has done for us, we have to look at the cross long enough and honestly enough to feel its weight. We cannot rush past it to the empty tomb, much as we love the empty tomb. The resurrection is the victory shout, but the cross is where the battle was fought and won.

So this is a history of the crucifixion — where the practice came from, how it worked, what it did to a man, and above all what happened on the one cross that changed the world. It is not a pretty story. It was never meant to be pretty. But it is a true story, and for everyone who has ever felt too far gone, too dirty, too broken, or too late, it is the best news there is. First, though, we have to be honest about the bad news, because the bad news is what makes the good news good.

Crucifixion was not invented by the Romans, and it was not invented for Jesus. By the time Jesus of Nazareth was nailed to a cross outside Jerusalem, the cross had already been a tool of terror for hundreds of years. Understanding that long, dark history matters, because God did not choose this death by accident. He chose the worst death men had ever devised, and He chose it on purpose.

2. Before Rome: A Punishment Built for Shame

Crucifixion is older than the Roman Empire. The Persians are generally credited with developing it — the historian Herodotus records Persian kings crucifying and impaling their enemies centuries before Christ. Alexander the Great took up the practice; after the long siege of Tyre, he is reported to have crucified two thousand of the city's surviving men along the shore, a line of crosses facing the sea as a warning to every ship that passed. The city of Carthage, Rome's great rival in the west, also crucified — and Carthage did not spare even its own. Carthaginian generals who lost battles could find themselves on a cross raised by their own people.

Why did nation after nation reach for this particular punishment when quicker deaths were readily available? Because crucifixion was never only about killing. It was about disgrace. A beheaded man died in a moment. A crucified man died in public, slowly, naked, lifted up where everyone could see him and no one could help him, displayed like a sign. The cross said to everyone who passed by: this is what becomes of the lowest of the low. It was designed to erase a man's dignity before it took his life, and to frighten everyone watching into obedience. It was terror with a body on it.

For the people of Israel, there was an added horror, and it came from their own Scriptures. The law of Moses spoke of a criminal hanged on a tree after execution, and it said this about him:

"And if a man has committed a crime punishable by death and he is put to death, and you hang him on a tree, his body shall not remain all night on the tree, but you shall bury him the same day, for a hanged man is cursed by God." — Deuteronomy 21:22–23 (ESV)

Cursed by God. That is the phrase that hung over every cross. To a Jewish eye, a man on a tree was not only suffering — he was under the curse of God Himself. This is one reason the first preaching of a crucified Messiah sounded like madness and blasphemy to so many in Israel. How could God's chosen King die God's cursed death? The apostle Paul would later take that very verse and turn it into the heart of the gospel:

"Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us — for it is written, 'Cursed is everyone who is hanged on a tree.'" — Galatians 3:13 (ESV)

But that is getting ahead of the story. First, Rome took the cross and perfected it into an instrument of empire.

3. Rome's Instrument: How Crucifixion Worked

Rome adopted crucifixion from the peoples she conquered and made it her own special punishment — the death reserved for slaves, rebels, pirates, and the lowest criminals of the provinces. The Roman statesman Cicero called it "the cruelest and most hideous punishment" and said that the very word "cross" should be kept far from the thoughts, eyes, and ears of a Roman citizen. The Jewish historian Josephus, who saw crosses by the hundreds during the siege of Jerusalem, called it "the most wretched of deaths." Roman writers did not speak this way about any other execution. They knew what it was.

Crucifixion in Roman practice was a process, not a single act, and nearly every stage of it was designed to add suffering or shame.

The scourging. Before the cross came the whip. The condemned man was stripped and tied to a post or pillar, and soldiers beat him with the flagrum — a short whip of leather thongs, often weighted with pieces of bone or metal. Roman scourging was not a counted punishment; it was not limited to a set number of blows, and men died under it before they ever reached a cross. It tore the skin and flesh of the back, shoulders, and legs, and left a man weak from pain and loss of blood before the crucifixion had even begun. Many victims went into shock at the post. The scourging alone marked a man as something less than human in Roman eyes — it was called the halfway death.

The road to the place of execution. After the scourging, the condemned was made to carry his own cross — usually the heavy horizontal crossbeam, called the patibulum, laid across his shoulders and sometimes tied to his arms. The upright post, the stipes, often stood permanently at the place of execution outside the city walls. The procession moved through public streets, deliberately, so that as many people as possible would see. A soldier carried or wore a sign — the titulus — stating the man's name and his crime, so that no one watching could mistake why he died. If the victim collapsed under the beam, the soldiers could compel a bystander to carry it for him, as they did on the day Jesus died.

The nailing and the raising. At the place of execution the crossbeam was fixed to the upright, and the man was fastened to it. Ropes were used in some crucifixions; nails were used in others, and nails were the more brutal and the more common in Roman practice in Judea — archaeologists have found the heel bone of a crucified man from the time of Jesus with the iron nail still driven through it. The nails for the hands were driven not through the palms, which would tear under a body's weight, but through the wrists or forearms, in the strong place where the bones and sinews could bear the load — the Gospels' word "hands" covers this part of the arm. The feet were then nailed or bound to the upright, sometimes with a small wooden block or peg — a sedile — that prolonged the agony by letting the victim support a fraction of his weight. Then the cross was lifted and dropped into its hole, and the jolt of it tore at every wound at once.

The titulus. Above the victim's head the sign was nailed: his name and his crime, written for the crowds to read. It served the empire's purpose — everyone who passed would know the charge and learn the lesson.

The slow mechanics of death. Crucifixion killed slowly — over hours, sometimes over days. Death came by a combination of causes: loss of blood from the scourging and the nails, shock, exposure to sun and cold, dehydration, and, as exhaustion deepened, suffocation. Hanging by the arms, a crucified man found it steadily harder to breathe; to draw a full breath he had to push up on his nailed feet, grinding the wounds, until pain and exhaustion would no longer let him, and the weight of his own body slowly closed his chest. That is why breaking the legs ended it so quickly — a man who could no longer push up could no longer breathe. The Romans called that act the crurifragium, and they used it when a death needed to be hastened, as it was on the day of the crucifixion of Jesus, when the coming Sabbath pressed the authorities to finish. A spear thrust — the coup de grâce — could also confirm or complete the death, and Roman soldiers, whose own lives depended on never letting a condemned man come down alive, were thorough about making sure.

The public warning. Crosses were raised along roadsides, outside city gates, on hills — wherever traffic was heaviest. After the slave revolt led by Spartacus was crushed in 71 B.C., the Romans crucified six thousand captured slaves along the Appian Way, the great road into Rome, so that the crosses lined the highway for mile after mile. The victims were ordinarily left on their crosses after death for the birds and the beasts, and burial was commonly denied — one final dishonor, and one more reason the cross terrified the ancient world. The victims were slaves, rebels, deserters, and foreign criminals. Roman citizens were exempt by law and by custom; a citizen was beheaded instead, which Rome considered the honorable death. When a governor in Sicily once crucified a man who claimed Roman citizenship, the scandal of it followed that governor for the rest of his life.

This was the machine of death that stood ready in Jerusalem on a Friday morning in the spring, when the crowd cried out for Jesus of Nazareth to be crucified.

4. The Crucifixion of Jesus

Everything Rome built the cross to do, it did to Jesus — and everything Jesus did on it, no other crucified man had ever done. The Gospels give us the account in order, and it deserves to be walked through slowly.

Gethsemane. On the night before He died, after the Last Supper with His disciples, Jesus went to a garden called Gethsemane, at the foot of the Mount of Olives, and prayed in agony. He knew exactly what was coming — He had told His disciples plainly, more than once, that He would be delivered over, mocked, flogged, and crucified. He did not stumble into the cross; He walked toward it with His eyes open, and in the garden He sweat and prayed through the horror of it: "My Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass from me; nevertheless, not as I will, but as you will" (Matthew 26:39). There Judas Iscariot arrived with a band of soldiers and officers, and betrayed Him with a kiss. Jesus was arrested, bound, and led away into the night. His disciples fled.

The trials. Through the night and into the early morning, Jesus was passed from court to court like a man already condemned. He was taken first to Annas, then to Caiaphas the high priest, where the council of the Sanhedrin heard false witnesses contradict one another, until the high priest put Him under oath and asked Him directly whether He was the Christ, the Son of God. Jesus answered, "You have said so. But I tell you, from now on you will see the Son of Man seated at the right hand of Power and coming on the clouds of heaven" (Matthew 26:64). They called it blasphemy, spat on Him, and struck Him. In the morning they delivered Him to Pontius Pilate, the Roman governor, because the council had no authority to carry out a death sentence. Pilate questioned Him and found no guilt in Him — he said so repeatedly, and so did Herod Antipas, to whom Pilate sent Him and who mocked Him and sent Him back. But the chief priests stirred the crowd, and when Pilate offered the customary Passover release of a prisoner, the crowd chose Barabbas — a man in prison for murder and insurrection — and demanded that Jesus be crucified. Pilate washed his hands before the crowd, handed Jesus over, and had Him scourged.

The scourging and the mockery. Roman soldiers flogged Jesus with the flagrum, the halfway death described above — a flogging so severe that Isaiah's prophecy, written seven centuries earlier, described the sufferer as so marred that He barely looked like a man. Then the soldiers took Him into the governor's headquarters and gathered the whole battalion around Him. They stripped Him and put a scarlet robe on Him. They twisted together a crown of thorns and pressed it onto His head, put a reed in His right hand as a pretend scepter, and knelt before Him in mock worship, crying, "Hail, King of the Jews!" They spat on Him, struck Him on the head again and again with the reed, and tore the robe off Him — reopening the wounds of the scourging — before leading Him out to be crucified.

The road and the hill. Jesus went out bearing His own cross, but weakened by the scourging He could not carry it the whole way, and the soldiers compelled a man named Simon, from Cyrene in North Africa, who was passing by from the country, to carry it behind Him. A great multitude followed, including women mourning and lamenting, to whom Jesus turned and spoke of the judgment coming on the city. The place was called Golgotha — in Aramaic, the Place of a Skull — a hill outside the walls of Jerusalem, by the road, where everyone passing in and out of the city for the Passover would see. There they offered Him wine mixed with myrrh, a dull drink for the pain, and He refused it. He would drink the cup His Father gave Him with a clear mind.

The third hour. Mark's Gospel fixes the time: "And it was the third hour when they crucified him" (Mark 15:25) — nine o'clock in the morning. They nailed Him to the cross between two robbers, one on His right and one on His left, fulfilling Isaiah's word that He would be numbered with the transgressors. The soldiers divided His garments among them and cast lots for His seamless tunic. Above His head Pilate placed the titulus, and it read, in Hebrew, in Latin, and in Greek, so that every pilgrim in the city could read it: "Jesus of Nazareth, the King of the Jews." The chief priests objected — write, they said, that He claimed to be king. Pilate answered, "What I have written I have written." Those who passed by wagged their heads and mocked Him. The chief priests and scribes mocked Him. Even the robbers crucified with Him reviled Him at first. And from that cross, in the middle of it all, Jesus spoke seven times — and every word is worth holding onto.

The seven sayings from the cross.

First: "Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do" (Luke 23:34). His first words from the cross were not a curse on His executioners but a prayer for them. The hands driving the nails were, at that very moment, being prayed for by the man on the cross.

Second: "Truly, I say to you, today you will be with me in paradise" (Luke 23:43). One of the robbers beside Him turned — stopped mocking, confessed his own guilt, defended Jesus, and prayed the simplest prayer of faith ever prayed: "Jesus, remember me when you come into your kingdom" (Luke 23:42). He brought nothing, promised nothing, and had no time to clean up his life or be baptized or do a single good work. He simply turned to Jesus and asked to be remembered. And the dying King beside him gave him paradise that very day. If there is hope for that man, there is hope for anyone who will turn.

Third: "Woman, behold, your son!" and to the disciple John, "Behold, your mother!" (John 19:26–27). In the middle of dying for the world, Jesus did not forget His own mother standing at the foot of the cross. He placed her into John's care, and from that hour John took her into his own home. The cross did not cancel His love for the people closest to Him; it carried it.

Fourth: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Matthew 27:46; Mark 15:34). At about the ninth hour Jesus cried out with a loud voice in Aramaic — "Eli, Eli, lema sabachthani?" — the opening words of Psalm 22. This is the deepest mystery of the cross: the Son, who had never known a moment outside the Father's love, bore our sin and the judgment our sin deserved, and cried out under the forsakenness that belonged to us. We will never fully sound the depth of that cry. But every forsaken-feeling sinner should know: He cried it so that we would never have to cry it finally.

Fifth: "I thirst" (John 19:28). Two words in English, one word in Greek. The man who offered living water to a Samaritan woman at a well now thirsted. John tells us He said it to fulfill the Scripture, and it also tells us He was truly man, truly suffering, truly bearing the whole weight in a real body — no illusion, no shortcut.

Sixth: "It is finished" (John 19:30). In Greek, one word: tetelestai — a word written across receipts in the ancient world: paid in full. This was not the cry of a victim giving up. It was the declaration of a work completed, a debt settled, a mission accomplished. Nothing was left owing.

Seventh: "Father, into your hands I commit my spirit!" (Luke 23:46). And having said this, He breathed His last. No one took His life from Him. The soldiers did not finish Him; the cross did not finally conquer Him. He had said it beforehand: "I lay down my life that I may take it up again. No one takes it from me, but I lay it down of my own accord" (John 10:17–18). He dismissed His own spirit, in trust, into His Father's hands.

Darkness, the veil, and the confession. From the sixth hour — noon — until the ninth hour, darkness covered the whole land, though it was the middle of the day. At the moment of His death, Matthew records what happened in the temple: "And behold, the curtain of the temple was torn in two, from top to bottom. And the earth shook, and the rocks were split" (Matthew 27:51). The great veil that had shut sinners out of the Holy of Holies for fifteen centuries was torn — from top to bottom, God's direction, not man's — because the way into God's presence now stood open through the death of His Son. And the Roman centurion who stood facing Jesus, who had overseen the execution and watched how He died, made the first confession of faith at the foot of the cross: "Truly this man was the Son of God!" (Mark 15:39). A hardened Roman soldier, of all people, was the first to say it.

The spear, and the burial. Because the next day was a Sabbath, the Jewish leaders asked Pilate to have the legs of the crucified men broken so the bodies could be taken down. The soldiers broke the legs of the two robbers — but coming to Jesus, they saw that He was already dead, and they did not break His legs. Instead, "one of the soldiers pierced his side with a spear, and at once there came out blood and water" (John 19:34) — the testimony of an eyewitness, John himself, that Jesus was truly, physically dead, not swooning, not faking. Then two men stepped out of the shadows where fear had kept them: Joseph of Arimathea, a rich and respected member of the council who had not consented to what was done, and Nicodemus, who had once come to Jesus by night. Joseph asked Pilate for the body, took it down, wrapped it in a clean linen shroud with the spices Nicodemus brought, and laid it in his own new tomb cut in the rock, in a garden near the place of the crucifixion. A great stone was rolled against the entrance. It was Friday, the day of Preparation, and the Sabbath was beginning. Everything looked finished. It was not.

5. Written Long Before: The Prophecies Fulfilled at the Cross

No crucifixion in history was like this one for another reason: it had been described in writing, in detail, centuries before it happened — and centuries before crucifixion was even practiced in Israel. The cross was not an accident that God scrambled to redeem. It was a plan He announced in advance.

Psalm 22. About a thousand years before Christ, David wrote a psalm that reads like an eyewitness account of the crucifixion. It opens with the very cry Jesus would take on His own lips: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Psalm 22:1). It describes the mockery of the crowd — "All who see me mock me; they make mouths at me; they wag their heads" (Psalm 22:7). It describes the physical agonies of a crucified man in language written long before Israel had ever seen a cross: "I am poured out like water, and all my bones are out of joint; my heart is like wax; it is melted within my breast" (Psalm 22:14). And then, with a precision that takes the breath away: "For dogs encompass me; a company of evildoers encircles me; they have pierced my hands and feet" (Psalm 22:16). And finally: "They divide my garments among them, and for my clothing they cast lots" (Psalm 22:18) — which is exactly what the soldiers did at the foot of the cross, in detail, without knowing they were fulfilling anything.

Isaiah 53. About seven hundred years before Christ, the prophet Isaiah wrote the fifty-third chapter of his book — the Suffering Servant — and it is the cross described in advance from God's side. "He was despised and rejected by men, a man of sorrows and acquainted with grief" (Isaiah 53:3). Then the explanation of what the cross was actually doing:

"But he was pierced for our transgressions; he was crushed for our iniquities; upon him was the chastisement that brought us peace, and with his wounds we are healed. All we like sheep have gone astray; we have turned — every one — to his own way; and the LORD has laid on him the iniquity of us all." — Isaiah 53:5–6 (ESV)

Pierced — the very word. Isaiah also said He would be silent before His accusers like a lamb led to the slaughter (53:7), that He would be cut off from the land of the living (53:8), that His grave would be with the wicked and with a rich man in His death (53:9) — a crucified criminal buried in a rich man's tomb — and that He would pour out His soul to death and be numbered with the transgressors while bearing the sin of many (53:12).

Zechariah 12:10. The prophet Zechariah heard God say of the day of His people's mourning: "they shall look on me, on him whom they have pierced" — God Himself speaking of being pierced. The apostle John, standing at the cross, watched the soldier's spear and wrote: "These things took place that the Scripture might be fulfilled… 'They will look on him whom they have pierced'" (John 19:36–37).

Not a bone broken. The Passover lamb, by God's command in Exodus, was to be eaten with this instruction: "you shall not break any of its bones" (Exodus 12:46). And David wrote of the righteous sufferer: "He keeps all his bones; not one of them is broken" (Psalm 34:20). So when the soldiers came to finish the crucified men by breaking their legs — the routine, expected act — they broke the legs of two men and passed by the third, because He was already dead. The Lamb of God's bones stayed whole, exactly as the Scripture had said fifteen centuries and five centuries before. These are not vague predictions stretched to fit. They are the fingerprints of a plan.

6. What the Cross Accomplished

Now we come to the heart of it, and we must come honestly, the way the Bible does: bad news first, then good news.

The bad news. The cross only makes sense when we understand what it was answering. Our sin is not a small thing, and God is not a small God. We have all gone astray — every one of us, to his own way. We have broken God's law in deed, in word, and in heart, and we cannot unbreak it. And God is just. He does not sweep sin under the rug of heaven; a judge who lets every crime go unpunished is not a good judge, and God is good all the way down. His wrath against sin is not a temper tantrum — it is the settled, holy opposition of a righteous God to everything that destroys what He loves. Left to ourselves, we stand guilty before Him, and the sentence is real. Any telling of the cross that skips this is not telling the cross at all, because the cross is where God dealt with exactly this.

The good news. And here is what He did: instead of leaving us under that sentence, God the Father sent His own Son — not a third party, not an unwilling victim, but God Himself in the flesh, who came gladly — and laid our iniquity on Him. At the cross, Jesus stood in the place of sinners. Theologians have given names to the different facets of what He accomplished there, and each one is a diamond worth turning in the light:

Substitution — He took our place and bore what we deserved. "For our sake he made him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God" (2 Corinthians 5:21). The sinless one was treated as sin, so that sinners could be treated as righteous. That is the great exchange, and there has never been news like it.

Propitiation — He turned away the wrath of God by bearing it Himself. Paul says God put Jesus forward "as a propitiation by his blood, to be received by faith" (Romans 3:25). God's justice was not ignored at the cross; it was satisfied — fully, publicly, and finally — by the sacrifice of His own Son.

Redemption — He paid the price to buy us out of slavery to sin. Jesus described His own mission this way: "For even the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45). A ransom is a price paid to set captives free. His life was the price. Our freedom is the result.

Reconciliation — He made peace between God and sinners. "Making peace by the blood of his cross" (Colossians 1:20), God reconciled to Himself those who were once His enemies, so that the war is over for everyone who comes to Him through Christ. Not a truce — peace.

This is why the word of the cross is the power of God to those who are being saved. It is not advice. It is not an example to copy, though it is that too. It is an accomplished rescue, finished — tetelestai — paid in full, before we ever asked, while we were still sinners.

7. The End of Crucifixion, and the Victory of the Cross

Crucifixion did not fade away quickly. Rome went on crucifying rebels, slaves, and criminals for another three centuries after Jesus. But something had changed in the world that could not be unchanged. The followers of the crucified man — who should have scattered in shame and never been heard from again — instead went everywhere announcing that He had risen from the dead, and that His cross was not a defeat but a throne. They were beaten for it, imprisoned for it, and killed for it, and they would not stop. Within a few generations, the faith of the cross had spread through the whole empire that invented the punishment.

In the early fourth century, the emperor Constantine — the first Roman emperor to profess faith in Christ — abolished crucifixion throughout the empire, out of reverence for the Lord who had died on one. The cruelest punishment Rome ever devised was ended by the victory of the very death it inflicted. And the cross itself — the symbol designed to say cursed, shamed, forgotten — became the most beloved emblem in the world: carved over doorways, worn around necks, raised over hospitals and graves, carried into places of suffering as the sign that God has been there and has not left His people alone in it. The instrument of shame became the banner of hope. That is not something men would have invented. That is something God did.

8. The Cross and You

Here is where the history has to stop being only history, because the cross was never meant to be admired from a distance. Every person who reads these words stands somewhere in this story. Some of us know exactly what it is to be at the bottom — to carry years we are ashamed of, to have hurt people we love, to have tried to fix ourselves and failed so many times that we stopped believing we could be fixed. The cross speaks directly to that person. It says: your sin is worse than you fear — bad enough that this is what it took to deal with it — and you are more loved than you dare hope — loved enough that God did not wait for you to climb up to Him, but came down and bore it Himself.

Remember the robber on the cross beside Jesus. He had nothing to offer and no time left to offer it. He could not be baptized, could not make restitution, could not attend a service or turn over a new leaf. He simply turned his head toward Jesus and asked to be remembered — and Jesus gave him paradise that day, on the spot, from one cross to another. That is the kind of Savior who died there. He has not changed. Whoever you are, whatever the years behind you look like, you are not too late and you are not too far gone. The word of the cross is folly to those who are perishing, but to us who are being saved, it is the power of God. Come to Him as you are. The price has already been paid — in full.

A closing prayer

Lord Jesus, You did not have to come, and You did not have to stay on that cross — but You did, because of the joy set before You and because of the people You came to save. Teach us never to be casual about what our sin cost You, and never to doubt what Your cross accomplished for us. For everyone reading who feels too far gone, let the thief on the cross be their hope today: it is not too late to turn. Hold us fast, as You have always held us. In Your name we pray, amen.

Christ Proclaimed. Jesus is Lord.
