Prayer — A History

Calling on the Name of the Lord, from Eden to Today

  • Teachings
  • Church History
  • Prayer

The Hope Connection — Lewis Fuller

1. As Old as Calling on His Name

Prayer is not a religious technique somebody invented. It is as old as man's need of God, and the Bible tells us exactly when it began. In the earliest chapters of Genesis, after sin had entered the world and death with it, after Cain had murdered Abel and a second son was born to Adam and Eve, we read a quiet sentence that changed history:

"To Seth also a son was born, and he called his name Enosh. At that time people began to call upon the name of the LORD." — Genesis 4:26 (ESV)

That is where prayer starts in the record of Scripture: not in a temple, not with a priesthood, not with a formula — with families who knew they needed God and called on His name. Before there were altars of stone there were voices in the dark. Before there was a tabernacle there was a cry.

Everything since has been the unfolding of that one act. Prayer is the creature turning back toward his Creator. It is the sinner speaking to the God he has offended and finding, to his astonishment, that God listens. It is intercession and confession, praise and lament, asking and thanking and sometimes just groaning. The whole history of the people of God can be told as a history of prayer — of who prayed, where they prayed, what they asked, and what God did in answer.

And prayer is, at its root, God's idea before it is ours. We do not have to persuade a reluctant heaven to open. The God of the Bible commands the very thing He delights to hear. "Call to me and I will answer you, and will tell you great and hidden things that you have not known" (Jeremiah 33:3). The New Testament's shortest command may be its most sweeping: "Pray without ceasing" (1 Thessalonians 5:17) — not a life spent on one's knees with eyes closed, but a life lived in an unbroken, running conversation with the Father, in the field and the kitchen and the sickroom as much as in the sanctuary. Prayer takes many shapes across the centuries — spoken and silent, written and groaned, sung from a hymnal and whispered in a cell — but the heart of it never changes: God's people talking to God, because God first spoke to them, and because in Christ the way into His presence stands open. Keep that in mind through everything that follows. The forms in this history will change a hundred times. The Father who hears does not change once.

This is that history: prayer in the Old Testament, prayer in the life of Jesus, prayer in the first church, and prayer down through twenty centuries of saints — desert monks and English archbishops, Puritan closets and college haystacks, a hundred-year prayer meeting in Germany and a noon prayer meeting in New York that shook a nation. It ends where it must end: with you, on your knees, in your own hard place, discovering that the God who heard Enosh's generation hears yours.

2. Prayer in the Old Testament: Giants on Their Knees

The Old Testament is a prayer book before it is anything else, because the Old Testament is the story of a God who speaks — and a God who speaks invites an answer.

Abraham is the first great intercessor. When God revealed that He was about to judge Sodom, Abraham — whose nephew Lot lived in that city — stood before the Lord and pleaded, bargaining the number down from fifty righteous to ten, and grounding his plea not in Sodom's merit but in God's character: "Shall not the Judge of all the earth do what is just?" (Genesis 18:25). That is intercession in its purest form: a man who knows God standing between God and people who deserve judgment, asking for mercy they have not asked for themselves.

Moses prayed on mountains and in the middle of disasters. When Israel made the golden calf at the foot of Sinai, Moses fell on his face and pleaded for the people — even offering to be blotted out of God's book in their place (Exodus 32:32). When Miriam was struck with leprosy, his prayer was five words long in Hebrew: "O God, please heal her — please!" (Numbers 12:13). Moses shows us that prayer is not measured by its length. It is measured by its nearness to God. He spoke with God "face to face, as a man speaks to his friend" (Exodus 33:11), and his prayers carried a nation.

Hannah prayed in the house of the Lord at Shiloh with no sound at all — her lips moving, her heart pouring out a grief she could not say aloud, so that the priest Eli thought her drunk. She asked God for a son and vowed him back to God, and God answered with Samuel, the prophet who would anoint kings. And Hannah's prayer of thanksgiving (1 Samuel 2) became a pattern that echoes all the way down to Mary's song in the New Testament. God hears the prayer nobody else can hear. That is a sentence worth underlining.

David gave the church her prayer book. The Psalms are not mostly songs about prayer; they are prayers — prayed by a king who had been a shepherd, a fugitive, an adulterer, and a mourner, and who hid nothing from God. In the Psalms there is praise at full volume and lament from the bottom of the pit:

"Out of the depths I cry to you, O LORD! O Lord, hear my voice! Let your ears be attentive to the voice of my pleas for mercy!" — Psalm 130:1–2 (ESV)

The church has prayed David's prayers ever since because David prayed the whole human condition: joy and guilt, gratitude and terror, "How long, O LORD?" and "The LORD is my shepherd." When you do not know how to pray, the Psalms are God's own vocabulary lessons, given in advance for people who would run out of words.

Solomon, dedicating the temple, prayed the great prayer of 1 Kings 8 — a prayer that looks outward in every direction: toward the sinner who repents, the stranger who comes from a far country, the army in battle, the people in exile. And his petition rests on a wonder: "But will God indeed dwell on the earth? Behold, heaven and the highest heaven cannot contain you; how much less this house that I have built!" (1 Kings 8:27). The God of Solomon's prayer cannot be contained, yet He bends His ear toward a house of stone because His people are calling there.

Daniel prayed in a foreign capital under a law that made his prayers a capital crime. When the decree was signed forbidding prayer to anyone but the king, Daniel went home and did what he had always done:

"When Daniel knew that the document had been signed, he went to his house where he had windows in his upper chamber open toward Jerusalem. He got down on his knees three times a day and prayed and gave thanks before his God, as he had done previously." — Daniel 6:10 (ESV)

Three times a day, on his knees, windows open — "as he had done previously." The lion's den did not make Daniel a man of prayer. A lifetime of prayer made Daniel ready for the lion's den. And notice: he gave thanks. In the shadow of a death sentence, the old man's prayer still had gratitude in it, because his prayers were not built on his circumstances. They were built on his God.

The Old Testament closes with the prayers of the exile and the return, and they teach the church two more shapes of prayer. Hezekiah, holding a letter full of threats from the Assyrian king, went up to the house of the Lord and spread the letter out before God — literally laid the problem on the floor of God's house — and prayed (2 Kings 19:14–19). God answered by destroying the army that wrote the letter. There are prayers in this life that need no eloquence at all; they just need the letter spread out before the Lord. And Nehemiah shows us both ends of prayer at once: the long, weeping, confessing prayer of Nehemiah 1, prayed over days for a ruined city — and the prayer of a single breath, shot up silently in the king's presence when the moment came: "Then I prayed to the God of heaven" (Nehemiah 2:4), right in the middle of a conversation, with no time for knees or closets. The church has called those "arrow prayers" ever since. The God who hears the prayer of a whole night hears the prayer of a single second, when it comes from a heart that knows Him.

3. Jesus and Prayer: The Praying Son

If you want to know whether prayer matters, watch Jesus. The eternal Son of God, who had enjoyed unbroken fellowship with His Father forever, prayed constantly during His earthly life — prayed early, prayed late, prayed in public and prayed alone, prayed before He chose the Twelve and after He fed the multitudes, prayed in thanksgiving and prayed in agony. If the Son of God needed to pray, no believer who has ever lived can afford to skip it.

His habits were deliberate. At the very beginning of His ministry, with crowds already pressing in on Him and a whole town looking for Him, Mark tells us where He went first:

"And rising very early in the morning, while it was still dark, he departed and went out to a desolate place, and there he prayed." — Mark 1:35 (ESV)

And before He chose the twelve apostles — one of the most consequential decisions of His ministry — Luke tells us He spent not an hour but a night at it:

"In these days he went out to the mountain to pray, and all night he continued in prayer to God." — Luke 6:12 (ESV)

Jesus also taught His disciples how to pray, and His teaching cuts in two directions at once. Against showmanship: go into your room, shut the door, and pray to your Father who is in secret (Matthew 6:6) — prayer is not a performance for an audience of men. Against empty repetition: do not heap up phrases as though God were hard of hearing (Matthew 6:7). And then He gave them, and us, the pattern prayer that the church has prayed ever since, in every language on earth:

"Pray then like this: 'Our Father in heaven, hallowed be your name. Your kingdom come, your will be done, on earth as it is in heaven. Give us this day our daily bread, and forgive us our debts, as we also have forgiven our debtors. And lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from evil.'" — Matthew 6:9–13 (ESV)

Look at its shape. It begins with the Father — His name, His kingdom, His will — before it ever gets to our bread. It asks for today's provision, not next year's security. It links our forgiveness received to our forgiveness given. Six short petitions, and there is not a human need outside their reach.

Jesus also taught prayer by parable, and His stories hold the two truths every pray-er must keep together. In the parable of the persistent widow, a helpless woman wears down an unjust judge by simply refusing to stop coming — and Jesus draws the lesson Himself: men "ought always to pray and not lose heart" (Luke 18:1). If shameless persistence moves a corrupt judge, how much more will a loving Father answer His own children? But in the very same chapter, Jesus tells of two men who went up to the temple to pray: a Pharisee, who prayed a fine-sounding prayer about himself, and a tax collector, who stood far off, would not lift his eyes, beat his breast, and said only, "God, be merciful to me, a sinner!" (Luke 18:13). The tax collector went home justified, and the Pharisee did not. Persistence, yes — but persistence in humility. God is not moved by the length of our prayers or the polish of them. He is moved by the lowliness of the heart that knows it brings nothing but need.

And on His last night, before Gethsemane, Jesus prayed the longest prayer of His recorded in Scripture — the prayer of John 17, sometimes called His high priestly prayer, where He prays first for Himself, then for His apostles, and then, astonishingly, for us: "I do not ask for these only, but also for those who will believe in me through their word" (John 17:20). If you have believed in Jesus through the apostles' word, you were prayed for by name — not your name, but your reality — on the night before the cross. The history of prayer includes a prayer that was prayed for you before you were born.

And then there is Gethsemane, the greatest prayer ever prayed. On the night He was betrayed, facing a cross He could see with terrible clarity, Jesus fell on His face and prayed the most honest prayer in Scripture:

"And going a little farther he fell on his face and prayed, saying, 'My Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass from me; nevertheless, not as I will, but as you will.'" — Matthew 26:39 (ESV)

He prayed it three times. He did not pretend He wanted the cup. He asked, plainly and repeatedly, for another way — and then He surrendered to the way there was. That is the pattern for every believer who has ever prayed in a hospital corridor or a jail cell or a dark kitchen at three in the morning: ask honestly, ask more than once, and let the last word be the Father's. Jesus' prayer was not answered by the cup being removed. It was answered by the Son being strengthened to drink it — and by the resurrection on the other side of it. Some of God's answers come as deliverance from the trial. The greatest ones come as deliverance through it.

Nor did Jesus stop praying when He ascended. The New Testament says He "always lives to make intercession" for His people (Hebrews 7:25) — that even now, at the Father's right hand, the Son prays for the same people He died for. The history of prayer does not run on the strength of the pray-ers. It runs on a praying Savior who never clocks out.

4. Prayer in the First Church: The Engine Room of Acts

The book of Acts could almost be called the book of answered prayer. The church was born in a prayer meeting: after the ascension, the disciples returned to Jerusalem and waited, exactly as Jesus had told them, and Luke tells us what the waiting looked like:

"All these with one accord were devoting themselves to prayer, together with the women and Mary the mother of Jesus, and his brothers." — Acts 1:14 (ESV)

Ten days of devoted prayer, and then Pentecost. The pattern never really changes in Acts. The apostles, overwhelmed by the needs of a growing church, refused to let administration crowd out intercession: "But we will devote ourselves to prayer and to the ministry of the word" (Acts 6:4) — prayer named first, as the senior partner. When persecution broke out, the church did not organize a protest; it held a prayer meeting, and the place where they were gathered was shaken (Acts 4:31). When James was executed and Peter arrested and chained between soldiers the night before his own scheduled death, Luke tells us what the church was doing while the sword hung over its leader:

"So Peter was kept in prison, but earnest prayer for him was made to God by the church." — Acts 12:5 (ESV)

That little word "but" is doing heavy lifting. Peter was in prison, but earnest prayer was made. The church's weapon against an empire's chains was a room full of ordinary believers praying in the night — and God answered so directly that Peter, standing at the door of the prayer meeting itself, had to keep knocking while the people inside struggled to believe their own prayers had been answered (Acts 12:13–16). There is comfort in that for every weak pray-er: God answered the prayers of people whose faith was still catching up with His power.

Prayer in Acts is also what carried the gospel across borders. Cornelius, a Roman centurion, was a man of prayer whose prayers came up as a memorial before God (Acts 10:4) — and his prayer meeting opened the door of the church to the Gentile world. Paul and Silas prayed and sang hymns in a Philippian jail at midnight, with their feet in the stocks, and God shook the prison open (Acts 16:25–26). The first church had no money, no buildings, no political protection, and no printing presses. What it had was prayer — and with prayer, it turned the world upside down.

Notice, too, that the first church prayed on a rhythm, not only in emergencies. The apostles went up to the temple at "the hour of prayer, the ninth hour" (Acts 3:1) — three in the afternoon, the set time — and it was on the way to that ordinary prayer time that a lame man was healed and a sermon was preached. Peter was on a rooftop at the sixth hour, praying at noon, when the vision came that opened the Gentile mission (Acts 10:9). Scheduled prayer and spontaneous prayer were not rivals in Acts; they were husband and wife in the same house. The set times kept the fire banked; the crises fanned it into flame.

5. Prayer Through the Centuries: Closets, Prayer Books, and a Hundred-Year Meeting

After the apostles, the praying never stopped — but it changed its shapes, and some of the shapes are worth remembering.

In the third and fourth centuries, men and women who came to be called the desert fathers and mothers withdrew into the wilderness of Egypt and Syria, fleeing a church grown comfortable after persecution ended, to give themselves to prayer in solitude. Anthony of Egypt spent some twenty years in the desert in prayer and spiritual warfare. Their sayings, collected by their disciples, are blunt and practical — they knew prayer as combat, not decoration. Not everything about the movement was healthy; withdrawal could become an escape from the neighbor God commands us to love. But the desert taught the church something she keeps having to relearn: a man alone with God is never wasting his time.

But before the desert, there were the martyrs, and the church learned to pray by listening to them die. Polycarp, the aged bishop of Smyrna, a disciple of the apostle John himself, was arrested in his eighties and brought into the stadium to be burned. The account his church preserved tells us that before the fire was lit, he asked to pray — and prayed aloud, at length, thanking God that he had been counted worthy to share the cup of Christ, while the crowd listened to a man bless God on his way into the flames. And there were the prayers of mothers. Monica, the mother of Augustine, prayed for her brilliant, wayward son for years — through his running, his heresies, and his immorality — weeping over him, a bishop told her, more than mothers weep over the bodily deaths of their children. She was assured that the child of those tears could not be lost. Augustine was converted at thirty-two, and became the greatest theologian of the early church. Somewhere tonight there is a mother praying for a son the way Monica prayed. Heaven has a long memory for those prayers, and so should we.

The desert's rhythm was ordered by Benedict of Nursia, whose Rule, written around the year 530, arranged the monk's day around set hours of prayer and psalm-singing — the "work of God," as he called it, built on David's word: "Seven times a day I praise you" (Psalm 119:164). For a thousand years, in monasteries across Europe, the Psalms were prayed through week by week, day and night, in an unbroken relay. Whatever else the medieval church got wrong, it kept the Psalter on its lips — and it preserved, copied, and carried the Scriptures through centuries when few others could read them.

The Reformation brought prayer home. In 1549, Archbishop Thomas Cranmer gave England the first Book of Common Prayer — the church's services, morning and evening prayer, the Psalms and the Scripture readings, all in English, the language of plowmen and merchants, instead of Latin. For the first time, ordinary believers did not merely listen to prayer; they prayed it, together, in words they understood, with the Bible open in their own tongue. Cranmer's written prayers — his "collects" — taught a nation to pray in sentences shaped like Scripture itself: addressing God for who He is, asking in Christ's name, resting on His promises.

The Puritans who followed took prayer out of the service book and into the closet. They prized "secret prayer" — the daily, private, unhurried meeting with God behind a shut door, exactly as Jesus commanded — along with family worship at the kitchen table and days of fasting and prayer when trouble came. Their diaries are full of recorded answers. They were not casual about prayer because they were not casual about God.

Then there are the movements, the moments when prayer caught fire across whole peoples. In 1727, in the little Moravian community of Herrnhut in Germany, under Count Nikolaus von Zinzendorf, believers organized a prayer watch: men and women taking hour-long turns, around the clock, so that intercession never ceased. That watch continued, hour by hour, for over one hundred years. Out of that prayer meeting came one of the great missionary movements in history — Moravian missionaries carried the gospel to the Caribbean, to Greenland, to Africa and the Americas, and their praying and singing influenced John Wesley himself. A century of prayer moved the map of the world.

In 1806, five students at Williams College in Massachusetts, caught in a thunderstorm during a prayer meeting, took shelter under a haystack and there committed themselves to carry the gospel overseas. The Haystack Prayer Meeting, as it came to be called, lit the fuse of the American foreign missions movement. And in 1857, a businessman named Jeremiah Lanphier opened a room on Fulton Street in New York City for a noon prayer meeting, in the middle of a financial panic that was throwing men out of work by the thousands. Six men came the first day. Within months, thousands were gathering at noon in churches and halls across the city and then across the nation — businessmen, clerks, the unemployed — in what became known as the Prayer Revival of 1857–58, in which it is estimated a million people were converted. No famous preacher stood at the center of it. A prayer meeting did.

The lesson of the centuries is plain: every great moving of God among His people has been preceded, accompanied, and sustained by prayer — usually the unglamorous kind, offered by people whose names history kept only some of.

Some of those names deserve a paragraph of their own. George Müller of Bristol, England, built orphanages in the 1800s that cared for more than ten thousand children over his lifetime, and he ran them on prayer as a stated policy: he never asked any man for money, never took a salary, never went into debt. He told God his needs and kept a journal of the answers — tens of thousands of them, recorded over sixty years, bread arriving at the door on mornings when the cupboards were empty and the children were already seated at the table. And Charles Spurgeon, the great London preacher of the same century, called the prayer meeting of his church its "boiler room" — the hidden furnace underneath all the visible machinery — and would take visitors downstairs to see the hundreds praying while he preached above, saying, "Here is the powerhouse of this church." Different centuries, different countries, one conviction: the praying is not the decoration on the work of God. The praying is the engine of it.

6. Prayer Today: The Same God, the Same Door

Prayer today is prayed in more languages, in more countries, by more believers than at any other moment in the history of the world. The church has spread to every continent, and where she has spread, she has prayed — in great cathedrals and in rented rooms, in suburban sanctuaries and in villages, in the open and in hiding.

And she prays, in many places, the way the first church prayed: under pressure. In countries where confessing Christ costs a job, a family, or a life, believers gather quietly and pray the prayers of Acts with an understanding the comfortable church has to work to recover. They pray for their persecutors, as Jesus commanded. They pray for boldness, as the church in Acts 4 prayed — not for the danger to be removed, but for courage inside it. The persecuted church has never once, in twenty centuries, been without prayer. That is not a coincidence. It is the reason she is still here.

Prayer today also happens in ordinary places, and that is where most of it has always happened. It happens at kitchen tables and hospital bedsides, in trucks on night roads and in break rooms and in the last quiet minute before sleep. It happens in ministries like this one, where prayer is not a program but a daily rhythm — a daily word of prayer and encouragement for whoever needs it, and a live time of prayer each Sunday night where requests are named and carried together. No invented details are needed to say the simple truth: the same door Hannah wept at, Daniel knelt at, and the Moravians kept watch at stands open tonight, and ordinary people are still walking through it.

The technology has changed — a prayer request can cross the world in a second now, and a church can pray for a stranger on another continent by name the same day. The God who answers has not changed at all. "The prayer of a righteous person has great power as it is working" (James 5:16). James wrote that about Elijah, a man, he says, "with a nature like ours" (James 5:17). Like ours. The history of prayer is not a history of superhuman saints. It is a history of ordinary, weak, sinful people who called on the name of the Lord — and of the God who, century after century, answered.

7. When You Have No Words

Maybe you have read this far and your honest thought is: I do not know how to pray like that. Your prayers feel small. You run out of words in half a minute. You get distracted, you repeat yourself, sometimes all you can manage is a groan over a situation you cannot fix and cannot even describe.

Then this last word of the history is for you, because the Bible's final teaching on prayer is about exactly that weakness — and it is some of the best news in the whole Book:

"Likewise the Spirit helps us in our weakness. For we do not know what to pray for as we ought, but the Spirit himself intercedes for us with groanings too deep for words." — Romans 8:26 (ESV)

You do not pray alone. When your words run out, the Holy Spirit takes your groaning — your grief, your fear, your wordless longing for God to act — and carries it to the Father in a language heaven understands. And at the Father's right hand, the Son who prayed in Gethsemane intercedes for you still. Your prayer life rests on two Pray-ers who never sleep and never fail. Yours is simply to come.

So come the way they all came. Come like Abraham, pleading for people you love. Come like Hannah, with words no one can hear. Come like Daniel, on schedule, windows open, whatever the decree says. Come like the church in Acts, together, in the night, for somebody in chains. Come like Jesus taught us — to the Father, in secret, with six small petitions that cover everything. Start where Enosh's generation started. Just call on the name of the Lord.

And if you need a place to begin tomorrow morning, begin the way the church has always begun. Set a time and keep it, even a short one — Daniel's strength was in his rhythm, not his emergencies. Open a Psalm and pray it back to God in your own words, putting your own names and troubles where David put his. Pray the Lord's Prayer slowly, one petition at a time, and let each line open into your own life. Pray with somebody else when you can — a spouse, a friend, a church that gathers for it — because prayer was never meant to be a solo instrument. And when the answer tarries, remember the widow in Jesus' parable, and do not lose heart. The history you have just read is four thousand years of evidence that God hears, God answers, and God is worth the asking.

Let us pray. Our Father in heaven, we thank You that from the first generation that called on Your name until this very hour, You have never once turned a deaf ear to Your people. You heard a barren woman in a temple and a prisoner in a foreign land; You heard Your own Son in a garden; You have heard the whispered prayers of saints whose names we will not know until heaven. Teach us to pray. Forgive our prayerlessness, our hurry, our unbelief. When we have no words, let Your Spirit pray in us; when we have too many, let Your Word shape them. And give us grace to keep calling on Your name until the day prayer gives way to praise, and faith gives way to sight. In the name of Jesus, who taught us to pray and who ever lives to intercede for us. Amen.
