A Deep Study

Faith in the God We Cannot See

Answering “I can’t believe in someone I can’t see”

  • Hebrews 11
  • Faith
  • Hope

"Now faith is the assurance of things hoped for, the conviction of things not seen."

Hebrews 11:1 (ESV)

The Question, Stated Honestly

Sooner or later, if you share your faith long enough, somebody you love is going to say it to you plainly:

"I can't put my faith in someone I cannot see."

Do not mock that sentence. Do not rush past it, and do not answer it like it was stupid — because it is not stupid. It is one of the most honest sentences a person can say. The friend who says it is usually not trying to win an argument. He is telling you how the world looks from where he stands: sight feels like the gold standard of knowing. We believe our eyes. We say "seeing is believing" like it settled something. And God — the God of the Bible — cannot be seen. That is not a rumor started by atheists. That is something the Bible itself says, out loud, more than once.

Here is the bad news first, because the bad news is what makes the good news good: the objection rests on something true. God is invisible. You cannot put Him under a light, sit Him across a table, or take a picture of Him. Any faith that pretends otherwise is selling something.

But here is where the objection quietly makes its mistake: it assumes that seeing is the only way — or even the best way — of knowing something is real. It assumes faith in an unseen God must be a blind leap into the dark, a guess dressed up in church clothes. The Bible disagrees. Not by denying that God is unseen, but by redefining what faith is, by showing us a God who made Himself known on purpose, and by pointing out that the seeing the skeptic is waiting for was never the doorway into faith in the first place — it is the fruit of it.

This study walks that road slowly, verse by verse. If you are the one who has said those words — "I can't believe in someone I can't see" — this was written with you in the room.

What Faith Actually Is

Everything turns on the definition. Most people who reject faith have rejected a definition the Bible never gave. They think faith means believing something without evidence, or believing it harder precisely because there is no evidence. Scripture says the opposite:

"Now faith is the assurance of things hoped for, the conviction of things not seen." (Hebrews 11:1)

Two words in that one verse carry the whole study.

The first is assurance — in the Greek, hypostasis (ὑπόστασις). It is built from hypo, "under," and stasis, "standing" — that which stands under a thing, its substance, its foundation. It is the same word Hebrews 1:3 uses when it calls the Son "the exact imprint of his nature" — God's very substance. Faith, then, is not a feeling floating in the air. It has substance. It stands on something. The hope of the Christian is not a wish; it has a foundation under it, and the foundation is what God has already said and already done.

The second word is conviction — in the Greek, elenchos (ἔλεγχος). It is a courtroom word: evidence, proof, the exhibit laid before the jury that settles the question. Read the verse again with that in its place: faith is "the evidence of things not seen." The writer does not say faith is what we use instead of evidence. He says faith itself stands in the place of evidence for things our eyes cannot reach — the way a signed deed is evidence you own land you may never have walked on.

So the Bible's own definition answers the objection before the objection finishes its sentence. Faith is not believing without evidence. Faith is trusting God on the strength of evidence — His works, His witnesses, His Word — for the things that lie past the reach of our eyes. A blind leap is not faith. It is the counterfeit faith gets accused of being.

Invisible by Nature, Not Absent

Now let us be honest about the invisibility itself, because the Bible is. There is a word the New Testament uses for it: aoratos (ἀόρατος) — a-, "not," plus horao, "to see." The unseen one. Watch how Scripture uses it:

"For his invisible attributes, namely, his eternal power and divine nature, have been clearly perceived, ever since the creation of the world, in the things that have been made. So they are without excuse." (Romans 1:20)

"He is the image of the invisible God, the firstborn of all creation." (Colossians 1:15)

"To the King of the ages, immortal, invisible, the only God, be honor and glory forever and ever. Amen." (1 Timothy 1:17)

"...for he endured as seeing him who is invisible." (Hebrews 11:27)

Notice what the Bible is doing. It does not treat God's invisibility as a problem to be explained away. It treats it as a title — "the King of the ages, immortal, invisible." Paul even adds, of that same God, that He "dwells in unapproachable light, whom no one has ever seen or can see" (1 Timothy 6:16). Why? Because "God is spirit" (John 4:24). A spirit does not have a body to be photographed. Invisibility is not God hiding. Invisibility is what God is, the way wetness is what water is.

And there is mercy in it. When Moses asked to see God's glory, God answered: "You cannot see my face, for man shall not see me and live" (Exodus 33:20). The veil between us and the unveiled glory of God is not His distance. For sinners, it is His kindness — the same kindness that lowers the voltage of the sun behind a morning sky so our eyes are not destroyed by looking at it.

So let the record be clear: the Christian does not believe in a God who used to be visible and went missing. The Christian believes in a God who is invisible by nature — and who, precisely because we could never climb up to see Him, came down and made Himself known. That is the next turn in the road, and it is the heart of everything.

The God Who Made Himself Seen

Here is the part of the story the objection usually has not heard: Christianity does not ask anyone to believe in a God who stayed invisible. The whole center of the faith is that the invisible God made Himself seen — once, in history, in a body that could be watched, heard, and touched.

"And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we have seen his glory, glory as of the only Son from the Father, full of grace and truth." (John 1:14)

"No one has ever seen God; the only God, who is at the Father's side, he has made him known." (John 1:18)

Colossians 1:15 said it in one line: Jesus is "the image of the invisible God." An image is what makes the invisible visible. You want to know what the unseen God is like? The faith does not point you at the sky and tell you to squint. It points you at a Man — His words, His mercy toward the people nobody wanted, His hands on lepers, His tears at a friend's grave, His silence before His accusers, His cross, and His empty tomb.

And it does not ask you to take that on a feeling. John opens his first letter like a witness taking the stand:

"That which was from the beginning, which we have heard, which we have seen with our eyes, which we looked upon and have touched with our hands, concerning the word of life — the life was made manifest, and we have seen it, and testify to it and proclaim to you the eternal life, which was with the Father and was made manifest to us — that which we have seen and heard we proclaim also to you, so that you too may have fellowship with us; and indeed our fellowship is with the Father and with his Son Jesus Christ." (1 John 1:1-3)

Heard. Seen with our eyes. Looked upon. Touched with our hands. John is practically arguing with this study's title: we saw Him, he says, so that you can believe the testimony of people who did. That is how nearly everything true reaches us. Luke says the same about his Gospel — that he built his account from "those who from the beginning were eyewitnesses," and wrote it carefully, "having followed all things closely for some time past," so his reader could "have certainty concerning the things you have been taught" (Luke 1:1-4). Luke was a doctor. He investigated. He interviewed. He checked.

And when Jesus rose, He did not leave His friends with a warm feeling about it. Acts 1:3 says "He presented himself alive to them after his suffering by many proofs, appearing to them during forty days and speaking about the kingdom of God." The word for proofs is tekmeria (τεκμήρια) — the strongest word Greek has for evidence: conclusive signs, the kind of proof that ends an argument. Forty days of them. Meals eaten. Wounds touched. Hundreds of witnesses, most of them still alive and available for cross-examination when Paul wrote about it (1 Corinthians 15:6).

So the shape of the Christian faith is this: God was seen — by witnesses, in public, on the record — and the rest of us believe through their word. Not instead of evidence. Through the evidence of testimony. Which is exactly the way the risen Jesus said it would work, as we will see in a moment.

Thomas: The Objection Was Already in the Room

You are not the first person to say "I can't believe in someone I can't see." One of Jesus's own twelve said it first, and said it harder than most people dare:

"Now Thomas, one of the twelve, called the Twin, was not with them when Jesus came. So the other disciples told him, 'We have seen the Lord.' But he said to them, 'Unless I see in his hands the mark of the nails, and place my finger into the mark of the nails, and place my hand into his side, I will never believe.'

Eight days later, his disciples were inside again, and Thomas was with them. Although the doors were locked, Jesus came and stood among them and said, 'Peace be with you.' Then he said to Thomas, 'Put your finger here, and see my hands; and put out your hand, and place it in my side. Do not disbelieve, but believe.' Thomas answered him, 'My Lord and my God!' Jesus said to him, 'Have you believed because you have seen me? Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have believed.'" (John 20:24-29)

Look at how Jesus handles the skeptic. He does not scold him. He does not say, "Thomas, your standards are too high." He shows up, eight days later, walks through a locked door, and offers the very evidence Thomas demanded — "Put your finger here." The risen Christ is not offended by honest questions. He answers them.

But then He turns His eyes past Thomas, down the centuries, to everyone who would ever read that page — to you — and He pronounces a blessing on the very people this study is about: "Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have believed." Not pitied. Not second-class. Blessed. Jesus knew the church would be built almost entirely of people who never saw Him with their eyes, and He did not call that a weakness of the faith. He called it a blessedness.

And notice Thomas's confession when he finally saw: "My Lord and my God!" It is the fullest confession of who Jesus is in all four Gospels — and it came from the man who doubted longest. If you are the doubter in the room, you are in the company of an apostle.

Written for People Who Weren't There

John tells us plainly why his Gospel exists:

"Now Jesus did many other signs in the presence of the disciples, which are not written in this book; but these are written so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that by believing you may have life in his name." (John 20:30-31)

These are written — so that you may believe. The book in your hands is not a leftover for people who missed the real thing. It is God's chosen provision for non-eyewitnesses, which is to say, for nearly the whole church across two thousand years. Peter wrote the same truth to scattered believers who had never laid eyes on Jesus:

"Though you have not seen him, you love him. Though you do not now see him, you believe in him and rejoice with joy that is inexpressible and filled with glory, obtaining the outcome of your faith, the salvation of your souls." (1 Peter 1:8-9)

Read that slowly. Peter does not write, "Though you have not seen him, you love him — and that's a shame, try harder." He writes it as the normal, healthy, joy-filled shape of Christian faith: not seeing, and yet loving; not seeing, and yet believing; not seeing, and rejoicing with joy inexpressible. The believers Peter praised most warmly were people in exactly your position.

Here is the detail that ought to stop us in our tracks. When Jesus prayed His great prayer in John 17 — the prayer of a man about to die for His friends — He prayed past His disciples to a category of people who did not exist yet: "I do not ask for these only, but also for those who will believe in me through their word" (John 17:20). Through their word. Jesus Himself defined how the unseen generations would believe: not by a private vision, not by sight, but through the word of the witnesses. And He prayed for those people by name, as it were, the night before the cross. If you believe through their word, you are not an accident of history. You were prayed for.

That verse is personal to me, and I will tell you why before this study ends.

Seen by His Effects: The Wind

Some things are known not by being seen but by what they do. Jesus reached for exactly that picture when a religious leader came to Him at night, confused about how a person could be born again:

"The wind blows where it wishes, and you hear its sound, but you do not know where it comes from or where it goes. So it is with everyone who is born of the Spirit." (John 3:8)

The Hebrew word underneath this whole conversation is Ruach (רוּחַ) — breath, wind, Spirit, one word for all three. Nobody has ever seen the wind. Not once, in all of human history, has a human eye seen wind itself. What we see is what the wind does: grass bending on a hillside at dawn, trees bowing, waves standing up on the water, a flag snapping straight. And on the strength of those effects, no sane person doubts the wind exists. We do not call belief in wind a "blind leap." We call it paying attention.

God says He is known the same way. "For what can be known about God is plain to them, because God has shown it to them" (Romans 1:19) — shown in "the things that have been made," so that His "invisible attributes... have been clearly perceived" (Romans 1:20). Clearly perceived — not directly seen, but genuinely perceived, through His works, the way wind is perceived in the grass. David sang it: "The heavens declare the glory of God, and the sky above proclaims his handiwork" (Psalm 19:1).

And the greatest of God's effects are not in the sky. They are in people. A man enslaved for thirty years walks free and stays free. A mother who buried her child stands at the grave and sings. Enemies forgive. Addicts recover and then go back into the same rooms they were rescued from to pull others out. You cannot see the wind — but try telling a man standing in it that nothing is moving just because he cannot point at it.

Moses Endured "As Seeing Him Who Is Invisible"

Of all the people in Scripture who might have demanded a sighting, Moses had the best claim. He led a nation. He faced down Pharaoh. And the writer of Hebrews, looking back over Moses's whole life, sums up his secret in one strange, beautiful phrase:

"By faith he left Egypt, not being afraid of the anger of the king, for he endured as seeing him who is invisible." (Hebrews 11:27)

As seeing him who is invisible. That is the Bible's own paradox, and it is not a mistake. Moses endured — through Pharaoh's threats, through forty years of wilderness, through a complaining nation — the way a man endures when he is looking at something real. He could not see God with his eyes. He lived as though he could, because by faith, in every way that matters for standing firm, he did.

Moses is not the exception in that chapter. He is the pattern. Hebrews 11 is a whole hall of people who believed without the sight the skeptic waits for:

"These all died in faith, not having received the things promised, but having seen them and greeted them from afar, and having acknowledged that they were strangers and exiles on the earth." (Hebrews 11:13)

They saw the promises — from afar, by faith — and greeted them like a man waves at a ship coming into harbor. They died before the promises arrived, and the Bible calls that not failure but faith. Then the chapter turns and looks at us:

"And all these, though commended through their faith, did not receive what was promised, since God had provided something better for us, that apart from us they should not be made perfect." (Hebrews 11:39-40)

Something better for us. We stand where Moses stood — believing in the God we cannot see — but we stand on this side of the cross, with the eyewitness record complete and the empty tomb behind us. If Moses could endure as seeing Him who is invisible with only a burning bush and a promise, what excuse do we have, holding the whole testimony in our hands?

Sight Is Not the Gold Standard

One more turn, and it may be the most important one for the honest skeptic: sight was never as reliable as we pretend. Paul states the Christian's operating rule without apology:

"...for we walk by faith, not by sight." (2 Corinthians 5:7)

"...as we look not to the things that are seen but to the things that are unseen. For the things that are seen are transient, but the things that are unseen are eternal." (2 Corinthians 4:18)

Notice the logic. Paul does not say the unseen things are less real than the seen ones. He says the opposite: the seen things are the temporary ones. Your eyes report to you a world that is passing away. The things you cannot see — God, His promises, the soul, eternity — are the things that last. To demand sight as the price of belief is to demand that the eternal submit to the inspection of the temporary.

And eyes deceive. The first sin in human history was, in part, an act of eyesight: "So when the woman saw that the tree was good for food, and that it was a delight to the eyes, and that the tree was to be desired to make one wise, she took of its fruit and ate..." (Genesis 3:6). Eve saw — accurately, as far as her eyes went. The fruit really was good-looking. What her eyes could not show her was the truth about it. Sight reported the surface and missed the reality, and the human race has been trusting its eyes over God's word ever since. Every addict knows this in his bones: the bottle, the pill, the next high all looked like relief. The eyes were not lying about the shine. They were lying about everything that mattered.

Meanwhile, every one of us — skeptic and believer alike — lives daily by faith in unseen things. You have never seen your own great-grandparents, yet you do not doubt they existed; you believe the testimony of your family. You have never seen gravity; you have seen things fall. You have never seen the love of the people who love you; you have seen what it does, and you stake your life on it. No one calls that irrational. Testimony and effects are not second-rate ways of knowing. For most of what matters most, they are the only ways of knowing — and God, in His kindness, has given us both in abundance.

The Reformed Shape of Faith: Knowledge, Assent, Trust

The old teachers of the church described saving faith in three movements, and it is worth learning them plainly, because they answer the objection at its root:

  1. Knowledge (notitia) — knowing what God has said and done. You cannot trust a Christ you know nothing about. This is why the witnesses wrote, why Luke investigated, why this study quotes Scripture in full instead of waving at it.
  2. Assent (assensus) — agreeing that it is true. Not just knowing the story of Jesus, but judging it to be fact: He lived, He died, He rose, and it happened in real history under a real Roman governor.
  3. Trust (fiducia) — resting your whole weight on Christ personally. A man can know a bridge is strong and agree it will hold, and still never cross it. Faith crosses. Faith is the hand; Christ is the treasure. The hand does not make itself rich by grasping — the gift makes the hand rich. That is why it is faith alone: nothing in my hand is contributed to the gift it receives.

And here is where the objection turns inside out completely. The skeptic says: "Show me God, and then I will believe." Jesus says the order runs the other way:

"Truly, truly, I say to you, unless one is born again he cannot see the kingdom of God." (John 3:3)

Cannot see. The seeing is on the far side of the new birth, not the near side. Paul explains why: "The natural person does not accept the things of the Spirit of God, for they are folly to him, and he is not able to understand them because they are spiritually discerned" (1 Corinthians 2:14). The problem was never that the evidence is missing. The problem is that dead eyes cannot take it in — and the cure for dead eyes is not more light but new life. God does not wait for us to see and then give us life. He gives life, and then we see. Paul again, of the God who said "let there be light": "For God, who said, 'Let light shine out of darkness,' has shone in our hearts to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ" (2 Corinthians 4:6). The glory of God has a face. It is the face of Jesus Christ — made known to us in the Gospel, and made visible to the heart when the Spirit opens our eyes.

So the true answer to "I can't believe in someone I can't see" is not "try harder to believe." It is this: come to Christ as you are — doubting, blind, honest about both — and ask the God who commanded light out of darkness to shine in your heart. The seeing you are waiting for is real. It is just on the other side of surrender, waiting for you the way it waited for Thomas, and for me.

How to Answer in Conversation

When somebody says it to you — at a kitchen table, in a recovery room, on a porch after a meeting — here is the pattern this study has built, in plain order:

  1. Agree honestly. "You're right about one thing: God can't be seen, and the Bible says so itself. Faith was never supposed to be pretending."
  2. Ask about the wind. "You've never seen the wind either — but you've never doubted it, because you see what it does. What if God is known the same way — by His effects?"
  3. Point to the eyewitnesses. "People did see Him. Jesus is the image of the invisible God. They heard Him, touched Him, watched Him die, and ate with Him after He rose — and they wrote it down. We believe through their word, and Jesus said that's exactly how it would work. He even blessed the people who'd believe that way."
  4. Turn it gently. "The Bible says the seeing comes after the new birth, not before it. So don't wait to see before you come. Come, and ask Him to open your eyes."
  5. Tell your story. Testimony is an effect of the wind. You are standing evidence.

And if you want it in one breath, the way I would say it:

"Friend, I understand that — I said some version of it myself. But you can't see the wind either, and you've never once doubted it, because you can see what it does. God made Himself seen once, in Jesus — real people touched Him and wrote it down — and the rest of us believe through their word. I never saw Him with my eyes. I was at the end of myself, thirty years in addiction, sitting in a men's program in South Carolina, and He met me through the words of a book — John 17, where Jesus prayed for the people who would believe through the apostles' word. That was me He was praying for. I didn't see Him. I saw what He did — in my life, from the inside — and that was enough. It's still enough, every day."

That last part is not a figure of speech. In April of 2024, at the Haven of Rest in Anderson, South Carolina, I read John 17:3 — "And this is eternal life, that they know you, the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom you have sent" — and verse 20, the prayer for those who would believe through the apostles' word, and I believed. No vision. No voice from the ceiling. A book, a broken man, and the God who was there all along, unseen — and who has not been absent one day since.

Reflect — Questions & Answers

Q: Is faith in an unseen God just believing without evidence?

A: No. Hebrews 11:1 defines faith with two evidence-words: hypostasis, substance that stands under our hope, and elenchos, the courtroom word for proof. Christian faith rests on evidence — God's works in creation, the eyewitness testimony of the apostles, and the "many proofs" (Acts 1:3, tekmeria) of the resurrection. What faith adds is trust: resting on Christ personally, not merely agreeing that the evidence is real.

Q: If God wants us to believe, why doesn't He just show Himself?

A: In one sense, He did — "the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we have seen his glory" (John 1:14). In another sense, unveiled sight of God is not safe for sinners: "man shall not see me and live" (Exodus 33:20). God's invisibility is His nature (He is spirit, John 4:24) and His mercy. And Jesus teaches that sight follows the new birth rather than preceding it (John 3:3) — God gives eyes, then we see.

Q: Was Thomas wrong to demand to see?

A: He was honest, and Jesus met his honesty with evidence, not rebuke — "Put your finger here, and see my hands" (John 20:27). But Jesus also used the moment to bless a different kind of believer: "Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have believed" (John 20:29). Honest questions are welcome at the feet of Christ. Demands that God perform on our terms before we will yield are not questions anymore; they are conditions — and Thomas, to his credit, dropped his the moment Christ stood before him.

Q: How is believing the apostles' testimony different from believing any old claim?

A: The testimony was investigated (Luke 1:1-4), given by named eyewitnesses who claimed to have seen, heard, and touched (1 John 1:1-3), and sealed by witnesses who gained nothing earthly from it and lost everything for it. And it arrives with God's own effects attached — two thousand years of changed lives, including the one writing this study, who believed through John 17:20 without ever seeing Jesus, exactly as Jesus prayed it would happen.

Q: I still can't see Him. What do I do?

A: What Thomas did, and what I did: come anyway. Bring the doubt with you; He is not afraid of it. Read John 17 and John 20 slowly, out loud if you can. Ask the God who said "let light shine out of darkness" to shine in your heart (2 Corinthians 4:6). Walk by faith, not by sight (2 Corinthians 5:7) — and watch the grass bend. The wind you cannot see has a name.
