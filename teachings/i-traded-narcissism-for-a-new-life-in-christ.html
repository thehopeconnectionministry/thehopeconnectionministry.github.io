I Traded Narcissism for a New Life in Christ

Not improvement — a new creation. The old self traded for the life of Christ.

  • New Creation
  • 2 Corinthians 5
  • Romans 7
  • Grace

Therefore, if anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation. The old has passed away; behold, the new has come.

2 Corinthians 5:17 (ESV)

The old life: when “me” sits on the throne

For over thirty years, my whole world revolved around one person: me. The drugs were only the loudest symptom. Underneath them was a life arranged entirely around my appetites — my next fix, my comfort, my feelings, my wants. People were either useful to me or in my way.

Paul describes that life with a phrase that stings because it's accurate. Writing about the last days, he says people will be “lovers of self” before they are anything else (2 Timothy 3:2), and the list that follows — proud, abusive, disobedient, ungrateful, unholy — reads like the fruit of one root: the self on the throne. Isaiah said it centuries earlier in a single line: “All we like sheep have gone astray; we have turned — every one — to his own way” (Isaiah 53:6). Narcissistic behavior is just that turning, lived out loud. It is the old Adam doing what he has done since Eden: putting himself where only God belongs.

And here is the part the self-help world cannot face: a man cannot trade in a life that owns him. I tried managing it, moderating it, apologizing for it. The throne never once negotiated. The law showed me the truth about the old life — Romans 7 calls it a body of death — and the law could diagnose me, but it could not deliver me. “Wretched man that I am! Who will deliver me from this body of death?” (Romans 7:24). That question has an answer, and it is not “try harder.”

The trade God made

“Thanks be to God through Jesus Christ our Lord!” (Romans 7:25).

The New Testament's word for what happened to me is not improvement. It is exchange. Listen to Paul, writing to a church full of former self-ruled people like me: “Therefore, if anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation. The old has passed away; behold, the new has come” (2 Corinthians 5:17).

The Greek word for “new” is kainos (ky-NOSS) — new in kind, not merely new in time. God did not hand me a newer model of the same self. He gave me a different life altogether. Ezekiel promised it long before: “I will give you a new heart, and a new spirit I will put within you. And I will remove the heart of stone from your flesh and give you a heart of flesh” (Ezekiel 36:26). A heart transplant, not a heart tune-up.

And the ground of that exchange is one verse later in Paul's argument: “For our sake he made him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God” (2 Corinthians 5:21). The Reformers called this the wonderful exchange: Christ took my record — the selfishness, the addiction, the thirty years of me-first — and I received His righteousness. I did not trade up. I was traded for — at a cross, by grace alone, through faith alone. That is why the old life no longer has a claim: the man who owed the debt was crucified with Christ, and the man writing to you now lives by another's life. “I have been crucified with Christ. It is no longer I who live, but Christ who lives in me” (Galatians 2:20).

Martin Luther captured the shape of the new life in a sentence worth memorizing: “A Christian is a perfectly free lord of all, subject to none. A Christian is a perfectly dutiful servant of all, subject to all.” Freed from the throne, the new man doesn't climb back onto it. He takes up a towel.

The new life: what it looks like on the ground

The trade is finished; the living of it is daily. Three marks show up again and again in Scripture:

  1. A new center. “Do nothing from selfish ambition or conceit, but in humility count others more significant than yourselves” (Philippians 2:3). Paul holds up Christ Himself as the pattern — the One who had every right to the throne and took the cross instead (Philippians 2:5–8). For a man who counted nobody above himself for three decades, this is not decoration. It is the daily shape of the new creation.
  2. A daily reckoning. The old self still knocks. Romans 6:11 commands what feelings never deliver: “Consider yourselves dead to sin and alive to God in Christ Jesus.” Dead men don't get a vote. When the old patterns surface, the answer is not despair and not denial — it is confession, a quick return to the truth of who we already are in Christ (1 John 1:9), and the put-off/put-on walk of Colossians 3.
  3. A renewed mind. “Do not be conformed to this world, but be transformed by the renewal of your mind” (Romans 12:2). The old life ran on the world's software — self first, always. The new life is re-trained by the Word until its instincts change: from “What do I get?” to “Who can I serve?” From “What do I feel?” to “What is true?”

The invitation

Maybe you see your old self in this teaching the way I see mine. Everything is about you — and you are miserable, because a throne makes a poor Savior. You do not need to become a better version of yourself. You need what I needed: to die with Christ and be made new by Him. The old can pass away. The new can come. “If anyone is in Christ” — anyone. It happened for a man with thirty years of me-first behind him, and it can happen for you today.

I spent thirty years building a throne for a king who was killing me. Jesus didn't renovate that throne. He crucified that king — and gave me a new life in His place.

Soli Deo Gloria.

Reflect — Questions & Answers

Q: Is “narcissistic behavior” really a spiritual problem, or just a personality problem?
A: Scripture treats it as the root sin of the fallen heart: “lovers of self” (2 Timothy 3:2), each one turned “to his own way” (Isaiah 53:6). Personality shapes how it looks, but self on the throne is the Adam in every one of us — which is why the answer is not management but a new heart (Ezekiel 36:26).

Q: What is the difference between improving myself and becoming a new creation?
A: Improvement keeps the same man and polishes him; the new creation is a different kind of life — kainos, new in kind (2 Corinthians 5:17). Improvement says “try harder”; the gospel says the old man was crucified with Christ and the new man lives by Christ's life in him (Galatians 2:20). One is your work on yourself. The other is God's work, received by faith.

Q: I am a Christian, but I still catch the old self-centered patterns. What do I do with them?
A: Three things, from the teaching above: reckon them dead (Romans 6:11), confess them quickly and honestly (1 John 1:9), and put on the opposite practice the same day (Colossians 3; Philippians 2:3–4). The presence of a battle doesn't mean the trade failed. It means the new life is at war with what God has already condemned — and that war is itself a sign of life.
