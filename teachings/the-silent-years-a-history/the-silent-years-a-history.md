# The 400 Silent Years — A History

### Between Malachi and Matthew: How God Prepared the World in the Quiet

*The Hope Connection — Lewis Fuller*

---

## 1. The Last Words and the Long Quiet

Open your Bible to the end of the Old Testament and read the last words God spoke there. Then turn one page — a single page — and you are in the Gospel of Matthew, with angels singing over Bethlehem. It feels like a breath. It was four hundred years.

The prophet Malachi ministered in Jerusalem sometime around the year 430 before Christ, after the exiles had come home from Babylon, after the temple had been rebuilt and the walls raised again. His little book is the last prophetic word of the Old Testament, and it ends with a promise and a warning woven together — a promise that God was not finished, and that before the great day of the Lord, a forerunner would come:

> "Behold, I will send you Elijah the prophet before the great and awesome day of the LORD comes. And he will turn the hearts of fathers to their children and the hearts of children to their fathers, lest I come and strike the land with a decree of utter destruction." — Malachi 4:5–6 (ESV)

And then — silence. No prophet rose in Israel for four centuries. No "thus says the LORD" rang out in the temple courts. Heaven, which had spoken through Moses and Isaiah and Jeremiah and a dozen more, went quiet, and generation after generation of God's people were born, lived, and died inside that quiet. Grandfathers told grandsons about the God who used to speak. The Scriptures were read in the synagogues, the feasts were kept, the sacrifices were offered — and the sky said nothing.

If you have ever lived in one of God's silences, you know something of what those centuries felt like. You pray and hear nothing back. You do the next right thing and the heavens stay shut. It is one of the hardest tests of faith there is, because silence feels like absence. It feels like God has packed up and left. But the whole argument of this history — and it is written all over these four hundred years — is that God's silence is not God's absence. He was never more at work in Israel's story than in the centuries when He was not speaking. He was moving empires like furniture. He was putting a language in place, building roads, translating His own Book, and setting a stage — so that when He finally spoke again, the whole world would be able to hear it.

The apostle Paul, looking back over the quiet, gave it the name it deserves:

> "But when the fullness of time had come, God sent forth his Son, born of woman, born under the law." — Galatians 4:4 (ESV)

The fullness of time. Not early, not late — full. The silent years were not an empty waiting room. They were a pregnancy. This is the story of what God was doing while He was not saying anything.

## 2. Persia, and the Coming of the Greeks

When Malachi laid down his pen, Israel was a small province of the Persian Empire — the same empire whose kings, Cyrus and Darius and Artaxerxes, had sent the exiles home and paid for the temple's rebuilding. Persian rule was, by ancient standards, tolerant. The Jews worshiped their God, kept their laws, and rebuilt their life in the land, and Persia mostly let them be. It was under Persia that Ezra read the Law to the people and Nehemiah raised the walls. The last century of the Old Testament world was, in its way, a mercy.

But empires do not last, and this one's end came at a speed nobody believed possible. In 334 before Christ, a young king from Macedon named Alexander crossed into Asia with an army of perhaps forty thousand men, and in about a decade he dismantled the Persian Empire piece by piece — Tyre, Gaza, Egypt, and finally Persia itself. He marched through the region of Judea in 332, and Jewish tradition long treasured a story that he spared Jerusalem and bowed before the high priest; whether every detail of that story can be proved, the fact beneath it can: Jerusalem survived Alexander, and the world around it changed forever. Alexander died in Babylon in 323, only thirty-two years old, having conquered the world he knew and having no heir old enough to hold it.

What followed was a generation of war among his generals — the Diadochi, the "successors" — as they carved the empire into kingdoms. By about 301 the map had settled into the shape it would keep for the rest of the silent years. Egypt went to the Ptolemies, the line of Alexander's general Ptolemy. Syria and Mesopotamia went to the Seleucids, the line of Seleucus. And Judea — small, stubborn, temple-centered Judea — lay exactly on the border between them, on the road every army had to march to get at the other. For the next century and more, God's people would live in the corridor between two Greek empires, taxed by one, then conquered by the other, then fought over by both.

The third century before Christ, the Ptolemaic century, was comparatively gentle. The kings in Alexandria were mostly content to collect their taxes and leave Jewish worship alone. But Alexander had done something deeper than conquer territory: he had spread the Greek language and Greek culture — Hellenism — across the whole eastern world. Greek became the language of trade, of government, of the street in every great city. Greek gymnasiums, Greek theaters, Greek ideas sprang up everywhere, and Jewish communities were scattered through all of it, for the dispersion of God's people, begun in the exile, only widened in these years. Jews lived in Egypt, in Syria, in Asia Minor, in Greece itself. In Alexandria alone the Jewish population grew so large that they occupied whole districts of the city. Those scattered communities — the Diaspora — would one day be the very network along which the apostles traveled with the gospel. God was already laying the track. Nobody could see where it ran yet.

## 3. The Scriptures Learn Greek: The Septuagint

Of all the quiet works of God in these centuries, the one whose fruit we still eat every day happened in Alexandria, in the library city of the Ptolemies.

The Jews of Egypt had a problem their grandfathers never faced: they were losing Hebrew. Raised in Greek-speaking streets, doing business in Greek, thinking in Greek, many could no longer follow the Scriptures when they were read in the ancient tongue. The Word of God was in danger of becoming a sealed book to God's own scattered people — the bad news of the Diaspora. So, beginning in the third century before Christ, under Ptolemy the Second, Jewish scholars in Alexandria undertook to translate the Scriptures into Greek. The Law of Moses came first, sometime around the middle of that century; the Prophets and the Writings followed over the next hundred years or so, until the whole of Israel's Bible stood in the language of the world.

The translation is called the Septuagint, from the Latin word for seventy, after the ancient tradition — preserved in a document known as the Letter of Aristeas — that seventy-two elders, six from each of the twelve tribes, were brought from Jerusalem to do the work, and finished the Law in seventy-two days. The details of the tradition grew in the telling, as such stories do, but the heart of it is historical fact: the Hebrew Bible was rendered into Greek in Alexandria in the silent years, by Jewish scholars, for Jewish readers.

Consider what God had done. When the fullness of time came and the apostles went out into the world, they did not have to begin by translating the Scriptures into the world's language. The translation was already three hundred years old, sitting in synagogues from Alexandria to Rome. The Septuagint was the Bible of the Greek-speaking synagogues, the Bible from which the apostles most often quoted when they wrote the New Testament, the bridge over which the Old Testament walked into the Gentile world. When Paul stood up in a synagogue in Antioch or Corinth and opened the Scriptures to prove that Jesus was the Christ, the scroll in his hands was very likely this Greek translation, born in the silence. God had the Book printed, so to speak, in the world's language centuries before He sent the men who would preach from it. That is not absence. That is a Father preparing a room.

The translation did its work quietly for centuries, in ways both large and small. Large: it carried Israel's Scriptures out of a single small nation and set them on the shelves of the whole Mediterranean world, so that God-fearing Gentiles — men and women like the Ethiopian official and Cornelius the centurion in the book of Acts — could read Moses and Isaiah in their own tongue and come hungry to Israel's God before they ever heard a sermon. Small, and yet not small at all: its very words became part of the gospel's evidence. When the translators rendered Isaiah's prophecy of the sign to the house of David, they chose the Greek word *parthenos* — virgin — for the Hebrew word for a young woman, and so the Greek Bible read by the first Christians said plainly, "Behold, the virgin shall conceive and bear a son." When Matthew came to write his Gospel and quoted that prophecy of Jesus' birth, he quoted it in the Septuagint's own words. A translation made by Jewish scholars in Alexandria, in the middle of the silence, had already sharpened the prophecy's point two centuries before the angel visited Nazareth. God wastes nothing — not even a translator's word choice.

## 4. The Abomination and the Hammer

Around the year 200 before Christ, the balance of power shifted. Antiochus the Third of the Seleucid line defeated the Egyptians at Panium, near the sources of the Jordan, and Judea passed from Ptolemaic hands into Seleucid ones. At first the change was mild — Antiochus confirmed the Jews in their laws and even helped supply the temple sacrifices. But his son is the villain of the silent years, and his name deserves to be remembered, because Scripture had named his work long before he was born.

Antiochus the Fourth, who called himself Epiphanes — "God manifest" — took the Seleucid throne in 175. The wits of the ancient world called him Epimanes instead: the Madman. He was brilliant, erratic, and determined to weld his fractured kingdom together with one culture, one religion, one Greek way of life. In Jerusalem he found willing partners among a faction of Jews eager to Hellenize the city — men who actually bought the high priesthood from him, built a Greek gymnasium hard by the temple, and set about erasing the marks of the covenant. When Antiochus, humiliated in Egypt by a Roman ambassador, turned his rage on Jerusalem in 167, the pressure became persecution of a kind Israel had not known since Babylon. The daily sacrifices were stopped. The Sabbath was profaned, circumcision forbidden on pain of death, the scrolls of the Law torn and burned, and mothers who circumcised their sons were killed with the infants hung around their necks. Then, in December of 167, the abomination itself: an altar to Zeus raised over the altar of God, and swine — the unclean animal — sacrificed upon it in the holy place.

None of this caught heaven off guard. Two and three centuries earlier, the prophet Daniel, writing in exile, had seen this very king and this very crime, and had written it down: "Forces from him shall appear and profane the temple and fortress, and shall take away the regular burnt offering. And they shall set up the abomination that makes desolate" (Daniel 11:31, ESV). Chapter 8 and chapter 11 of Daniel trace Antiochus's career with a precision that has troubled unbelieving scholars ever since — some of them would rather date Daniel's book to Antiochus's own lifetime than admit that God foretold him. We need no such evasion. The God who was silent in the second century had spoken about the second century in the sixth. He had told His people in advance: this horror is coming, it has limits, and it will not last.

It did not last, because God raised up a hammer. In the village of Modein, an aged priest named Mattathias refused to sacrifice to the king's gods, killed the king's officer and the apostate Jew who stepped forward to comply, tore down the altar, and fled to the hills with his five sons, crying, "Let everyone who is zealous for the law and supports the covenant come out with me!" It was 167, and the Maccabean revolt had begun. When Mattathias died within the year, the leadership passed to his son Judas, surnamed Maccabeus — most likely from the Aramaic word for "hammer" — and Judas the Hammer waged a guerrilla war against the empire that became the stuff of legend: small bands of covenant-keepers ambushing armies, refusing to fight on the Sabbath until necessity taught them otherwise, and winning battle after impossible battle. In December of 164, three years to the month after the desecration, Judas's men retook the temple, tore down the polluted altar, built a new one, and rededicated the house of God with eight days of feasting — and found, by the beloved tradition, a single day's supply of consecrated oil that burned for all eight. The feast is Hanukkah, the Feast of Dedication, and it is no mere footnote to the New Testament. It is in your Bible:

> "At that time the Feast of Dedication took place at Jerusalem. It was winter, and Jesus was walking in the temple, in the colonnade of Solomon." — John 10:22–23 (ESV)

The Lord Jesus Himself walked in the temple at the feast that celebrated the silent years' great deliverance. The books that tell the full story, First and Second Maccabees, stand in the collection called the Apocrypha — valuable history, read by the Reformers with profit, but not received as Scripture, for reasons the companion history of the Bible sets out. What matters here is the pattern, because it is the pattern of the whole four hundred years: the worst hour was not the last hour, and the God who seemed silent was keeping His people through the fire by the hand of deliverers He raised up from nowhere.

## 5. The Hasmonean Kingdom and Its Decay

The revolt won more than a cleansed temple. Judas fell in battle in 160, and his brothers Jonathan and then Simon carried the struggle forward, from guerrilla war into statecraft, until in 142 the Seleucids, weakened and distracted, conceded what the Maccabees had bled for: Simon was recognized as high priest and ruler of an effectively independent Jewish state. For the first time since Babylon destroyed Jerusalem four and a half centuries earlier, Israel governed itself in its own land under its own priest-kings. Simon's line — called the Hasmoneans, from an ancestor named Hasmon — would rule for roughly eighty years. It was the last Jewish kingdom before the coming of Christ, and its story is the old, sad story of deliverance rotting from within.

Simon was murdered by his own son-in-law in 134. His son John Hyrcanus ruled long and expanded the borders — conquering Idumea to the south and forcing its people to convert, destroying the Samaritan temple on Mount Gerizim, breaking with the Pharisees and favoring their rivals. Hyrcanus's son Aristobulus took the further step no high priest had taken since the exile: he put a crown on his head and called himself king. Then came Alexander Jannaeus, who reigned from 103 to 76 and turned the kingdom's strength against its own people; when the Pharisees and their allies rose against him, he crucified eight hundred of them in Jerusalem, Josephus tells us, while feasting with his concubines in view of the crosses, their families slaughtered before their eyes. The priest-king of God's people, crucifying God's people. The deliverers had become the oppressors, and everybody in Judea knew it.

Jannaeus's widow, Salome Alexandra, reigned nine comparatively peaceful years and restored the Pharisees to influence. But when she died in 67, her two sons, Hyrcanus and Aristobulus, fell into civil war over the throne — and both of them, in the fatal move, appealed to the same rising power to settle their quarrel: Rome. Each brother sent gifts and arguments to the Roman general Pompey, then campaigning in the East, asking him to make him king. It is one of history's recurring tragedies: men who will not submit to God will submit to anybody else, and call it strategy. Rome answered the invitation. The Hasmonean kingdom had opened its own door to the empire that would one day crucify its Messiah.

## 6. The Parties Formed, and the Synagogue in Every Town

The silent years also built the religious world Jesus would walk into — every party He argued with, every institution He taught in, took shape in these centuries. When you open the Gospels, you are meeting the children of the four hundred years.

**The Pharisees** arose during the Hasmonean era, most likely out of the Hasidim, the "pious ones" who had rallied to the Maccabees. Their name probably means "the separated ones." They were largely laymen — scribes, teachers, tradesmen — devoted to the written Law and to the oral tradition that fenced it, and they held doctrines the Old Testament taught and the Sadducees denied: the resurrection of the dead, angels and spirits, and the sovereignty of God working through the free acts of men. The common people loved them. Jesus reserved His fiercest words for their hypocrisy — but note that He ate in their homes, argued with them as men worth arguing with, and shared their belief in the resurrection against the Sadducees.

**The Sadducees** were the aristocracy: wealthy, priestly families, many of them tied to the high priesthood, who ran the temple and cooperated with whatever empire currently held the whip hand. They accepted the Law of Moses as Scripture but rejected the Prophets' and Writings' authority as the Pharisees reckoned it, rejected the oral tradition entirely, and denied the resurrection, angels, and spirits — which is why, Luke notes dryly, they were called Sadducees. Their power died with the temple in the year 70, exactly as their theology deserved: men who live for the present arrangement perish with it.

**The Essenes** were the ones who walked away. Disgusted with a priesthood they judged corrupt — many trace their break to the Hasmoneans seizing the high priesthood, which belonged by descent to the line of Zadok — they withdrew to the desert, to settlements like the one at Qumran by the Dead Sea, where they lived in common, studied and copied the Scriptures, kept ritual purity with fierce discipline, and waited for God to come and set things right. They are never named in the New Testament, but the scrolls they hid in the caves are the subject of a companion piece on this shelf, and their existence explains something important: by the time of Jesus, the longing for God to break the silence had driven some of His people all the way out into the wilderness to wait for Him. It is no accident that when the silence finally broke, it broke in the wilderness, to a man dressed like an Essene's cousin and eating desert food.

**The Zealots** stood at the other pole — not withdrawal but the sword. Their roots ran back to the zeal of Phinehas and of Mattathias himself, and their defining refusal was simple: no king but God, and therefore no tax to Caesar. The movement crystallized around Judas the Galilean, who raised a revolt when Rome imposed a census in the year 6 after Christ, and whom the New Testament itself remembers — Gamaliel mentions him in Acts 5. In the generation after Jesus, zeal of this kind would help drive the nation into the catastrophic war with Rome. Among the Twelve, Jesus chose Simon the Zealot — and seated him beside Matthew the tax collector, a collaborator with Rome. Only the kingdom of God could hold those two men at one table. It still can.

Two more fixtures of the Gospel world were born in the silence. The **scribes** — professional scholars and copyists of the Law, heirs of Ezra's office — became the teachers of Israel, and the **synagogue** became her classroom. Synagogues had arisen, most likely, in the exile and the dispersion, wherever ten Jewish men could gather to hear the Law read and explained; by the silent years' end they stood in every town of Judea and Galilee and throughout the Diaspora, each with its scrolls, its ruler, its Sabbath service of reading and teaching. Jesus "taught in their synagogues" as a matter of course; Paul, in city after city, went to the synagogue first. God, in the quiet centuries, had built a pulpit in every town of the Jewish world and trained a people to gather weekly around the reading of a Book. Then He sent preachers to stand in those pulpits and open that Book and say: this is about Him.

## 7. Rome Arrives, and the Stage Is Set

Pompey entered Jerusalem in 63 before Christ, after a three-month siege, and walked — to the horror of every Jew who heard of it — into the Holy of Holies itself, the room no man but the high priest could enter, and that only once a year, with blood. He looked around, touched nothing, and left the temple treasures where they stood; the sacrifices resumed the next day. But the point had been made and could never be unmade: Judea belonged to Rome now. The Hasmoneans lingered as Roman puppets until the Senate, in 40, appointed a stronger man to hold the province: Herod, an Idumean — a descendant, ironically, of the very people John Hyrcanus had forcibly converted — who fought his way into Jerusalem in 37 and reigned as Rome's client king until his death in 4 before Christ.

Herod the Great is the Herod of Matthew's Gospel, and history confirms the Gospel's portrait: a builder of genius and a murderer of anyone he feared. He raised fortresses and palaces, founded the port city of Caesarea, and — his crowning work — rebuilt the temple in Jerusalem on a scale that made it one of the wonders of the world, beginning the work around 20 before Christ and enlarging the temple mount with retaining walls whose stones still stand today. The Jews who challenged Jesus in that temple said, "It has taken forty-six years to build this temple" (John 2:20, ESV), and they were simply reporting the news of their lifetime. But the same Herod executed his wife Mariamne, his mother-in-law, and three of his own sons, and ordered the slaughter of Bethlehem's baby boys when the wise men did not return — a crime so consistent with his character that no one who knows Herod doubts it. The emperor Augustus, hearing of the family murders, is said to have quipped that it was safer to be Herod's pig than Herod's son. Such was the king on Israel's throne when Israel's true King was born in a stable six miles from the palace.

Herod died in 4 before Christ — the king who hunted the newborn King did not live to see Him grow — and Rome divided his kingdom among his sons: Archelaus in Judea, Herod Antipas in Galilee, Philip in the northeast. Archelaus proved so brutal and incompetent that in the year 6 after Christ, Rome deposed him and made Judea a directly governed Roman province, ruled from Caesarea by officials called prefects, and later procurators. One consequence of that change echoes through the Gospels: a province's governor held the power of capital punishment, which is why the Sanhedrin, having condemned Jesus, still had to haul Him before the prefect Pontius Pilate — in office from 26 to 36 — and why Pilate, not Caiaphas, sat in the judgment seat on the morning of the crucifixion. Antipas, meanwhile, reigned on in Galilee through the whole of Jesus' ministry — the "fox" of Luke 13, the man who beheaded John the Baptist and mocked Jesus on the last night of His life. The political map of the Gospels — tetrarchs in the north, a Roman prefect in Jerusalem, a temple aristocracy caught between them — was drawn entirely in the silent years and their immediate aftermath.

And yet — here is the providence that runs under the whole era — the Roman world Herod served had become, without knowing it, the delivery system for the gospel. Consider what stood ready by the time of the Nativity. One empire meant one peace: the Pax Romana, Augustus's peace, kept the roads safe and the sea lanes open from Britain to Syria. Roman engineering had webbed the world with paved highways along which a tentmaker from Tarsus could walk from Jerusalem to Rome. Alexander's conquests, three centuries old, had left one common language — Greek — understood in every market town of the East, with the Scriptures already translated into it. Roman citizenship gave Paul legal protection in a dozen tight places. And a Roman census — Caesar Augustus's decree "that all the world should be registered" (Luke 2:1, ESV) — moved a carpenter and his pregnant wife from Nazareth to Bethlehem, so that the Son of David would be born in David's town, exactly where Micah had said, five centuries before, that He would be. Rome thought it was counting taxpayers. God was keeping a prophecy. When Paul wrote that Christ came in "the fullness of time," this is the fullness he meant: language, roads, peace, law, a translated Bible, a synagogue in every town, and a people groaning under a foreign yoke and aching for a deliverer. Every piece of it was assembled in the silent years. Every piece of it was assembled by God.

## 8. The Silence Breaks

The last promise of the Old Testament was the promise of a forerunner: Elijah would come, and turn the hearts of the fathers to the children. Malachi had said it twice over — in chapter 3, "Behold, I send my messenger, and he will prepare the way before me" (Malachi 3:1, ESV), and in the book's closing words, which we read at the start of this history. Four hundred years God's people waited for that messenger. The silence broke, fittingly, not in a palace or a marketplace but in the temple, at the hour of prayer, to an old priest named Zechariah who was burning incense while the people prayed outside — a man, Luke tells us, who had his own long silence to bear, for he and his wife Elizabeth were childless and advanced in years, and they had prayed about it for a very long time.

The angel Gabriel — the same Gabriel who had come to Daniel in the exile and shown him the shape of these very centuries — stood at the right side of the altar of incense, the first word from heaven in four hundred years:

> "But the angel said to him, 'Do not be afraid, Zechariah, for your prayer has been heard, and your wife Elizabeth will bear you a son, and you shall call his name John.'" — Luke 1:13 (ESV)

And Gabriel reached straight back across the silence and took hold of Malachi's promise and named it fulfilled: the child "will turn many of the children of Israel to the Lord their God, and he will go before him in the spirit and power of Elijah, to turn the hearts of the fathers to the children, and the disobedient to the wisdom of the just, to make ready for the Lord a people prepared" (Luke 1:16–17, ESV). Jesus Himself settled the identification beyond argument: of John the Baptist He said, "he is Elijah who is to come" (Matthew 11:14, ESV). The last word of the Old Testament and the first event of the New are the same promise — spoken, then kept. Between them stand four hundred years in which God said nothing and did everything.

That is the word this history leaves with you, and it is a word for anyone living in a quiet season of God's dealings — the addict who has prayed for years over a child and heard nothing, the man in the hospital bed watching the ceiling, the believer whose heaven feels like brass. The silence of God is not the absence of God. In the longest silence in the Bible, He was toppling Persia and raising Greece, translating His Book into the language of the world, building a pulpit in every town, paving the roads His apostles would walk, and counting down to a night in Bethlehem when the silence would end not with a whisper but with a sky full of angels. He had not forgotten His people. He had not forgotten His promise. He was filling the time to its fullness — and the God who kept the last promise of Malachi as the first event of Matthew is keeping His promises still, in your quiet, on His schedule, which has never once been late.

**A prayer.** Lord God, You are the God who speaks and the God who is silent, and You are faithful in both. We thank You for the four hundred years — for the translation prepared, the roads built, the people preserved through mad kings and failing kingdoms, and for the morning the silence broke in Your temple with good news for an old man who had almost stopped hoping. Teach us to trust You in our own quiet seasons. When heaven seems shut, remind us that You are filling the time, and that no promise You have made has ever fallen to the ground. Make ready our hearts, as You made ready the world, for the coming of Your Son. In Jesus' name, amen.

Christ Proclaimed. Jesus is Lord.
