Mark 8 — The Servant Confessed: Who Do You Say That I Am?

Jesus in Mark — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Gospel of Mark
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Mark 8 is the hinge of the whole Gospel. For eight chapters the question has been building — "Who then is this?" — and now Peter answers it: "You are the Christ." But no sooner is Jesus confessed than He begins to teach what His Messiahship means: not a throne seized by force, but a cross carried in obedience. Here Jesus reveals Himself as the Christ who must suffer, the Master who calls His followers to lose their lives in order to save them.

Commentary

The feeding of the four thousand (8:1–10). In those days, when again a great crowd has gathered, and they have nothing to eat, Jesus calls His disciples to Him and says to them, "I have compassion on the crowd, because they have been with me now three days and have nothing to eat. And if I send them away hungry to their homes, they will faint on the way. And some of them have come from far away." Splanchnizomai again — compassion from the gut. This crowd, likely largely Gentile in the Decapolis region, has stayed with Him three days. His disciples answer, "How can one feed these people with bread here in this desolate place?" They have seen Him feed five thousand, and still they ask. Mark is honest about the disciples' slowness because Peter was honest about it. And Jesus asks, "How many loaves do you have?" They say, "Seven." And He directs the crowd to sit down on the ground. And He takes the seven loaves, and having given thanks, He breaks them and gives them to His disciples to set before the people. They also have a few small fish. And having blessed them, He says that these also should be set before them. And they eat and are satisfied. And they take up the broken pieces left over, seven baskets full. And there are about four thousand people. And He sends them away and immediately gets into the boat with His disciples and goes to the district of Dalmanutha. Two feedings — one for Israel, one reaching the Gentiles — and baskets of abundance left over both times. The Shepherd feeds every flock that comes to Him.

The sign seekers and the leaven (8:11–21). The Pharisees come and begin to argue with Him, seeking from Him a sign from heaven to test Him. After all He has done — thousands fed, demons cast out, dead raised — they demand a sign. And He sighs deeply in His spirit, and says, "Why does this generation seek a sign? Truly, I say to you, no sign will be given to this generation." And He leaves them, gets into the boat again, and goes to the other side. A sign demanded in unbelief is a sign refused; faith is not built on spectacle.

Now the disciples have forgotten to bring bread, and they have only one loaf with them in the boat. And Jesus cautions them, saying, "Watch out; beware of the leaven of the Pharisees and the leaven of Herod." Leaven works silently and spreads through the whole lump — so does the unbelief of the Pharisees, who demand signs, and the worldliness of Herod, who heard gladly and killed anyway. But the disciples, thinking literally, discuss with one another the fact that they have no bread. And Jesus, aware of this, says to them, "Why are you discussing the fact that you have no bread? Do you not yet perceive or understand? Are your hearts hardened? Having eyes do you not see, and having ears do you not hear? And do you not remember? When I broke the five loaves for the five thousand, how many baskets full of broken pieces did you take up?" They say to Him, "Twelve." "And the seven for the four thousand, how many baskets full of broken pieces did you take up?" And they say to Him, "Seven." And He says to them, "Do you not yet understand?" Twelve baskets and seven baskets stand as permanent exhibits: the God who fed thousands from nothing will not strand His disciples over one loaf. Their problem is not provision. It is perception.

The blind man at Bethsaida (8:22–26). And they come to Bethsaida. And some people bring to Him a blind man and beg Him to touch him. And He takes the blind man by the hand and leads him out of the village, and when He has spit on his eyes and laid His hands on him, He asks him, "Do you see anything?" And he looks up and says, "I see people, but they look like trees, walking." Then Jesus lays His hands on his eyes again; and he opens his eyes, his sight is restored, and he sees everything clearly. And He sends him home, saying, "Do not even enter the village." This is the only gradual healing in the Gospels, and Mark places it here on purpose. It is a parable in action of the disciples themselves: they see Jesus, but dimly — men like trees, walking. Their sight will come in stages. First they will see that He is the Christ. Then, slowly and painfully, they will see what kind of Christ He is.

Peter's confession (8:27–30). And Jesus goes on with His disciples to the villages of Caesarea Philippi — far north, at the headwaters of the Jordan, a place of pagan shrines and imperial temples, where a marble temple to Caesar Augustus stood. Against that backdrop of worldly power, Jesus asks the question of the ages: "Who do people say that I am?" And they tell Him, "John the Baptist; and others say, Elijah; and others, one of the prophets." Good answers, honorable answers — and all of them wrong. And He asks them:

"But who do you say that I am?" — Mark 8:29 (ESV)

Peter answers Him:

"You are the Christ." — Mark 8:29 (ESV)

Christos — the Greek for the Hebrew Messiah, the Anointed One, the King promised to David, the One on whom the Spirit rested. Peter has it right. The demons knew it, the Father declared it, and now a man confesses it. And Jesus strictly charges them to tell no one about Him — not because it is false, but because the word "Christ" is loaded with expectations of conquest that must first be corrected by the cross. Before the world can hear who He is, His own disciples must learn what He came to do.

The first passion prediction (8:31–33). And He begins to teach them that the Son of Man must suffer many things and be rejected by the elders and the chief priests and the scribes and be killed, and after three days rise again. And He says this plainly. Four verbs, all under one great "must" — the divine dei, it is necessary. Suffer. Be rejected. Be killed. Rise again. This is not a tragedy overtaking Jesus; it is a plan unfolding through Him, written beforehand in the Scriptures — the suffering Servant of Isaiah 53, the pierced One of Psalm 22, the resurrection of Psalm 16:10. And Peter takes Him aside and begins to rebuke Him. Peter, who just confessed Him as Christ, now corrects Him as if he knew better. But turning and seeing His disciples, Jesus rebukes Peter and says, "Get behind me, Satan! For you are not setting your mind on the things of God, but on the things of man." The mouth that confessed the Christ can, one breath later, speak for Satan. Any gospel without a cross is Satan's gospel, no matter who preaches it.

The call to take up the cross (8:34–9:1). And calling the crowd to Him with His disciples, He says to them:

"If anyone would come after me, let him deny himself and take up his cross and follow me. For whoever would save his life will lose it, but whoever loses his life for my sake and the gospel's will save it." — Mark 8:34–35 (ESV)

The cross was not a piece of jewelry in that world. It was Rome's instrument of shameful execution, and every hearer knew what "take up his cross" meant: a condemned man carrying the beam on which he would die. Jesus is not calling for inconvenience. He is calling for death to self — denial of the old life, its claims, its comforts, its throne. And then the arithmetic of eternity:

"For what does it profit a man to gain the whole world and forfeit his soul? For what can a man give in return for his soul?" — Mark 8:36–37 (ESV)

A man can win everything the world sells and lose the only thing he cannot buy back. And Jesus closes with a warning and a promise: "For whoever is ashamed of me and of my words in this adulterous and sinful generation, of him will the Son of Man also be ashamed when he comes in the glory of his Father with the holy angels." And He says to them, "Truly, I say to you, there are some standing here who will not taste death until they see the kingdom of God after it has come with power" — a promise the next chapter begins to fulfill on the mountain, and that Pentecost will fulfill in full.

Key verses

"And he asked them, 'But who do you say that I am?' Peter answered him, 'You are the Christ.'" — Mark 8:29 (ESV)

"And he began to teach them that the Son of Man must suffer many things and be rejected by the elders and the chief priests and the scribes and be killed, and after three days rise again." — Mark 8:31 (ESV)

"If anyone would come after me, let him deny himself and take up his cross and follow me." — Mark 8:34 (ESV)

"For what does it profit a man to gain the whole world and forfeit his soul?" — Mark 8:36 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Mark 8 is that we want a Christ without a cross — we confess Him with our lips and then rebuke Him when His way costs us something. We are the disciples with eyes that do not see, trying to gain the world while our souls slip away. But the good news is that Jesus walks the road we refuse to walk. The Son of Man must suffer, be rejected, and be killed — for us — and after three days rise again. He loses His life so that those who lose theirs for His sake will save them.

A note from the Reformers. Spurgeon pressed Mark 8:36 on his London hearers again and again: a man may gain the whole world and lose his own soul, and there is no market where a lost soul can be bought back. It can only be ransomed — and not with silver or gold, but by Christ alone, solus Christus, who was already walking toward the cross where the price would be paid.

Reflection question: Jesus asks you personally: "Who do you say that I am?" Write down your answer in one sentence — and then ask yourself honestly whether you are setting your mind on the things of God or the things of man when His way leads toward a cross.
