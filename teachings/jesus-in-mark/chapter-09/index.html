Mark 9 — The Servant Transfigured: Glory on the Mountain, Faith in the Valley

Jesus in Mark — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Gospel of Mark
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Mark 9 swings between glory and struggle. On the mountain, Jesus is transfigured — His divine glory blazing through His humanity — and the Father's voice commands, "Listen to him." In the valley, He casts out a demon His disciples could not move, and He teaches that true greatness is servanthood and that the stakes of sin are eternal. Here Jesus reveals Himself as the glorious Son whose path still runs downhill, to the cross.

Commentary

The Transfiguration (9:2–8). And after six days Jesus takes with Him Peter and James and John, and leads them up a high mountain by themselves. And He is transfigured before them, and His clothes become radiant, intensely white, as no one on earth could bleach them. Mark's homely detail — no fuller, no launderer on earth could whiten like this — makes the glory more real, not less. This is the glory of the Son unveiled for one night: the same glory John saw when he wrote, "We have seen his glory, glory as of the only Son from the Father" (John 1:14). And there appear to them Elijah with Moses, and they are talking with Jesus. Moses, the lawgiver, and Elijah, the prophet — the Law and the Prophets together — talking with the One they both pointed to. Luke tells us they spoke of His departure, His exodus, which He was about to accomplish at Jerusalem: the cross is the subject of heaven's conversation.

And Peter says to Jesus, "Rabbi, it is good that we are here. Let us make three tents, one for you and one for Moses and one for Elijah." For he does not know what to say, for they are terrified. Peter wants to freeze the moment, to keep glory without going down to the cross. And a cloud overshadows them, and a voice comes out of the cloud:

"This is my beloved Son; listen to him." — Mark 9:7 (ESV)

At the baptism the Father spoke to Jesus; now He speaks about Jesus, to the disciples, and the command is singular: listen to Him. Moses and Elijah matter — but the Son is the final Word. And suddenly, looking around, they no longer see anyone with them but Jesus only. The Law and the Prophets step back, and Christ remains. That is the shape of all true faith.

Coming down the mountain (9:9–13). And as they are coming down the mountain, He charges them to tell no one what they have seen, until the Son of Man has risen from the dead. So they keep the matter to themselves, questioning what this rising from the dead might mean. The resurrection is so far outside their categories that even a plain statement of it confuses them. And they ask Him, "Why do the scribes say that first Elijah must come?" The scribes taught from Malachi 4:5 that Elijah would come before the day of the Lord. And Jesus says to them, "Elijah does come first to restore all things. And how is it written of the Son of Man that he should suffer many things and be treated with contempt? But I tell you that Elijah has come, and they did to him whatever they pleased, as it is written of him." Elijah has come — in John the Baptist, who came in Elijah's spirit and power — and Israel's leaders did to him whatever they pleased: a prison, a platter, a tomb. The forerunner's fate is the pattern of the Son's. Glory on the mountain does not cancel suffering in the valley; it confirms the One who will endure it.

The boy with the unclean spirit (9:14–29). And when they come to the disciples, they see a great crowd around them, and scribes arguing with them. And immediately all the crowd, when they see Him, are greatly amazed, and run up to Him and greet Him — perhaps a lingering radiance, like Moses' shining face, still marks Him. And He asks the scribes, "What are you arguing about with them?" And someone from the crowd answers Him, "Teacher, I brought my son to you, for he has a spirit that makes him mute. And whenever it seizes him, it throws him down, and he foams and grinds his teeth and becomes rigid. So I asked your disciples to cast it out, and they were not able." The disciples, who cast out many demons in chapter 6, meet one they cannot move. And Jesus answers them, "O faithless generation, how long am I to be with you? How long am I to bear with you? Bring him to me."

And they bring the boy to Him. And when the spirit sees Him, immediately it convulses the boy, and he falls on the ground and rolls about, foaming at the mouth. And Jesus asks his father, "How long has this been happening to him?" And he says, "From childhood. And it has often cast him into fire and into water, to destroy him. But if you can do anything, have compassion on us and help us." If you can — the father's faith is real but ragged, worn down by years of watching his son suffer. And Jesus says to him, "'If you can'! All things are possible for one who believes." Immediately the father of the child cries out and says:

"I believe; help my unbelief!" — Mark 9:24 (ESV)

It may be the most honest prayer in the Bible — faith and doubt in one breath, and Jesus receives it. He does not wait for perfect faith; He honors the faith that confesses its own weakness. And when Jesus sees that a crowd is coming running together, He rebukes the unclean spirit, saying to it, "You mute and deaf spirit, I command you, come out of him and never enter him again." And after crying out and convulsing him terribly, it comes out, and the boy is like a corpse, so that most of them say, "He is dead." But Jesus takes him by the hand and lifts him up, and he arises. It reads like a resurrection, because it is a preview of one. And when He has entered the house, His disciples ask Him privately, "Why could we not cast it out?" And He says to them, "This kind cannot be driven out by anything but prayer." Some strongholds do not yield to technique, reputation, or past success. They yield only to God, sought in prayer. The disciples had been given authority, but authority must be exercised in dependence, or it rusts.

The second passion prediction (9:30–32). They go on from there and pass through Galilee. And He does not want anyone to know, for He is teaching His disciples, saying to them, "The Son of Man is going to be delivered into the hands of men, and they will kill him. And when he is killed, after three days he will rise." The verb is "delivered" — handed over. Judas will hand Him over, the council will hand Him over, Pilate will hand Him over — and behind them all, the Father hands Him over for us all. But they do not understand the saying, and are afraid to ask Him. Mark does not flatter the apostles. They hear the clearest words and miss them, because a suffering Messiah has no shelf in their minds to sit on.

Who is the greatest? (9:33–37). And they come to Capernaum. And when He is in the house He asks them, "What were you discussing on the way?" But they keep silent, for on the way they had argued with one another about who was the greatest. The ink on the passion prediction is barely dry, and the apostles are competing for rank. And He sits down and calls the twelve. And He says to them:

"If anyone would be first, he must be last of all and servant of all." — Mark 9:35 (ESV)

And He takes a child and puts him in the midst of them, and taking him in His arms, He says to them, "Whoever receives one such child in my name receives me, and whoever receives me, receives not me but him who sent me." In that world a child had no status, no rights, nothing to offer a climber. Greatness in the kingdom is measured by whom you will stoop to receive. The way up is down; the throne is reached through the servant's door.

The outsider and the warnings (9:38–50). John says to Him, "Teacher, we saw someone casting out demons in your name, and we tried to stop him, because he was not following us." But Jesus says, "Do not stop him, for no one who does a mighty work in my name will be able soon afterward to speak evil of me. For the one who is not against us is for us. For truly, I say to you, whoever gives you a cup of water to drink because you belong to Christ will by no means lose his reward." The kingdom is bigger than our circle. Then the tone turns severe. "Whoever causes one of these little ones who believe in me to sin, it would be better for him if a great millstone were hung around his neck and he were thrown into the sea." And the warnings roll on: if your hand causes you to sin, cut it off; if your foot, cut it off; if your eye, tear it out — "it is better for you to enter life crippled than with two hands to go to hell, to the unquenchable fire." Hell here is Gehenna, the valley of Hinnom outside Jerusalem, where refuse burned continually and where, in dark days, children had been sacrificed to Molech. Jesus uses the most drastic language in the Bible about sin and hell, because He loves people too much to lie to them about the stakes. Radical surgery on sin — mortification, the Reformers called it — is not optional for the disciple. "For everyone will be salted with fire. Salt is good, but if the salt has lost its saltiness, how will you make it salty again? Have salt in yourselves, and be at peace with one another." The chapter that began with glory ends with the call to be purified, salty, and at peace — servants, not rivals.

Key verses

"And a cloud overshadowed them, and a voice came out of the cloud, 'This is my beloved Son; listen to him.'" — Mark 9:7 (ESV)

"Immediately the father of the child cried out and said, 'I believe; help my unbelief!'" — Mark 9:24 (ESV)

"If anyone would be first, he must be last of all and servant of all." — Mark 9:35 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Mark 9 is that even Christ's own disciples fail where faith is needed most — they cannot cast out the demon, cannot understand the cross, and argue about rank on the road to it. And sin is no small matter: Jesus speaks of hell's unquenchable fire with His own lips. But the good news is that Jesus receives the weakest faith honestly offered — "I believe; help my unbelief" is enough for Him — and the glorious Son who shone on the mountain came down into the valley to do for us what we could never do for ourselves.

A note from the Reformers. Luther cherished the father's cry in this chapter — "I believe; help my unbelief!" — as the truest picture of justifying faith. The faith that saves is not a faith that boasts of its own strength; it is a weak hand clinging to a strong Christ, confessing its weakness even as it clings. Faith alone justifies, but it is never a smug faith.

Reflection question: Where is your faith ragged right now — a child, a habit, a long unanswered prayer? Pray the father's prayer word for word today: "I believe; help my unbelief." What would it mean to trust that Jesus receives that prayer?
