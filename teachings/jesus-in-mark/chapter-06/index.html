Mark 6 — The Servant Rejected and Sending: Nazareth, the Twelve Sent Out, and the Feeding of the Five Thousand

Jesus in Mark — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Gospel of Mark
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Mark 6 shows the Servant rejected by His own hometown and yet sending out His apostles in power. It shows His compassion on shepherdless crowds, His provision for five thousand with five loaves, and His mastery over the sea in the dark of night. It also shows the cost of the kingdom: between the sending and the feeding stands the beheading of John the Baptist, the first martyr of the Gospel.

Commentary

Rejected at Nazareth (6:1–6). Jesus goes away from there and comes to His hometown, Nazareth, and His disciples follow Him. And on the Sabbath He begins to teach in the synagogue, and many who hear Him are astonished, saying, "Where did this man get these things? What is the wisdom given to him? How are such mighty works done by his hands? Is not this the carpenter, the son of Mary and brother of James and Joses and Judas and Simon? And are not his sisters here with us?" And they take offense at Him. Familiarity has bred contempt. They know His family, His trade, His childhood — and their knowledge becomes the very thing that blinds them. And Jesus says to them:

"A prophet is not without honor, except in his hometown and among his relatives and in his own household." — Mark 6:4 (ESV)

And He can do no mighty work there, except that He lays His hands on a few sick people and heals them. And He marvels because of their unbelief. Twice in the Gospels Jesus is said to marvel — here, at unbelief, and at the great faith of the Roman centurion. Unbelief is the one thing that limits the blessing of God among a people — not because Jesus lacks power, but because He will not force faith on those who refuse Him. And He goes about among the villages teaching.

The Twelve sent out (6:7–13). And He calls the twelve and begins to send them out two by two, and gives them authority over the unclean spirits. He charges them to take nothing for their journey except a staff — no bread, no bag, no money in their belts — but to wear sandals and not put on two tunics. They are to travel light and depend on the hospitality of those who receive them: "Whenever you enter a house, stay there until you depart from there." And if any place will not receive them and they will not listen, they are to shake off the dust from their feet when they leave, as a testimony against them — the gesture Jews used leaving Gentile territory, here turned as a solemn warning to Israel's own towns. So they go out and proclaim that people should repent. And they cast out many demons and anoint with oil many who are sick and heal them. The mission of Jesus is now multiplied through ordinary men. The apostles preach His message, do His works, and share His rejection.

The death of John the Baptist (6:14–29). King Herod — Herod Antipas, tetrarch of Galilee — hears of Jesus, for His name has become known. Some say, "John the Baptist has been raised from the dead. That is why these miraculous powers are at work in him." Others say He is Elijah; others, a prophet like one of the prophets of old. But when Herod hears of it, he says, "John, whom I beheaded, has been raised." Herod's conscience is haunted, and Mark now tells us why.

Herod had sent and seized John and bound him in prison for the sake of Herodias, his brother Philip's wife, because he had married her. For John had been saying to Herod, "It is not lawful for you to have your brother's wife" — Leviticus 18:16 and 20:21 stood against the marriage, and John, the last of the Old Testament prophets, would not trim the law of God to suit a king. And Herodias had a grudge against him and wanted to put him to death. But she could not, for Herod feared John, knowing that he was a righteous and holy man, and he kept him safe. When he heard him, he was greatly perplexed, and yet he heard him gladly. Herod is the picture of a divided man: conscience pulling one way, appetite the other.

But an opportunity comes when Herod on his birthday gives a banquet for his nobles and military commanders and the leading men of Galilee. The daughter of Herodias comes in and dances, and pleases Herod and his guests. And the king says to the girl, "Ask me for whatever you wish, and I will give it to you." And he vows with an oath, "Whatever you ask me, I will give you, up to half of my kingdom." She goes out and says to her mother, "What shall I ask?" And she says, "The head of John the Baptist." And she comes in immediately with haste to the king and asks, saying, "I want you to give me at once the head of John the Baptist on a platter." And the king is exceedingly sorry, but because of his oaths and his guests he does not want to break his word to her. And immediately the king sends an executioner with orders to bring John's head. He goes and beheads him in the prison and brings his head on a platter and gives it to the girl, and the girl gives it to her mother. When John's disciples hear of it, they come and take his body and lay it in a tomb.

Mark tells this story at length for a reason. John is the forerunner in death as in life: arrested, imprisoned, and killed because a king would not lose face — and his disciples bury him, just as Joseph of Arimathea will bury Jesus. The way of the cross is being walked before Jesus ever reaches it. And Herod, who heard John gladly and still killed him, is a warning to every hearer who enjoys the word but will not obey it.

The feeding of the five thousand (6:30–44). The apostles return to Jesus and tell Him all that they have done and taught. And He says to them, "Come away by yourselves to a desolate place and rest a while." For many are coming and going, and they have no leisure even to eat. Even the Son of God builds rest into the rhythm of ministry — a word for every exhausted servant. And they go away in the boat to a desolate place by themselves. Now many see them going and recognize them, and they run there on foot from all the towns and get there ahead of them. When He goes ashore He sees a great crowd, and He has compassion on them —

"When he went ashore he saw a great crowd, and he had compassion on them, because they were like sheep without a shepherd. And he began to teach them many things." — Mark 6:34 (ESV)

The Greek word for compassion here is splanchnizomai — the same deep, gut-level mercy we saw in chapter 1. Israel's leaders had failed the flock; Ezekiel 34 condemns the shepherds who fed themselves instead of the sheep, and promises that God Himself will come and shepherd His people. Here He is, ashore, teaching them. And His compassion does not stop at teaching. When it grows late, His disciples come to Him and say, "This is a desolate place, and the hour is now late. Send them away to go into the surrounding countryside and villages and buy themselves something to eat." But He answers them, "You give them something to eat." And they say to Him, "Shall we go and buy two hundred denarii worth of bread and give it to them to eat?" — two hundred denarii being the better part of a working man's year. And He says to them, "How many loaves do you have? Go and see." And when they have found out, they say, "Five, and two fish."

And He commands them all to sit down in groups on the green grass — Mark remembers the green grass; it was spring, near Passover. So they sit down in groups, by hundreds and by fifties, like an army in ranks, like Israel in the wilderness. And taking the five loaves and the two fish, He looks up to heaven and says a blessing and breaks the loaves and gives them to the disciples to set before the people. And He divides the two fish among them all. And they all eat and are satisfied. And they take up twelve baskets full of broken pieces and of the fish. And those who eat the loaves are five thousand men — besides women and children, as Matthew tells us. Five loaves become a feast, and the fragments gathered are more than the meal they started with. In the hands of Jesus, little is much, and the Shepherd feeds His flock in the wilderness just as God fed Israel with manna.

Walking on the water (6:45–52). Immediately He makes His disciples get into the boat and go before Him to the other side, to Bethsaida, while He dismisses the crowd. And after He has taken leave of them, He goes up on the mountain to pray. And when evening comes, the boat is out on the sea, and He is alone on the land. And He sees that they are making headway painfully, for the wind is against them. And about the fourth watch of the night — between three and six in the morning — He comes to them, walking on the sea. He means to pass by them — Mark's phrase echoes the Old Testament theophanies, where the Lord "passes by" His servants, as He passed by Moses in Exodus 33 and Elijah in 1 Kings 19. But when they see Him walking on the sea, they think it is a ghost, and cry out, for they all see Him and are terrified. But immediately He talks with them and says to them:

"Take heart; it is I. Do not be afraid." — Mark 6:50 (ESV)

"It is I" — in Greek, egō eimi, the words of God's own name, "I AM." And He gets into the boat with them, and the wind ceases. And they are utterly astounded, for they do not understand about the loaves, but their hearts are hardened. Even the apostles are slow to see. The God who multiplied bread in the wilderness is the God who walks on the sea, and His presence in the boat is enough: the wind stops when He steps in.

Healings at Gennesaret (6:53–56). When they have crossed over, they come to land at Gennesaret and moor to the shore. And when they get out of the boat, the people immediately recognize Him and run about the whole region and begin to bring the sick people on their beds to wherever they hear He is. And wherever He comes, in villages, cities, or countryside, they lay the sick in the marketplaces and implore Him that they may touch even the fringe of His garment. And as many as touch it are made well. The chapter that began with rejection at Nazareth ends with faith in Gennesaret: even the fringe of His garment carries healing to those who reach for Him in faith.

Key verses

"A prophet is not without honor, except in his hometown and among his relatives and in his own household." — Mark 6:4 (ESV)

"When he went ashore he saw a great crowd, and he had compassion on them, because they were like sheep without a shepherd. And he began to teach them many things." — Mark 6:34 (ESV)

"Take heart; it is I. Do not be afraid." — Mark 6:50 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Mark 6 is that familiarity can blind us to Jesus, conscience can be outvoted by appetite like Herod's, and even apostles row against the wind with hardened hearts. The people who knew Jesus longest believed in Him least, and the king who heard the prophet gladly still handed over his head. But the good news is that the Shepherd's compassion does not depend on our deserving. He feeds the crowd that interrupted His rest, He comes to His disciples across the water in the fourth watch of the night, and His word still stands over every contrary wind: "Take heart; it is I. Do not be afraid."

A note from the Reformers. Spurgeon loved to point out that in this chapter the same heart does both works: the Lord who commands His tired disciples to rest is the Lord who cannot turn away a hungry crowd. The Servant serves tired men as gladly as hungry ones — and He still does. Rest in Him is not laziness; it is part of His care for His workers.

Reflection question: Herod heard John gladly but never obeyed him. Is there a word from God that you enjoy hearing but have not yet obeyed — and what would obedience look like this week?
