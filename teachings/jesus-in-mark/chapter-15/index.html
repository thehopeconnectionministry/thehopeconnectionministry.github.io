Mark 15 — The Servant Crucified: The King on the Cross

Jesus in Mark — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Gospel of Mark
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Mark 15 is the darkest chapter in the Gospel and the center of the whole Bible's story. Jesus stands before Pilate, is exchanged for a murderer, is mocked as a king by the soldiers He came to save, and is crucified between two robbers. He dies with the cry of Psalm 22 on His lips, the temple curtain tears from top to bottom, and a Roman centurion makes the confession the whole Gospel has been building toward. Here Jesus reveals Himself as the crucified King, the forsaken Substitute, and — in the centurion's words — truly the Son of God.

Commentary

Before Pilate (15:1–15). And as soon as it is morning, the chief priests hold a consultation with the elders and scribes and the whole council. And they bind Jesus and lead Him away and deliver Him over to Pilate. The Sanhedrin can condemn, but only Rome can execute, and the charge must change costumes on the way: "blasphemy" means nothing to a Roman governor, so the charge becomes treason — a king against Caesar. And Pilate asks Him, "Are you the King of the Jews?" And He answers him, "You have said so." It is neither a denial nor a rallying cry: yes, He is the King — but a King whose kingdom is not of this world, as John records Him explaining. And the chief priests accuse Him of many things. And Pilate again asks Him, "Have you no answer to make? See how many charges they bring against you." But Jesus makes no further answer, so that Pilate is amazed. The silence of Isaiah 53:7 again: "like a lamb that is led to the slaughter... he opened not his mouth."

Now at the feast Pilate used to release for them one prisoner whom they asked. And among the rebels in prison, who had committed murder in the insurrection, there was a man called Barabbas. And the crowd comes up and begins to ask Pilate to do as he usually does for them. And he answers them, saying, "Do you want me to release to you the King of the Jews?" For he perceives that it is out of envy that the chief priests have delivered Him up. Pilate knows. Mark makes sure we know he knows. But the chief priests stir up the crowd to have him release Barabbas for them instead. And Pilate again says to them, "Then what shall I do with the man you call the King of the Jews?" And they cry out again, "Crucify him." And Pilate says to them, "Why? What evil has he done?" But they shout all the more, "Crucify him." So Pilate, wishing to satisfy the crowd, releases for them Barabbas, and having scourged Jesus, he delivers Him to be crucified.

Stand still at the exchange, because it is the gospel in a tableau. Barabbas — his name means "son of the father" — is a murderer and a rebel, guilty and condemned. Jesus is the true Son of the Father, innocent and condemned. The guilty man walks free because the innocent man takes his place. That is substitution, acted out in a Jerusalem street before it is accomplished on the cross: the sinner released, the Savior delivered over. And Pilate, who three times in the other Gospels declares Jesus innocent, delivers Him anyway — "wishing to satisfy the crowd." It is the portrait of every man who knows what is right and does what is safe.

The soldiers mock the King (15:16–20). And the soldiers lead Him away inside the palace (that is, the governor's headquarters), and they call together the whole battalion. And they clothe Him in a purple cloak, and twisting together a crown of thorns, they put it on Him. And they begin to salute Him, "Hail, King of the Jews!" And they strike His head with a reed and spit on Him and kneel down in homage to Him. Every gesture is mockery — and every gesture is truer than they know. He is the King of the Jews. The crown of thorns preaches: the curse of Genesis 3, thorns and thistles, is being pressed into the brow of the second Adam who came to bear it. And when they have mocked Him, they strip Him of the purple cloak and put His own clothes on Him. And they lead Him out to crucify Him. The third prediction is fulfilled to its last item: mocked, spit upon, flogged — and now, killed.

The crucifixion (15:21–32). And they compel a passerby, Simon of Cyrene, who is coming in from the country, the father of Alexander and Rufus, to carry His cross. Mark names the sons — Alexander and Rufus were evidently known to the church in Rome (Paul greets a Rufus in Romans 16:13), men whose father carried the crossbeam of the Lord when His own strength, spent by scourging, could not. Simon came to watch a Passover and ended up under the cross: a picture of discipleship itself, for Jesus had said, "Take up your cross and follow me." And they bring Him to the place called Golgotha (which means Place of a Skull). And they offer Him wine mixed with myrrh, but He does not take it — a drugged drink, offered in mercy to dull the pain; Jesus refuses it, and will drink the cup His Father gave Him with His mind clear to the end. And they crucify Him and divide His garments among them, casting lots for them, to decide what each should take — Psalm 22:18, fulfilled by soldiers gambling at the foot of the cross, unaware they are acting out Scripture. And it is the third hour — nine in the morning — when they crucify Him. And the inscription of the charge against Him reads, "The King of the Jews." Pilate's placard, meant as Rome's sneer, stands in Mark as heaven's headline: this is the charge, and the charge is true. And with Him they crucify two robbers, one on His right and one on His left — Isaiah 53:12, "numbered with the transgressors," and a grim echo of James and John, who asked for the right hand and the left in His glory. This is what the places at His right and left in His kingdom turned out to be.

And those who pass by deride Him, wagging their heads and saying, "Aha! You who would destroy the temple and rebuild it in three days, save yourself, and come down from the cross!" So also the chief priests with the scribes mock Him to one another, saying, "He saved others; he cannot save himself. Let the Christ, the King of Israel, come down now from the cross that we may see and believe." They speak better theology than they know: He saved others; He cannot save Himself — not because He lacks the power, but because saving others is precisely why He will not save Himself. If He comes down, we are lost. Those who are crucified with Him also revile Him.

Darkness, the cry, and the death (15:33–41). And when the sixth hour has come — noon — there is darkness over the whole land until the ninth hour. Creation itself goes dark at midday: Amos 8:9, "I will make the sun go down at noon and darken the earth in broad daylight," on the day of the Lord. This is judgment weather. And at the ninth hour Jesus cries with a loud voice:

"Eloi, Eloi, lema sabachthani?" which means, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" — Mark 15:34 (ESV)

Mark keeps the Aramaic, as with Talitha cumi and Abba, because these are the words Peter never forgot. They are the opening words of Psalm 22 — the psalm of the righteous sufferer that ends in vindication and worldwide praise, and Jesus, who knows the whole psalm, takes its darkest line onto His own lips. This is the forsakenness at the heart of the gospel: the Son, who had known nothing but the Father's love from eternity, bears in our place the judgment our sins deserve — God forsaken by God, for us. Luther marveled at this cry all his life; he said that here the highest article of faith stands naked: Christ, made a curse for us (Galatians 3:13), tasting the abandonment that was ours, so that it would never be ours again. And some of the bystanders, hearing it, say, "Behold, he is calling Elijah." And someone runs and fills a sponge with sour wine, puts it on a reed and gives it to Him to drink, saying, "Wait, let us see whether Elijah will come to take him down." And Jesus utters a loud cry and breathes His last. A loud cry — at the moment of death, when crucified men died of suffocation and exhaustion in whispers. John tells us what the cry said: "It is finished." He does not fade; He dismisses His spirit, laying down His life of Himself (John 10:18).

And the curtain of the temple is torn in two, from top to bottom. The curtain that barred the Holy of Holies, embroidered and massive, separating sinners from the presence of God — torn, and torn from the top: heaven's hand does this, not man's. The Greek verb is schizō — the same word Mark used in chapter 1 when the heavens were torn open at Jesus' baptism. The Gospel is bookended by tearing: heaven torn open to send the Son down, the curtain torn open to bring sinners in. The way into the presence of God is open, because the forsaken One has borne the judgment outside the camp (Hebrews 10:19–20; 13:12). And when the centurion, who stands facing Him, sees that in this way He breathes His last, he says:

"Truly this man was the Son of God!" — Mark 15:39 (ESV)

The first human being in Mark's Gospel to call Jesus the Son of God is a Roman soldier, a Gentile, an executioner — standing in the blood of his work, looking at a corpse. The priests could not see it in the temple; the disciples could not yet say it at the cross; the centurion sees it in a dead man on a Roman cross. This is Mark's whole argument arriving: the Son of God is not recognized in spite of the cross but at it. Soli Deo gloria — glory to God alone, spoken first by the least likely man in the world. There were also women looking on from a distance, among whom were Mary Magdalene, and Mary the mother of James the younger and of Joses, and Salome. When He was in Galilee, they followed Him and ministered to Him. And there were also many other women who came up with Him to Jerusalem. The men fled; the women stayed — at a distance, but they stayed, and their names are in the record forever.

The burial (15:42–47). And when evening has come, since it is the day of Preparation, that is, the day before the Sabbath, Joseph of Arimathea, a respected member of the council, who is also himself looking for the kingdom of God, takes courage and goes to Pilate and asks for the body of Jesus. A member of the very council that condemned Him — but Mark tells us he was looking for the kingdom, and John tells us he was a disciple, secretly, for fear. The cross ends his secrecy: grief and love make him bold at exactly the moment when boldness costs the most. Pilate is surprised to hear that He is already dead. And summoning the centurion, he asks him whether He is already dead. And when he learns from the centurion that He is dead, he grants the corpse to Joseph. Let the record be plain, because the resurrection will depend on it: Jesus truly died, certified by the officer in charge of the execution, before witnesses. And Joseph buys a linen shroud, and taking Him down, wraps Him in the linen shroud and lays Him in a tomb that has been cut out of the rock. And he rolls a stone against the entrance of the tomb. Isaiah 53:9 — "with a rich man in his death" — fulfilled by a borrowed tomb and a rich man's courage. Mary Magdalene and Mary the mother of Joses see where He is laid. The women mark the place. They will be back at sunrise.

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Mark 15 is the worst news in the world, and we are not bystanders in it: it is our sin that stands in this chapter — in the crowd crying "Crucify," in Pilate washing his hands of the truth, in the priests' envy, in the disciples' absence. The judgment that fell in darkness at noon was the judgment our sins had earned, and the forsakenness Jesus cried out under was the forsakenness that belonged to us. But the good news is the best news in the world: He bore it in our place. Barabbas walked free; so do we. The curtain is torn from top to bottom; the way to God stands open. And the first confession of the finished work comes from the executioner himself: truly, this man was — and is — the Son of God.

A note from the Reformers. This chapter is why the Reformers would not budge on solus Christus and sola gratia. Nothing is added to this cross — no disciple stands helping, no ritual completes it, no merit supplements it. Calvin called the cross the tribunal where God's justice and mercy meet. Luther simply pointed sinners to it: look at Christ bearing what you deserved, and let your conscience rest there and nowhere else.

Reflection question: The centurion confessed Jesus at the cross, not at a miracle. What does the cross show you about who Jesus is that nothing else could? And like Barabbas, will you simply walk free — receiving the exchange — instead of trying to add something of your own to it?
