Mark 10 — The Servant Gives His Life: The Road to Jerusalem

Jesus in Mark — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Gospel of Mark
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Mark 10 is the chapter of the ransom. Jesus is on the road to Jerusalem now, teaching on marriage, blessing children, confronting a rich young man, predicting His death a third time, and answering the ambition of James and John with the key verse of the whole Gospel. Here Jesus reveals Himself as the Servant who came not to be served but to serve — and to give His life as a ransom for many.

Commentary

The question about divorce (10:1–12). And He left there and went to the region of Judea and beyond the Jordan, and crowds gathered to Him again. And again, as was His custom, He taught them. And Pharisees came up and in order to test Him asked, "Is it lawful for a man to divorce his wife?" The question is a trap with Herod's shadow on it: John the Baptist was beheaded for speaking about unlawful marriage in this very region. Jesus answers with a question: "What did Moses command you?" They say, "Moses allowed a man to write a certificate of divorce and to send her away," pointing to Deuteronomy 24:1. And Jesus says to them, "Because of your hardness of heart he wrote you this commandment. But from the beginning of creation, 'God made them male and female.' 'Therefore a man shall leave his father and mother and hold fast to his wife, and the two shall become one flesh.' So they are no longer two but one flesh. What therefore God has joined together, let not man separate."

Jesus goes behind Moses' concession to God's creation. Genesis 1:27 and Genesis 2:24 are His authorities: marriage is not a human contract to be dissolved at will but a union God Himself joins — one flesh, male and female, from the beginning. The permission in Deuteronomy was an accommodation to hardness of heart, not the design of God. And in the house, the disciples ask Him again about this matter, and He says to them, "Whoever divorces his wife and marries another commits adultery against her, and if she divorces her husband and marries another, she commits adultery." The words are hard because the covenant is holy. Marriage pictures Christ and the church; it is not to be entered lightly or left lightly.

Let the children come (10:13–16). And they are bringing children to Him that He might touch them, and the disciples rebuke them. But when Jesus sees it, He is indignant — one of the strongest emotional words Mark uses of Him, and it is aimed at His own disciples — and says to them:

"Let the children come to me; do not hinder them, for to such belongs the kingdom of God. Truly, I say to you, whoever does not receive the kingdom of God like a child shall not enter it." — Mark 10:14–15 (ESV)

A child does not earn, negotiate, or deserve. A child receives — empty-handed, dependent, trusting. That is the only way anyone enters the kingdom, from the least to the greatest. And He takes them in His arms and blesses them, laying His hands on them. The Servant who is marching to His death has arms and time for children.

The rich young man (10:17–31). And as He is setting out on His journey, a man runs up and knelt before Him and asks Him, "Good Teacher, what must I do to inherit eternal life?" Everything about the man looks promising: he runs, he kneels, he is sincere, he is moral, he is rich. And Jesus says to him, "Why do you call me good? No one is good except God alone." Not a denial of His own goodness, but a probe: do you know who you are talking to? "You know the commandments: 'Do not murder, Do not commit adultery, Do not steal, Do not bear false witness, Do not defraud, Honor your father and mother.'" The man answers, "Teacher, all these I have kept from my youth." And Jesus, looking at him, loves him — Mark's tender note; this searching word comes from a heart of love — and says to him, "You lack one thing: go, sell all that you have and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven; and come, follow me." Disheartened by the saying, he goes away sorrowful, for he has great possessions.

The one thing he lacks is the one thing he will not give up. His wealth is not merely something he owns; it owns him — it is his god, and the first commandment stands against it. Jesus does not soften the word, because a surgeon who will not cut cannot heal. And Jesus looks around and says to His disciples, "How difficult it will be for those who have wealth to enter the kingdom of God!" And the disciples are amazed at His words. But Jesus says to them again, "Children, how difficult it is to enter the kingdom of God! It is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle than for a rich person to enter the kingdom of God." The largest animal in Palestine through the smallest opening in a household — a picture of sheer impossibility. And they are exceedingly astonished, and say to Him, "Then who can be saved?" Jesus looks at them and says:

"With man it is impossible, but not with God. For all things are possible with God." — Mark 10:27 (ESV)

That is the gospel of this story. Salvation is impossible for man — rich or poor, moral or ruined — and entirely possible for God. Peter begins to say to Him, "See, we have left everything and followed you." And Jesus says, "Truly, I say to you, there is no one who has left house or brothers or sisters or mother or father or children or lands, for my sake and for the gospel, who will not receive a hundredfold now in this time, houses and brothers and sisters and mothers and children and lands, with persecutions, and in the age to come eternal life. But many who are first will be last, and the last first." Whatever is surrendered for Christ is repaid a hundredfold in the family of God — with persecutions, He honestly adds — and eternal life in the age to come.

The third passion prediction (10:32–34). And they are on the road, going up to Jerusalem, and Jesus walks ahead of them. And they are amazed, and those who follow are afraid. The Servant strides ahead of His followers toward the city that kills the prophets, and even His pace frightens them. And taking the twelve again, He begins to tell them what is to happen to Him, saying, "See, we are going up to Jerusalem, and the Son of Man will be delivered over to the chief priests and the scribes, and they will condemn him to death and deliver him over to the Gentiles. And they will mock him and spit on him, and flog him and kill him. And after three days he will rise." Mock, spit, flog, kill — every detail is named beforehand, and every detail will happen exactly as He says. This is not a man overtaken by events. This is the Shepherd laying down His life of His own accord, on schedule.

The request of James and John (10:35–45). And James and John, the sons of Zebedee, come up to Him and say to Him, "Teacher, we want you to do for us whatever we ask of you." Even after the third prediction, ambition is alive in the apostles. And He says to them, "What do you want me to do for you?" And they say to Him, "Grant us to sit, one at your right hand and one at your left, in your glory." And Jesus says to them, "You do not know what you are asking. Are you able to drink the cup that I drink, or to be baptized with the baptism with which I am baptized?" The cup is the cup of God's wrath against sin, the baptism the flood of judgment He will go under. And they say to Him, "We are able." And Jesus says to them, "The cup that I drink you will drink, and with the baptism with which I am baptized, you will be baptized, but to sit at my right hand or at my left is not mine to grant, but it is for those for whom it has been prepared." James will indeed drink the cup — the first apostle martyred, in Acts 12 — and John will suffer exile; but the thrones belong to the Father's appointment. And when the ten hear it, they begin to be indignant at James and John — not because they are humbler, but because they wanted the seats themselves. And Jesus calls them to Him and says to them, "You know that those who are considered rulers of the Gentiles lord it over them, and their great ones exercise authority over them. But it shall not be so among you. But whoever would be great among you must be your servant, and whoever would be first among you must be slave of all." And then the key verse of Mark's Gospel, the sentence the whole book leans on:

"For even the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many." — Mark 10:45 (ESV)

Ransom — in Greek, lytron — is the price paid to free a slave or release a captive. Jesus does not say He came to show us how to live, though He does. He says He came to die — and that His death is a payment, a purchase, a price that sets many free. This is substitution: the Servant gives His life for many, in their place. Isaiah 53 said it centuries before: "He was pierced for our transgressions... the Lord has laid on him the iniquity of us all." Mark 10:45 is where the Gospel tells us plainly why the cross had to happen.

Blind Bartimaeus (10:46–52). And they come to Jericho. And as He is leaving Jericho with His disciples and a great crowd, Bartimaeus, a blind beggar, the son of Timaeus, is sitting by the roadside. And when he hears that it is Jesus of Nazareth, he begins to cry out and say, "Jesus, Son of David, have mercy on me!" Son of David — a messianic title on a beggar's lips; the blind man sees what the seeing crowds miss. And many rebuke him, telling him to be silent. But he cries out all the more, "Son of David, have mercy on me!" And Jesus stops and says, "Call him." And they call the blind man, saying to him, "Take heart. Get up; he is calling you." And throwing off his cloak, he springs up and comes to Jesus. The cloak was his livelihood — spread to catch coins — and he throws it aside without a thought. And Jesus says to him, "What do you want me to do for you?" And the blind man says to Him, "Rabbi, let me recover my sight." And Jesus says to him:

"Go your way; your faith has made you well." — Mark 10:52 (ESV)

And immediately he recovers his sight and follows Him on the way. James and John asked for thrones; Bartimaeus asked for mercy — and mercy is what the Servant came to give. The chapter ends with a healed blind man following Jesus on the road to Jerusalem: the picture of every disciple. We were blind; He opened our eyes; now we follow Him on the way.

Key verses

"Let the children come to me; do not hinder them, for to such belongs the kingdom of God." — Mark 10:14 (ESV)

"With man it is impossible, but not with God. For all things are possible with God." — Mark 10:27 (ESV)

"For even the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many." — Mark 10:45 (ESV)

"And Jesus said to him, 'Go your way; your faith has made you well.' And immediately he recovered his sight and followed him on the way." — Mark 10:52 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Mark 10 is that we are the rich young man more often than we admit — there is a "one thing" we will not surrender, and with man, salvation is impossible. We cannot buy heaven, earn heaven, or climb to heaven; the camel will not fit through the needle's eye. But the good news is the heart of this Gospel: what is impossible with man is possible with God, because the Son of Man came to do the impossible Himself — to serve, and to give His life as a ransom for many. We are not asked to pay the price. We are told the price has been paid.

A note from the Reformers. Luther called the heart of this chapter the "joyful exchange": Christ takes what is ours — our sin, our death, our curse — and gives us what is His — His righteousness, His life, His blessing. Mark 10:45 is that exchange in a single sentence. His life, given as a ransom; our freedom, received as a gift. Grace alone, Christ alone, from beginning to end.

Reflection question: What is your "one thing" — the possession, habit, grudge, or comfort that Jesus keeps putting His finger on? What would it look like to sell it, surrender it, and follow Him today?
