Mark 16 — The Servant Risen: He Is Not Here

Jesus in Mark — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Gospel of Mark
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Mark 16 opens on the first Easter morning in history, and everything turns on three words: "He has risen." The women who watched Him die and saw where He was laid come to the tomb at sunrise and find the stone rolled away and a young man in white announcing the impossible. Here Jesus reveals Himself as the risen Son of God — vindicated by the Father, gone before His disciples into Galilee as He promised, and still, wonderfully, gathering failures like Peter back to Himself.

Commentary

The women at the tomb (16:1–4). When the Sabbath is past, Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James, and Salome buy spices, so that they may go and anoint Him. The Sabbath rest is over at sundown Saturday; they buy their spices that evening and wait for daylight. These are the same women who watched from a distance at the cross and saw where He was laid — Mark has kept their names in front of us for three scenes running, because they are the witnesses. And very early on the first day of the week, when the sun has risen, they go to the tomb. The first day of the week — Sunday — will never be ordinary again. And they are saying to one another, "Who will roll away the stone for us from the entrance of the tomb?" The stone is their one practical worry, and it shows the measure of their faith at that hour: they expect a corpse, a heavy stone, and a sad duty. Love brought them; resurrection faith did not — not yet. And looking up, they see that the stone has been rolled back — it is very large. God has answered the question they were asking each other before they finished asking it. How often He does.

The announcement (16:5–7). And entering the tomb, they see a young man sitting on the right side, dressed in a white robe, and they are alarmed. Matthew and Luke fill out the picture: this is an angel of the Lord. And he says to them:

"Do not be alarmed. You seek Jesus of Nazareth, who was crucified. He has risen; he is not here. See the place where they laid him." — Mark 16:6 (ESV)

Every clause is loaded. Do not be alarmed — heaven's first word to trembling people. You seek Jesus of Nazareth — the angel names Him by His hometown, the place that rejected Him. Who was crucified — the resurrection does not erase the cross; the risen One is forever the crucified One. He has risen — in Greek, ēgerthē, a passive: He was raised. The resurrection is the Father's act, heaven's verdict on the Son's finished work, the great reversal of the Sanhedrin's sentence and Pilate's cross. He is not here — the tomb is a place to visit, not a place to worship; Christianity has no corpse at its center. See the place where they laid him — the evidence is offered for inspection. The Christian faith does not ask anyone to believe against the evidence; it invites them to look at the empty place.

"But go, tell his disciples and Peter that he is going before you to Galilee. There you will see him, just as he told you." — Mark 16:7 (ESV)

And Peter. Two words, and Spurgeon built a whole sermon on them — rightly. Why is Peter singled out? Not because he stood tallest; because he fell hardest. Three denials, with oaths, by a fire, three nights ago. If the message had been "tell his disciples," Peter might have spent the rest of his life wondering whether he was still counted among them. So heaven names him. The risen Christ counts the denier by name, calls him back by name, and appoints the meeting — in Galilee, where it all began, where Jesus had promised in Mark 14:28, "After I am raised up, I will go before you to Galilee." He is going before them — the Shepherd ahead of the sheep again, leading them back to the place of their first calling, to start again. Grace does not merely forgive the past; it reopens the future.

The women's fear (16:8). And they go out and flee from the tomb, for trembling and astonishment have seized them, and they say nothing to anyone, for they are afraid. And in the earliest manuscripts, that is where Mark ends — mid-breath, with an empty tomb, a promised meeting, and three frightened women running into the sunrise. It is a startling ending, and Mark's readers have always felt it was meant to startle. The whole Gospel has been building the question — Who then is this? — and Mark ends by handing the question to the reader. The disciples failed and were named and restored. The women are afraid and silent — for now; the other Gospels tell us their silence broke into proclamation before the morning was out. But Mark stops here, and the unfinished sentence becomes an invitation: the story is true, the tomb is empty, the Lord is risen and going before you. What will you do with it? Every reader must finish the chapter with his own feet — by fleeing in fear, or by going to Galilee to meet the risen Christ.

A note on Mark 16:9–20. Honesty requires a brief word here, and there is no cause for alarm in it. The two oldest complete manuscripts of Mark (Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus, from the fourth century) end the Gospel at verse 8, and some early church writers noted that the longer ending was absent from many copies of their day. Verses 9–20 — the appearances to Mary Magdalene and the two travelers, the Great Commission, and the ascension — appear in the great majority of later manuscripts and were known very early; Irenaeus quoted verse 19 as Mark's in the second century. Nothing in those verses teaches anything not taught plainly elsewhere in Scripture: the appearances are in Matthew, Luke, and John; the commission is in Matthew 28 and Acts 1; the ascension is in Luke and Acts. The church has received these verses for centuries and may read them with profit, while knowing the manuscript history honestly. The resurrection itself does not rest on the disputed verses at all — it stands in verse 6, in every manuscript on earth: "He has risen; he is not here." Our faith is in the risen Christ, not in any single paragraph, and the evidence for the empty tomb is exactly where Mark left it: unshaken.

Key verses

"Do not be alarmed. You seek Jesus of Nazareth, who was crucified. He has risen; he is not here. See the place where they laid him." — Mark 16:6 (ESV)

"But go, tell his disciples and Peter that he is going before you to Galilee. There you will see him, just as he told you." — Mark 16:7 (ESV)

"And they went out and fled from the tomb, for trembling and astonishment had seized them, and they said nothing to anyone, for they were afraid." — Mark 16:8 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Mark 16 is that even the most faithful people in the story — the women who never left Him — came to the tomb expecting nothing but death, and fled from the best news in the world in fear. Left to ourselves, we do not rise to the resurrection; we run from it, trembling. But the good news is that the resurrection does not depend on our courage. He has risen — whether the women could yet speak of it or not. He goes before His disciples into Galilee — before the denier, before the scattered, before the afraid. The risen Servant-King comes looking for His failures, names them one by one — and Peter, and you — and gives them a meeting place. That is grace alone, from the first verse of this Gospel to the last.

A note from the Reformers. Spurgeon preached on the two words "and Peter" with tears in his voice, and called them among the sweetest words in the Bible for a backslider: the risen Lord does not merely forgive His people in bulk — He names the fallen one personally, lest that one soul should think himself excepted from the mercy. If you take one sentence from this commentary into a hard night, take that one: the message still runs, "tell his disciples — and Peter."

Reflection question: The risen Jesus names Peter personally after his worst failure. What failure of yours do you most need to hear Him name-and-restore today — and will you meet Him where He has promised to be, in His Word, instead of hiding from Him?

Closing — Mark's Gospel in One Sentence

Sixteen chapters, and they can be carried in a single sentence:

Jesus Christ, the Son of God, came not to be served but to serve, and gave His life as a ransom for many — and the Father, having raised Him from the dead, has vindicated the suffering Servant as the Son of God and King of all.

In the "Jesus in Every Book of the Bible" walkthrough, this is Mark's testimony: Jesus in Mark is the Servant who suffers and the Son of God vindicated. Other Gospels linger on His teaching discourses and His birth; Mark shows us His hands — stretched out to lepers, broken over bread, pierced on the cross — and His feet, always moving: into Galilee, into Gentile country, up to Jerusalem, out of the tomb, and on ahead of His disciples into Galilee. The walkthrough of Scripture is one long road, and Mark's stretch of it runs from a torn-open heaven at the Jordan to a torn-open curtain at the cross to an opened tomb at sunrise. At every step the same Christ is revealed: the Servant-King who suffers, who serves, and who saves.

If you have walked through this commentary chapter by chapter, you have heard the five watchwords of the Reformers sound on every page, because they are Mark's watchwords first: Scripture alone, as the written Word frames every scene; grace alone, as mercy reaches lepers, demoniacs, foreigners, and failures who earned none of it; faith alone, as empty hands — a woman's touch, a father's cry, a beggar's shout — receive what they could never buy; Christ alone, as the ransom is paid by no other hand than His; and glory to God alone, as a Roman centurion gives heaven its verdict at the foot of the cross. Hold those five, keep the law and the gospel distinct, and you will read not only Mark but the whole Bible the way it asks to be read.

The last word belongs to the Lord's own promise. He is going before you — into your Galilee, your ordinary work, your hometown, your next hard week. There you will see Him, just as He told you.

A Closing Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God and Servant of all,

We thank You that You came — not to be served, but to serve, and to give Your life as a ransom for many. We thank You for every step of the road: for the touch that cleansed the leper, for the word that stilled the sea, for the faith You honored in the desperate and the outcast, for the cup You drank in the garden when we would have slept, and for the cross where You bore what we deserved, forsaken so that we would never be forsaken.

We confess that we are Peters and sleepers and runners — that we have denied You in small ways and fled from You in fear. Thank You that Your message still names us: and Peter — and us. Raise our hearts with Your resurrection. Teach us to stay awake, to take up our cross, to be last of all and servant of all, and to tell our friends what You have done for us and how You have had mercy on us.

Go before us, Lord, as You promised. And let all that we are and all that we do be Yours alone — Scripture our rule, grace our ground, faith our hand, Christ our Savior, and glory to God alone, forever.

Christ Proclaimed. Jesus is Lord.

Amen.
