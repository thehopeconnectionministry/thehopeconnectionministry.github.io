Mark 2 — The Servant Forgives: Authority to Forgive Sins

Jesus in Mark — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Gospel of Mark
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

In Mark 2, Jesus reveals Himself as the One who forgives sins — and that is a claim only God can make. He also shows Himself as the Friend of sinners who eats with tax collectors, the Lord of the Sabbath, and the Bridegroom whose presence means the new age has arrived. Every movement in this chapter presses the same question: by what authority does this man do these things? Mark's answer is that the authority is God's own, because this man is God.

Commentary

The paralytic and his friends (2:1–12). After some days, Jesus returns to Capernaum, and word spreads that He is at home — likely Peter's house. The crowd packs the place so full there is no room, not even at the door, and Jesus preaches the word to them. And four men come, carrying a paralytic. Unable to get near Jesus because of the crowd, they remove the roof above Him — they dig through the flat roof of mud and branches — and let down the bed on which the paralytic lies. And when Jesus sees their faith, He says to the paralytic something nobody expected:

"Son, your sins are forgiven." — Mark 2:5 (ESV)

The man came for his legs; Jesus went for his soul. The deepest need is never the one we think it is. And the scribes sitting there question in their hearts: "Why does this man speak like that? He is blaspheming! Who can forgive sins but God alone?" They are right about the principle and wrong about the person. Jesus, perceiving in His spirit that they question within themselves, says, "Which is easier, to say to the paralytic, 'Your sins are forgiven,' or to say, 'Rise, take up your bed and walk'?" Forgiveness is the easier thing to say, because no one can see whether it happened. So Jesus proves the invisible with the visible: "But that you may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins" — He says to the paralytic — "I say to you, rise, pick up your bed, and go home." And he rose and immediately picked up his bed and went out before them all, so that they were all amazed and glorified God, saying, "We never saw anything like this!"

The Greek word at the center of this chapter is exousia — authority, the rightful power to act. Jesus does not borrow authority like the scribes do. It is His own. And the title He uses for Himself is "the Son of Man," from Daniel 7:13–14, where one like a son of man comes with the clouds of heaven and receives dominion and glory and a kingdom. Mark will use that title again and again, and each time it carries the same double weight: this is the One who will suffer, and this is the One who will reign.

The call of Levi (2:13–17). Jesus goes out again beside the sea, and all the crowd comes to Him, and He teaches them. And as He passes by, He sees Levi the son of Alphaeus sitting at the tax booth — a tax collector, a man who had sold himself to Rome and grown rich off his own people's backs — and He says to him, "Follow me." And Levi rises and follows Him. Just like that. The same call that reached fishermen reaches a collaborator. Grace does not check your reputation before it calls your name.

And Jesus reclines at table in Levi's house, and many tax collectors and sinners recline with Him and His disciples, for there are many who follow Him. And the scribes of the Pharisees, seeing that He eats with tax collectors and sinners, say to His disciples, "Why does he eat with tax collectors and sinners?" And Jesus hears it and says to them:

"Those who are well have no need of a physician, but those who are sick. I came not to call the righteous, but sinners." — Mark 2:17 (ESV)

This is one of the most comforting sentences in the Bible. Jesus is the Physician. He did not come for people who think they are well. He came for people who know they are sick — sick with sin, sick with shame, sick with a life they cannot fix. The door of His house is open to sinners. It is closed only to those who will not admit they are sinners at all.

The question about fasting (2:18–22). Now John's disciples and the Pharisees are fasting, and people come and ask Jesus, "Why do John's disciples and the disciples of the Pharisees fast, but your disciples do not fast?" And Jesus answers: "Can the wedding guests fast while the bridegroom is with them? As long as they have the bridegroom with them, they cannot fast. The days will come when the bridegroom is taken away from them, and then they will fast in that day." The Bridegroom is here — Jesus is claiming the title of the Husband of God's people from Hosea and Isaiah — and while He is present, the mood is feast, not fast. But listen: "the days will come when the bridegroom is taken away." Even here, in chapter 2, Jesus is pointing to the cross. He will be taken away violently, and then His disciples will mourn.

Then come the twin pictures: "No one sews a piece of unshrunk cloth on an old garment... And no one puts new wine into old wineskins. If he does, the wine will burst the skins — and the wine is destroyed, and so are the skins. But new wine is for fresh wineskins." The gospel Jesus brings is new wine. It cannot be contained in the old skins of ritual religion and man-made tradition. It is not a patch on the old life. It is a new life entirely.

Lord of the Sabbath (2:23–28). One Sabbath, Jesus is going through the grainfields, and His disciples begin to make their way, picking and eating heads of grain — permitted by Deuteronomy 23:25, but counted as "work" by the Pharisees' tradition. The Pharisees say, "Look, why are they doing what is not lawful on the Sabbath?" And Jesus answers them with Scripture: "Have you never read what David did, when he was in need and was hungry, he and those who were with him: how he entered the house of God, in the time of Abiathar the high priest, and ate the bread of the Presence, which it is not lawful for any but the priests to eat, and also gave it to those who were with him?" David, God's anointed, fed his hungry men with holy bread, and God did not condemn him. Human need stands above ritual rule. And then Jesus draws the line all the way to Himself:

"And he said to them, 'The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath. So the Son of Man is lord even of the Sabbath.'" — Mark 2:27–28 (ESV)

The Sabbath was God's gift to man, not man's burden for God. And the One who stands in the grainfield is greater than David and greater than the Sabbath itself — He is its Lord. In five short stories, Mark 2 has shown us a man who forgives sins, calls sinners, claims to be the Bridegroom, and claims lordship over the Sabbath. No wonder the opposition is gathering.

Key verses

"And when Jesus saw their faith, he said to the paralytic, 'Son, your sins are forgiven.'" — Mark 2:5 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Mark 2 is that our deepest sickness is sin, and no physician on earth can cure it — only God can forgive sins, and we stand before Him guilty. The scribes were right about that much: forgiveness belongs to God alone. But the good news is that the God who alone can forgive has come to us in the flesh, sat down at our tables, and called us by name. The Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins — your sins — and He uses that authority not to condemn sinners but to call them home.

A note from the Reformers. This was Luther's kind of chapter. After years of fearing God as a righteous Judge, he found his peace in the Christ of Mark 2 — the Physician who eats with sinners. That is sola gratia, grace alone, and sola fide, faith alone: the sick do not earn the Physician; they receive Him.

Reflection question: The four friends carried their paralyzed friend to Jesus and would not let a crowd or a roof stop them. Who in your life needs to be carried to Jesus — and what is one thing you can do this week to bring them to Him?
