Mark 5 — The Servant Delivers: Legion, a Dying Girl, and a Desperate Woman

Jesus in Mark — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Gospel of Mark
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Mark 5 is a chapter of rescue. Jesus crosses the sea into Gentile country and frees a man possessed by a legion of demons, restores a woman who has bled for twelve years, and raises a twelve-year-old girl from the dead. Here Jesus reveals Himself as the Deliverer stronger than every chain, the Healer who is never defiled by our uncleanness but instead makes us clean, and the Lord of life who treats death the way a father treats sleep.

Commentary

The man among the tombs (5:1–20). They come to the other side of the sea, to the country of the Gerasenes. And when Jesus steps out of the boat, immediately a man with an unclean spirit comes out of the tombs and meets Him. He lives among the tombs. No one can bind him anymore, not even with a chain, for he has often been bound with shackles and chains, but he wrenched the chains apart and broke the shackles in pieces. No one has the strength to subdue him. Night and day among the tombs and on the mountains he is always crying out and cutting himself with stones. This is one of the saddest pictures in the Bible — a man destroyed and destroying himself, living among the dead, beyond the reach of every human remedy.

And when he sees Jesus from afar, he runs and falls down before Him. And crying out with a loud voice, he says, "What have you to do with me, Jesus, Son of the Most High God? I adjure you by God, do not torment me." For Jesus had been saying to him, "Come out of the man, you unclean spirit!" And Jesus asks him, "What is your name?" He replies, "My name is Legion, for we are many." A Roman legion numbered up to six thousand soldiers. This man is occupied territory, garrisoned by an army of evil. And the demons beg Jesus earnestly not to send them out of the country. Now a great herd of pigs, about two thousand, is feeding on the hillside, and they beg Him, "Send us to the pigs; let us enter them." So He gives them permission. And the unclean spirits come out and enter the pigs, and the herd rushes down the steep bank into the sea and drowns.

The herdsmen flee and tell it in the city and in the country. And people come to see what has happened, and they come to Jesus and see the demon-possessed man — the one who had the legion — sitting there, clothed and in his right mind. Sitting. Clothed. In his right mind. Three small phrases, and they describe a whole resurrection. And the people are afraid — and they begin to beg Jesus to depart from their region. The price of the pigs frightened them more than the deliverance of the man amazed them. It is possible to prefer your pigs to your Savior. And as Jesus is getting into the boat, the man who had been possessed begs to be with Him. And Jesus does not permit him but says to him:

"Go home to your friends and tell them how much the Lord has done for you, and how he has had mercy on you." — Mark 5:19 (ESV)

And he goes away and begins to proclaim in the Decapolis — the Ten Cities, Gentile country — how much Jesus has done for him, and everyone marvels. The first missionary to the Gentiles in Mark's Gospel is a man who, one day earlier, was living naked among the tombs. That is what mercy does.

Jairus and the woman (5:21–43). Jesus crosses again in the boat to the other side, and a great crowd gathers about Him beside the sea. Then comes one of the rulers of the synagogue, Jairus by name, and seeing Jesus, he falls at His feet and implores Him earnestly, saying, "My little daughter is dying. Come and lay your hands on her so that she may be made well and live." A synagogue ruler, a man of standing, on his knees in the dirt before Jesus. Desperation levels every man. And Jesus goes with him, and a great crowd follows and throngs about Him.

And there is a woman who has had a discharge of blood for twelve years, and who has suffered much under many physicians, and has spent all that she had, and is no better but rather grew worse. Mark, who so often tells the story of Peter's memories, does not spare the doctors — Peter remembered what the woman said about them. She has heard the reports about Jesus, and she comes up behind Him in the crowd and touches His garment, for she says, "If I touch even his garments, I will be made well." And immediately the flow of blood dries up, and she feels in her body that she is healed of her disease. Twelve years of bleeding — twelve years of uncleanness under the law of Leviticus 15, twelve years of being untouchable, twelve years of spending everything she has on cures that made her worse — ended in a single touch.

And Jesus, perceiving in Himself that power has gone out from Him, immediately turns about in the crowd and says, "Who touched my garments?" His disciples say, "You see the crowd pressing around you, and yet you say, 'Who touched me?'" But He looks around to see who has done it. And the woman, knowing what has happened to her, comes in fear and trembling and falls down before Him and tells Him the whole truth. The whole truth — Mark's Gospel is full of people who tell Jesus the truth and find mercy. And He says to her:

"Daughter, your faith has made you well; go in peace, and be healed of your disease." — Mark 5:34 (ESV)

Not "the touch made you well" — your faith made you well. The garment was nothing; the trust was everything. And He calls her "Daughter" — the only time in the Gospels Jesus addresses a woman this way. The untouchable outcast leaves as a daughter of the King.

While He is still speaking, there come messengers from the ruler's house saying, "Your daughter is dead. Why trouble the Teacher any further?" But overhearing what they say, Jesus says to the ruler of the synagogue:

"Do not fear, only believe." — Mark 5:36 (ESV)

Five words to carry through every dark night of a believer's life. Jesus comes to the house and sees a commotion, people weeping and wailing loudly — the hired mourners of that culture, already at their work. And He says, "Why are you making a commotion and weeping? The child is not dead but sleeping." And they laugh at Him. But He puts them all outside and takes the child's father and mother and those who are with Him — Peter, James, and John — and goes in where the child is. Taking her by the hand, He says to her:

"Talitha cumi," which means, "Little girl, I say to you, arise." — Mark 5:41 (ESV)

Talitha cumi — Aramaic, the language Jesus spoke at home. Mark preserves the very syllables, because Peter was in that room and never forgot them. It is the tenderest command in the Gospel: as a father wakes a child for breakfast. And immediately the girl gets up and begins walking, for she was twelve years of age — twelve years, the same span the woman bled; the woman's misery began the year this girl's life began, and both end on the same day, at the same hand. And they are immediately overcome with amazement. And He strictly charges them that no one should know this, and tells them to give her something to eat. The Lord who raises the dead remembers that the living need lunch. Nothing about His care is abstract.

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Mark 5 is that some bondage is beyond every human chain and some sickness beyond every human doctor — and death itself comes for our children while we are still on the road. We are not strong enough for what is against us. But the good news is that Jesus is. A legion of demons is not too many for Him. Twelve years of bleeding is not too long for Him. Death is not too final for Him. He meets us among the tombs, in the crowd, and at the bedside, and His word stands: "Do not fear, only believe."

A note from the Reformers. Luther called faith a living, daring confidence in God's grace — and Mark 5 is full of it: a woman reaching for a garment's hem, a father begging through his tears. Neither brought Christ a résumé; both brought Him empty hands and honest need. That is sola fide: faith alone lays hold of Christ, and Christ never sends an empty hand away ashamed.

Reflection question: Which of these three rescued people is most like you today — the man among the tombs who needed deliverance, the woman who had spent everything and needed healing, or Jairus, who needed hope when the news turned worst? Bring that need to Jesus by name in prayer.
