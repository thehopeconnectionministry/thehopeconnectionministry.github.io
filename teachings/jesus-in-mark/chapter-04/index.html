Mark 4 — The Servant Sows: Parables of the Kingdom and the Storm

Jesus in Mark — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Gospel of Mark
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

In Mark 4, Jesus reveals Himself as the Sower of the kingdom — the Teacher whose word carries the life of God — and as the Lord of wind and sea, whose mere word stills a storm. The chapter is built around hearing: how a person receives the word of Jesus decides everything. And it ends with the disciples asking the question this whole Gospel asks: "Who then is this?"

Commentary

The parable of the sower (4:1–20). Again Jesus begins to teach beside the sea, and such a very large crowd gathers that He gets into a boat and sits in it on the sea, and the whole crowd is beside the sea on the land. And He teaches them many things in parables, and in His teaching He says to them: "Listen!" That word stands at the front of the chapter like a bell: Listen. "Behold, a sower went out to sow." The seed falls on four kinds of ground. Some falls along the path, and the birds come and devour it. Some falls on rocky ground, where it has little soil, and it springs up quickly, but when the sun rises it is scorched, and since it has no root it withers away. Some falls among thorns, and the thorns grow up and choke it, and it yields no grain. And other seeds fall into good soil and produce grain, growing up and increasing and yielding thirtyfold and sixtyfold and a hundredfold. And He says, "He who has ears to hear, let him hear."

When He is alone, those around Him with the twelve ask Him about the parables. And He says to them: "To you has been given the secret of the kingdom of God, but for those outside everything is in parables, so that 'they may indeed see but not perceive, and may indeed hear but not understand, lest they should turn and be forgiven.'" Jesus quotes Isaiah 6:9–10, the prophet's commission to a people who would harden themselves. Parables are mercy and judgment at once: they reveal the kingdom to those who lean in, and they conceal it from those who have already decided not to hear. Then Jesus explains the parable plainly. "The sower sows the word." The path is the heart where Satan immediately takes away the word that was sown. The rocky ground is the one who hears the word and immediately receives it with joy, but has no root in himself — when tribulation or persecution arises on account of the word, immediately he falls away. The thorns are the cares of the world and the deceitfulness of riches and the desires for other things, which enter in and choke the word, and it proves unfruitful. But the good soil —

"But those that were sown on the good soil are the ones who hear the word and accept it and bear fruit, thirtyfold and sixtyfold and a hundredfold." — Mark 4:20 (ESV)

Four soils, one seed. The difference is never the seed. The difference is the hearing. Mark is writing to Christians in Rome under pressure, and this parable tells them — and us — that shallow roots will not survive the heat, and crowded hearts will not bear fruit. The word must be received deep, and kept clear.

The lamp and the measure (4:21–25). "Is a lamp brought in to be put under a basket, or under a bed, and not on a stand? For nothing is hidden except to be made manifest; nor is anything secret except to come to light. If anyone has ears to hear, let him hear." The light of the kingdom is not meant to be hidden; what Jesus whispers now will be shouted from housetops later. And then the warning about the measure: "Pay attention to what you hear: with the measure you use, it will be measured to you, and still more will be added to you. For to the one who has, more will be given, and from the one who has not, even what he has will be taken away." How you listen determines what you receive. Listen carelessly and you lose even what you thought you had. Listen hungrily and heaven keeps adding.

The seed growing secretly (4:26–29). "The kingdom of God is as if a man should scatter seed on the ground. He sleeps and rises night and day, and the seed sprouts and grows; he knows not how. The earth produces by itself, first the blade, then the ear, then the full grain in the ear. But when the grain is ripe, at once he puts in the sickle, because the harvest has come." Only Mark records this parable, and it is a gift to every discouraged servant of God. The farmer does not make the seed grow; he cannot even explain how it grows. He sows, he sleeps, he rises — and the earth produces by itself. The Greek word is automatē — automatically, of its own accord, the same root that gives us our word "automatic." The growth of the kingdom is God's work, not ours. We sow the word and we trust; the life is in the seed, and the harvest belongs to God.

The mustard seed (4:30–34). "With what can we compare the kingdom of God... It is like a grain of mustard seed, which, when sown on the ground, is the smallest of all the seeds on earth, yet when it is sown it grows up and becomes larger than all the garden plants and puts out large branches, so that the birds of the air can make nests in its shade." The mustard seed was proverbially tiny, and the grown plant could reach ten feet or more. That is the shape of God's kingdom: despised beginnings, glorious endings. It starts with a crucified man and a handful of frightened followers; it ends with the nations sheltering in its branches, like the great tree of Daniel 4 and Ezekiel 17. And Mark tells us Jesus spoke the word to the crowds in parables as they were able to hear it, but explained everything in private to His own disciples.

Jesus calms the storm (4:35–41). On that day, when evening has come, Jesus says, "Let us go across to the other side." And leaving the crowd, they take Him with them in the boat, just as He is. And a great windstorm arises, and the waves break into the boat, so that the boat is already filling. But Jesus is in the stern, asleep on the cushion. The cushion — Peter remembered the cushion. And they wake Him and say to Him, "Teacher, do you not care that we are perishing?" And He awakes and rebukes the wind and says to the sea:

"Peace! Be still!" — Mark 4:39 (ESV)

The words in Greek are terse and commanding: Siōpa, pephimōso — "Be silent; be muzzled." Jesus speaks to the storm the way He spoke to the demon in chapter 1, and the storm obeys the way the demon obeyed. And the wind ceases, and there is a great calm. And He says to them, "Why are you so afraid? Have you still no faith?" And they are filled with great fear and say to one another:

"Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?" — Mark 4:41 (ESV)

Who indeed. The Psalms say it is the Lord who stills the roaring of the seas (Psalm 65:7; Psalm 89:9), the Lord who made the sea and rules it. The disciples are beginning to understand that the man asleep in their boat is no ordinary man. Their fear has changed direction: before, they feared the storm; now they fear the One who commands it. That is the right fear, and it is the beginning of wisdom.

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Mark 4 is that most hearing is shallow hearing — the word is snatched away, scorched away, or choked by cares and riches, and storms will come that show us how little faith we actually have. Left to ourselves, we are the disciples in the boat, terrified and asking whether He cares. But the good news is that the seed carries its own life, the kingdom grows while we sleep, and the One in the boat with us is the Lord of the sea. He does care. He speaks, and there is a great calm.

A note from the Reformers. Spurgeon preached from the parable of the sower all his life, and his counsel was simple: do not waste time guessing which soil you are as if it were a riddle — hear the word, receive it, and keep hearing it. Faith comes by hearing (Romans 10:17). And Calvin, on the seed growing secretly, reminded the church that the growth of the kingdom is God's hidden work: we sow and we sleep, and He gives the increase.

Reflection question: Which soil describes your heart right now — the path, the rocks, the thorns, or the good ground? What one care or desire might be choking the word in you, and will you name it before God today?
