Mark 3 — The Servant Appoints: The Twelve, the Accusation, and the True Family

Jesus in Mark — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Gospel of Mark
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Mark 3 shows Jesus gathering His people and facing down His enemies. He appoints the Twelve — the foundation of the new Israel — and He answers the most serious charge ever leveled against Him: that He works by Satan's power. Here Jesus reveals Himself as the Stronger Man who binds the strong man and plunders his house, and He redefines family itself: His true brothers and sisters are those who do the will of God.

Commentary

The man with the withered hand (3:1–6). Again Jesus enters the synagogue, and a man is there with a withered hand. And they watch Jesus, to see whether He will heal on the Sabbath, so that they might accuse Him. Mark's phrase is chilling: they are not watching to learn; they are watching to prosecute. And Jesus says to the man with the withered hand, "Come here." And He says to them, "Is it lawful on the Sabbath to do good or to do harm, to save life or to kill?" But they are silent. And He looks around at them with anger, grieved at their hardness of heart. Mark does not hide the anger of Jesus. The Lord who is meek and lowly in heart is also angry — angry at hardness that would rather protect a rule than relieve a suffering man. And He says to the man, "Stretch out your hand." He stretched it out, and his hand was restored. The Pharisees went out and immediately held counsel with the Herodians against Him, how to destroy Him. The Herodians were political operators loyal to Herod's house — normally the Pharisees' enemies. Hatred of Jesus makes strange allies. The plot to kill Him begins in chapter 3, and it will not rest until chapter 15.

The crowds by the sea (3:7–12). Jesus withdraws with His disciples to the sea, and a great crowd follows — from Galilee and Judea, from Jerusalem and Idumea, from beyond the Jordan, and from around Tyre and Sidon. When the great crowd heard all that He was doing, they came to Him. And He tells His disciples to have a boat ready for Him because of the crowd, lest they crush Him, for He has healed many, so that all who have diseases press around Him to touch Him. And whenever the unclean spirits see Him, they fall down before Him and cry out, "You are the Son of God." And He strictly orders them not to make Him known. The demons keep confessing what men will not — and Jesus keeps silencing them, because He will define His Sonship by the cross, not by their testimony.

The appointing of the Twelve (3:13–19). And Jesus goes up on the mountain and calls to Him those whom He desires, and they come to Him. And He appoints twelve, whom He also names apostles, "so that they might be with him and he might send them out to preach and have authority to cast out demons." Notice the order. First: to be with Him. Before preaching, before authority, before mission — presence. The apostolic life begins in being with Jesus, and so does every Christian life. The twelve are Simon, whom He surnames Peter; James the son of Zebedee and John his brother, whom He surnames Boanerges, that is, Sons of Thunder; Andrew, and Philip, and Bartholomew, and Matthew, and Thomas, and James the son of Alphaeus, and Thaddaeus, and Simon the Zealot, and Judas Iscariot, who betrayed Him. Twelve — for the twelve tribes of Israel. In these men, Jesus is founding a new people of God. And Mark does not hide the last name on the list. Judas is there from the beginning, called like the rest. The shadow of betrayal falls across the whole Gospel.

The accusation and the answer (3:20–30). Then Jesus goes home, and the crowd gathers again, so that they cannot even eat. And when His family hears it, they go out to seize Him, for they are saying, "He is out of his mind." Even His own family misunderstands Him. And the scribes who came down from Jerusalem — an official delegation — say, "He is possessed by Beelzebul," and "by the prince of demons he casts out the demons." Beelzebul was a name for Satan, likely drawn from the old Philistine god Baal-zebub. Their charge is the darkest possible: the works of the Holy Spirit, they say, are the works of the devil.

Jesus calls them to Him and answers in parables. "How can Satan cast out Satan? If a kingdom is divided against itself, that kingdom cannot stand. And if a house is divided against itself, that house will not be able to stand. And if Satan has risen up against himself and is divided, he cannot stand, but is coming to an end." The charge is not just wicked; it is nonsense. Then Jesus turns the picture around: "But no one can enter a strong man's house and plunder his goods, unless he first binds the strong man. Then indeed he may plunder his house." Satan is the strong man, and his house is this world, and his goods are the people he holds captive. Jesus is the Stronger Man who has come to bind him and set the captives free. Every demon cast out is a room plundered in Satan's house.

Then comes the solemn warning: "Truly, I say to you, all sins will be forgiven the children of man, and whatever blasphemies they utter, but whoever blasphemes against the Holy Spirit never has forgiveness, but is guilty of an eternal sin" — for they were saying, "He has an unclean spirit." This is the sin of calling the Spirit's work the devil's work — of seeing Jesus clearly and, with hardened eyes, naming good as evil and evil as good. It is not a slip of the tongue; it is a settled, final rejection of the light. The very breadth of the promise before it should be noticed: all sins will be forgiven the children of man. The only unforgivable position is the one that refuses forgiveness itself.

The true family (3:31–35). And His mother and His brothers come, and standing outside they send to Him and call Him. And a crowd is sitting around Him, and they say to Him, "Your mother and your brothers are outside, seeking you." And He answers them, "Who are my mother and my brothers?" And looking about at those who sit around Him, He says, "Here are my mother and my brothers! For whoever does the will of God, he is my brother and sister and mother."

"For whoever does the will of God, he is my brother and sister and mother." — Mark 3:35 (ESV)

Jesus is not dishonoring His family — He is widening the door. Blood ties you to a family by birth; the will of God ties you to Jesus by new birth. The people sitting in that circle, listening to His word and doing it, are closer to Him than blood. That circle is still open. Whoever does the will of God — repenting, believing, obeying — is welcomed into the family of the Son of God.

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Mark 3 is that hearts can grow so hard that they watch a man healed and respond by plotting a death — and can even look at the work of the Holy Spirit and call it the work of Satan. Sin, left alone, does not stay neutral; it hardens. But the good news is that Jesus has come as the Stronger Man to bind Satan and plunder his house, and that all sins — all of them — will be forgiven the children of man who come to Him. And the family He is building is not closed by blood or birth. Whoever does the will of God is His brother, His sister, His mother.

A note from the Reformers. Terrified consciences have asked for five hundred years whether they have committed the unforgivable sin of this chapter. Luther's pastoral answer still stands: the very fact that you fear you have grieved the Holy Spirit is proof you have not finally rejected Him — a heart that trembles at this warning is a heart the Spirit is still working in. Let the breadth of the promise carry you: all sins will be forgiven the children of man who come to Christ.

Reflection question: Jesus appointed the Twelve first of all "to be with him." Before your serving, your working, your striving — are you spending time simply being with Jesus? What would it look like to put His presence first this week?
