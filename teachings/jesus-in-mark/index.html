A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

The Hope Connection
Lewis Fuller

"For even the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many." — Mark 10:45 (ESV)

Mark's Gospel moves fast. It is the shortest of the four Gospels, and it reads like someone telling the story of Jesus with their sleeves rolled up, in a hurry to get to the next thing. There is a reason for that hurry. Mark writes about a Jesus who does not stand still. He heals, He preaches, He casts out demons, He crosses seas, He feeds thousands — and then He sets His face toward Jerusalem, toward the cross, toward death. The Gospel of Mark is the Gospel of action. It is the good news of a Servant who came to work, to suffer, and to save.

Who wrote it. The church has always believed this Gospel came from John Mark. Mark was the cousin of Barnabas, and he traveled with Paul and Barnabas on their first missionary journey. He left them partway through, and that failure caused a sharp disagreement between Paul and Barnabas years later — but Mark was restored. By the end of Paul's life, Paul writes of Mark as "very useful to me for ministry" (2 Timothy 4:11). Mark also stood close to the apostle Peter. The early church father Papias tells us that Mark wrote down what he heard from Peter's preaching — he was Peter's interpreter and companion, and Peter called him "my son Mark" (1 Peter 5:13). So when you read Mark, you are reading the eyewitness memories of Peter, the fisherman who walked with Jesus, set down by the young man who served beside him. That is why Mark's account is so vivid and so full of small, lived-in details — the green grass where the crowds sat down to be fed, the cushion Jesus slept on in the back of the boat, the names of people no one else names. Peter remembered those things because he was there.

How to Use This Commentary

This commentary was built to be used, not just read. Here is how to get the most out of it.

The order of every session. Work through each chapter the same way, in this order:

  1. Read the chapter in your own Bible first. Open your Bible to the chapter of Mark and read it all the way through before you read a word of commentary. The commentary serves the Scripture; it never replaces it. Read it out loud if you can — Mark wrote for hearers as much as readers, and you will catch things with your ears that your eyes slide past.
  2. Then read the commentary section for that chapter. Keep your Bible open beside it and look up the verses as they come. Start with "Jesus in this chapter" — before anything else, fix your eyes on how Christ is revealed there. Charles Spurgeon said a passage is not finished until Christ has been seen in it, and that is the aim of every section here.
  3. Then sit with the reflection question. Each chapter ends with one reflection question and a "bad news and good news" paragraph. Do not rush past them. Read the bad news honestly — let the law do its searching work — and then read the good news slowly, out loud if you can. Answer the reflection question in prayer, out loud to God, not just in your head.
  4. Write in a notebook. Keep a simple notebook with this study. Write down your answer to the reflection question, your answer to the study guide question for that chapter (see the Mark Study Guide below), and the memory verse for the chapter. A truth is not really yours until you have written it, prayed it, and lived it. You do not need anything fancy — a dollar notebook and a pen will do the whole Gospel.

The rhythm: three sessions a week. This fits the study rhythm used across The Hope Connection: three sessions a week, one book at a time, until the book is done. At one chapter per session, three sessions a week, you will walk through all sixteen chapters of Mark in about five and a half weeks. If you want to go slower — and slower is often better — spread one chapter across the whole week: session one, read the chapter in your Bible and the "Jesus in this chapter" opening; session two, read the commentary; session three, return to the chapter, work through the questions, and pray it back to God. Either pace is fine. What matters is the steadiness, not the speed.

No catch-up piles. This rule is not negotiable, because it protects the whole habit: if you miss a day, you do not owe anybody two chapters. You simply pick up at the next chapter at the next session. Life happens — sickness, family, work, hard weeks. A study rhythm that piles up guilt every time life interrupts will not survive a month. This one bends and keeps going. Missed a week? Start again at the next chapter. The Lord is not keeping a tally, and neither are we.

Using it alone. Everything above is written for one reader, one Bible, one notebook, and the Lord. Pray before you open the book — a short, real prayer: "Lord, open my eyes, and let this Word search me." Pray again when you finish, turning the chapter's good news into thanksgiving and its bad news into confession.

Using it with a spouse or a friend. Read the chapter out loud together at the start of the session — take turns reading. Then read the commentary, or take turns reading it aloud too. Talk through the reflection question and the study guide question together, honestly. Pray for each other out of what the chapter showed you. Mark is a Gospel about following Jesus together — the disciples never walk alone in it — and two people studying it together will see things one reader alone will miss.

Using it in a small group. One chapter per meeting. Have someone read the chapter aloud from the Bible to open. Walk through the commentary section together, stopping wherever the text raises a question. Save real time at the end for the reflection question and the study guide question — that is where study becomes life. Close in prayer, and let the chapter's memory verse be the group's verse for the week: read it together at the start of the next meeting and see who can say it from memory. Keep the group small enough that everybody talks, and keep the door open — Mark's Gospel is good news for people who have never opened a Bible, and every chapter here is written plain enough for a brand-new believer to follow.

The memory verses. The study guide below gives one memory verse for each chapter — sixteen verses, the spine of Mark's Gospel. If you take one verse a week alongside the chapter rhythm, by the time you finish the book you will carry its whole story in your heart: the kingdom at hand, the Physician of sinners, the faith that makes well, the ransom for many, and the empty tomb. That is treasure no one can take from you.

Study Questions — One per Chapter

Work each question in your notebook after the chapter's reflection question. Many of them follow the Bible's own pattern of putting off the old and putting on the new (Ephesians 4:22–24; Colossians 3:8–10).

  1. Mark 1 — Prepare the way. Jesus touched the leper before He cleansed him. Put off avoiding the "untouchable" people in your life; put on the compassion of Christ. Who is one person you have kept at a distance that you could draw near to this week — and what is the first step?
  2. Mark 2 — Come to the Physician. The paralytic's friends would not let a crowd or a roof stop them. Put off the pride that hides your sickness; put on the honesty that brings it to Jesus. What is one sin or struggle you have been covering that you will bring into the light — to God, and if needed to a trusted brother or sister — this week?
  3. Mark 3 — Do the will of God. Jesus calls those who do God's will His family. Put off one clear disobedience you have been postponing; put on one concrete act of obedience. Name both, with a day this week when the obedience will happen.
  4. Mark 4 — Guard your hearing. The seed is never the problem; the soil is. Put off one thing that is choking the word in you — a care, a screen habit, a hunger for more. Put on one practice that deepens your hearing: reading the chapter aloud, or hearing it again before the week ends. Which will you do?
  5. Mark 5 — Tell what the Lord has done. Jesus sent the delivered man home to tell his friends. Put off silence about your story; put on witness. Who are your "friends at home" — the people who knew you before — and when will you tell one of them what the Lord has done for you?
  6. Mark 6 — Rest and compassion. Jesus told tired disciples to rest, and then had compassion on the crowd that interrupted the rest. Put off both hurry and hardness. Put on one real block of rest this week, and one act of compassion toward someone who is like a sheep without a shepherd. Write down both.
  7. Mark 7 — Heart before hands. The Pharisees washed hands and kept unclean hearts. Put off one religious habit you do by routine while your heart is far away; put on heart-worship: before your next time of prayer, confess one thing from Jesus' list in Mark 7:21–22 that you recognize in yourself, and ask for a clean heart.
  8. Mark 8 — Take up your cross. Confessing Christ is the beginning; carrying a cross is the road. Put off one comfort, habit, or ambition that competes with following Jesus. Put on one specific act of self-denial this week — something that costs you — and do it for His sake and the gospel's.
  9. Mark 9 — Be last of all. The disciples argued about greatness on the road to the cross. Put off one rivalry or hunger to be noticed. Put on one hidden act of service for someone who can give you nothing back — someone with no status, like the child Jesus set in the midst. Keep it secret.
  10. Mark 10 — Open your hands. The rich young man went away sorrowful because his hands were full. Put off clinging to one possession, comfort, or security. Put on generosity: give one thing away this week — money, time, or something you own — to someone in need, and receive the kingdom like a child, empty-handed.
  11. Mark 11 — Pray and forgive. Jesus joins mountain-moving prayer to a forgiving heart. Put off one grudge you have been carrying. Put on this practice: before you pray each day this week, forgive that person by name — and then pray one big, mountain-sized prayer you have been afraid to pray.
  12. Mark 12 — Love God wholly. The great commandment asks for heart, soul, mind, and strength — everything. Where is your love for God divided right now: heart, soul, mind, or strength? Put off the divided part's rival. Put on one concrete act of love for a neighbor this week that costs you something real.
  13. Mark 13 — Stay awake. Jesus ends His teaching on the end times with one command: stay awake. Put off one thing that puts your soul to sleep — spiritual laziness, endless distraction, prayerlessness. Put on one watchfulness habit this week: a set time of morning prayer, or ending each day by remembering that Christ will return.
  14. Mark 14 — Watch and pray. In Gethsemane the disciples slept and then fled; Peter boasted and then denied. Put off self-reliance — the "I never will" boasting of the flesh. Put on humble dependence: set aside one hour this week to watch and pray, and in some hard thing say with Jesus, "Not what I will, but what you will."
  15. Mark 15 — Stand at the cross. The disciples fled, but the centurion confessed, and Joseph of Arimathea came out of hiding to bury Jesus. Put off silence and fear of what people will think. Put on the centurion's confession: say out loud to one person this week, "Truly this man was the Son of God" — and tell them why you believe it.
  16. Mark 16 — Go and tell. The women fled in fear and said nothing to anyone — at first. But the message could not stay buried. Put off fear that keeps the good news in the tomb. Put on going: tell one person this week that Jesus is risen. And if you have failed Him, like Peter, hear the message again: "tell his disciples and Peter" — and go back to Him.

Introduction — Mark's Gospel at a Glance

Mark's Gospel moves fast. It is the shortest of the four Gospels, and it reads like someone telling the story of Jesus with their sleeves rolled up, in a hurry to get to the next thing. There is a reason for that hurry. Mark writes about a Jesus who does not stand still. He heals, He preaches, He casts out demons, He crosses seas, He feeds thousands — and then He sets His face toward Jerusalem, toward the cross, toward death. Who wrote it. The church has always believed this Gospel came from John Mark. Mark was the cousin of Barnabas, and he traveled with Paul and Barnabas on their first missionary journey. He left them partway through, and that failure caused a sharp disagreement between Paul and Barnabas years later — but Mark was restored. By the end of Paul's life, Paul writes of Mark as "very useful to me for ministry" (2 Timothy 4:11). Mark also stood close to the apostle Peter. The early church father Papias tells us that Mark wrote down what he heard from Peter's preaching — he was Peter's interpreter and companion, and Peter called him "my son Mark" (1 Peter 5:13). So when you read Mark, you are reading the eyewitness memories of Peter, the fisherman who walked with Jesus, set down by the young man who served beside him. That is why Mark's account is so vivid and so full of small, lived-in details — the green grass where the crowds sat down to be fed, the cushion Jesus slept on in the back of the boat, the names of people no one else names. Peter remembered those things because he was there.

Who it was written for. Mark wrote for believers in Rome — ordinary Christians, many of them Gentiles, living under the shadow of the most powerful empire on earth. He explains Jewish customs his readers would not have known, and he translates Aramaic words into Greek. These were people who needed to know that their Lord was not defeated by Rome's cross, but was the Son of God who conquered it. Mark's Gospel was likely written in the 50s or 60s of the first century, before the destruction of the Jerusalem temple in AD 70, and possibly during the years when Christians in Rome were beginning to suffer for their faith under Nero. It is a Gospel for people who are suffering and wondering whether it is worth it to keep following Jesus. Mark's answer is simple: look at Him. He suffered first.

The Gospel of action. Mark's favorite word is "immediately." In the Greek it is euthys, and Mark uses it more than forty times — more than all the other Gospels put together. Immediately Jesus is baptized. Immediately He is driven into the wilderness. Immediately He calls His disciples. Immediately He heals. The story never dawdles, because the work of Jesus never dawdles. Grace is on the move.

The suffering Servant. Mark shows us Jesus as the Servant of Isaiah's songs, the One who came to serve rather than to be served. But Mark will not let us stop there. The Servant is also the Son of God, the Christ, the King — and the path to His throne runs straight through the cross. Mark's Gospel is the only one that names the cry of dereliction, the centurion's confession, and the torn curtain, and then ends with women fleeing the empty tomb in fear. It is a Gospel that demands a decision. Who is this man? Mark begins with the answer — "The beginning of the gospel of Jesus Christ, the Son of God" — and then spends sixteen chapters proving it, until a Roman soldier standing at the foot of the cross says the same thing out loud.

The key verse. The whole Gospel gathers itself into one sentence, spoken by Jesus as He walks toward Jerusalem:

"For even the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many." — Mark 10:45 (ESV)

That is Mark's Gospel in miniature. Jesus came. He served. He gave His life. He gave it as a ransom — the price paid to set captives free. That is the story we are about to walk through, chapter by chapter. And the theme running through every chapter is this: Jesus the Servant-King, who suffers, who serves, and who saves.

Read in the Reformation way. This commentary reads Mark the way the Reformers taught the church to read every book of the Bible, and it is worth naming their five watchwords up front, because they run through every chapter that follows. Sola scriptura — Scripture alone is our rule; Mark himself opens with "as it is written," and every claim in these pages stands or falls by the text. Sola gratia — grace alone saves; no one in this Gospel earns Jesus' mercy, from the leper to the rich young ruler. Sola fide — faith alone receives that grace; again and again Jesus says, "Your faith has made you well." Solus Christus — Christ alone is the Savior; the ransom of Mark 10:45 is His work, not ours. And soli Deo gloria — glory to God alone; even a Roman centurion ends up giving God the glory at the foot of the cross. Along the way you will meet brief nods to Luther, Calvin, and Spurgeon where they genuinely shed light on a passage — a line or two, never a lecture. And in every chapter the law and the gospel are kept distinct, the way the Reformers insisted: the law shows us our sin and our need (the bad news), and the gospel shows us Christ and His finished work (the good news). Mix those two up and you lose both. Keep them straight and Mark's Gospel will do what it was written to do.

How to use this commentary. Each chapter section begins with "Jesus in this chapter" — a short look at how Christ is revealed there. Then the commentary walks the chapter's movements in order, explaining the text plainly and fully. Key verses are quoted in full from the English Standard Version. Each chapter closes with "The bad news and the good news" — the law-then-gospel pattern, the weight of the chapter and the hope in Christ — and one reflection question to carry into prayer. Read it slowly. Read it with your Bible open. And remember, as you read, that this is not a book about a dead teacher. This is the living Christ, the Son of God, meeting you where you are.
