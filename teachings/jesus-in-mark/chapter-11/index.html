Mark 11 — The Servant Enters Jerusalem: The King, the Fig Tree, and the Temple

Jesus in Mark — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Gospel of Mark
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

In Mark 11, Jesus enters Jerusalem as King — riding a colt in fulfillment of Zechariah's prophecy — and immediately asserts His authority over the temple, His Father's house. The cursed fig tree frames the temple cleansing: a show of leaves without fruit is judged. Here Jesus reveals Himself as the King who inspects His house, the Prophet who pronounces judgment on fruitless religion, and the Teacher who calls His people to faith, prayer, and forgiveness.

Commentary

The triumphal entry (11:1–11). Now when they draw near to Jerusalem, to Bethphage and Bethany, at the Mount of Olives, Jesus sends two of His disciples and says to them, "Go into the village in front of you, and immediately as you enter it you will find a colt tied, on which no one has ever sat. Untie it and bring it. If anyone says to you, 'Why are you doing this?' say, 'The Lord has need of it and will send it back here immediately.'" And they go away and find a colt tied at a door outside in the street, and they untie it. And some of those standing there say to them, "What are you doing, untying the colt?" And they tell them what Jesus has said, and they let them go. And they bring the colt to Jesus and throw their cloaks on it, and He sits on it.

Every detail preaches. A colt on which no one has ever sat — an animal set apart, never used for common work, fit for a sacred purpose. And Jesus rides it into Jerusalem exactly as Zechariah 9:9 foretold: "Behold, your king is coming to you; righteous and having salvation is he, humble and mounted on a donkey, on a colt, the foal of a donkey." Not a war horse — a donkey's colt. The King comes humble, having salvation. And many spread their cloaks on the road, and others spread leafy branches that they had cut from the fields. And those who go before and those who follow are shouting:

"Hosanna! Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord! Blessed is the coming kingdom of our father David! Hosanna in the highest!" — Mark 11:9–10 (ESV)

Hosanna is a Hebrew cry — hoshia-na, "Save us, we pray" — drawn from Psalm 118:25–26, the psalm sung at the feasts for the coming King. The crowd is singing the right song, even if most of them do not yet understand what kind of salvation He brings. And Jesus enters Jerusalem and goes into the temple. And when He has looked around at everything, as it is already late, He goes out to Bethany with the twelve. Mark's quiet sentence carries weight: the King inspects His house, takes note of everything, and withdraws. Judgment is coming — but first, a night in Bethany.

The fig tree and the temple (11:12–19). On the following day, when they come from Bethany, Jesus is hungry. And seeing in the distance a fig tree in leaf, He goes to see if He can find anything on it. When He comes to it, He finds nothing but leaves, for it is not the season for figs. And He says to it, "May no one ever eat fruit from you again." And His disciples hear it. This is not a tantrum over breakfast. In the Old Testament the fig tree is a standing picture of Israel — Jeremiah 8:13, Hosea 9:10, Micah 7:1 all use it — and a tree covered in leaves with no fruit is a picture of religion with all the appearance of life and none of the reality. Jesus is performing a parable in action, and the next scene tells us what it means.

And they come to Jerusalem. And He enters the temple and begins to drive out those who sell and those who buy in the temple, and He overturns the tables of the money-changers and the seats of those who sell pigeons. And He does not allow anyone to carry anything through the temple. The court of the Gentiles — the only place where the nations could come and pray — had been turned into a market and a shortcut, with commerce set up by the priestly establishment for profit. And He teaches and says to them:

"Is it not written, 'My house shall be called a house of prayer for all the nations'? But you have made it a den of robbers." — Mark 11:17 (ESV)

Jesus weaves two prophets together: Isaiah 56:7, where God's house is a house of prayer for all the nations, and Jeremiah 7:11, where the temple had become a hideout for robbers who thought the building would protect them from judgment. The house built for the nations' prayers had become a machine for fleecing them. And the chief priests and the scribes hear it and seek a way to destroy Him, for they fear Him, because all the crowd is astonished at His teaching. And when evening comes, they go out of the city. The inspection is over. The sentence has been spoken. The leaders' answer is not repentance but a murder plot.

The withered fig tree, faith, and prayer (11:20–25). As they pass by in the morning, they see the fig tree withered away to its roots. And Peter remembers and says to Him, "Rabbi, look! The fig tree that you cursed has withered." In less than a day, judgment has done its work — a warning in wood and leaf of what is coming on a fruitless temple system within a generation. And Jesus answers them:

"Have faith in God. Truly, I say to you, whoever says to this mountain, 'Be taken up and thrown into the sea,' and does not doubt in his heart, but believes that what he says will come to pass, it will be done for him. Therefore I tell you, whatever you ask in prayer, believe that you have received it, and it will be yours." — Mark 11:22–24 (ESV)

"This mountain" is likely the Mount of Olives where they stand — or the temple mount itself. The point is not a technique for moving geology; it is the nature of faith. Faith does not doubt God's power or goodness; it asks great things of a great God and trusts Him for the answer. And Jesus binds prayer to forgiveness: "And whenever you stand praying, forgive, if you have anything against anyone, so that your Father also who is in heaven may forgive you your trespasses." The people who pray to a forgiving Father must be a forgiving people. Unforgiveness and prayer cannot share a heart.

The question of authority (11:27–33). And they come again to Jerusalem. And as Jesus is walking in the temple, the chief priests and the scribes and the elders come to Him, and they say to Him, "By what authority are you doing these things, or who gave you this authority to do them?" It is the right question asked with the wrong heart — they do not want to submit to His authority; they want to trap it. Jesus says to them, "I will ask you one question; answer me, and I will tell you by what authority I do these things. Was the baptism of John from heaven or from man? Answer me." And they discuss it with one another, saying, "If we say, 'From heaven,' he will say, 'Why then did you not believe him?' But shall we say, 'From man'?" — they are afraid of the people, for they all hold that John really was a prophet. So they answer Jesus, "We do not know." And Jesus says to them, "Neither will I tell you by what authority I do these things." Their answer is a lie dressed as humility: they know, but they will not say, because the truth would cost them their position. Jesus will not hand pearls of revelation to men who have already decided to trample them. The authority question is never answered for those who ask it as a weapon. It is answered — fully, gloriously — for those who ask it on their knees.

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Mark 11 is that it is possible to be covered in leaves and bear no fruit — to have the building, the rituals, the reputation, and none of the life. God's King inspects His house, and fruitless religion, however impressive its foliage, stands under judgment. But the good news is that this King came not only to judge but to open the house: His Father's house is meant to be a house of prayer for all the nations — for Gentile beggars and broken sinners, for you — and He is about to tear down the barriers with His own body. And He invites us still: "Have faith in God."

A note from the Reformers. Calvin taught that prayer is the chief exercise of faith — and this chapter shows both sides of his point. The King who curses the fruitless tree is the same Lord who turns to His disciples and says, "Have faith in God," inviting them to ask great things. Profession without fruit is only leaves; faith that truly prays, and forgives, is fruit. The two always grow on the same tree.

Reflection question: If Jesus walked through your life the way He walked through the temple, looking around at everything — what tables would He overturn? And what fruit is your life bearing that matches its leaves?
