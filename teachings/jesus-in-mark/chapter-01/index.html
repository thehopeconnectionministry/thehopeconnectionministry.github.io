Mark 1 — The Servant Begins: Baptism, Temptation, and the First Call

Jesus in Mark — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Gospel of Mark
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Jesus enters His public ministry here, and Mark introduces Him in four great strokes: He is the beloved Son, declared by the Father at the Jordan; He is the tempted Son, driven by the Spirit into the wilderness and standing firm where Israel and Adam fell; He is the preaching King, announcing that the kingdom of God has come near; and He is the compassionate Healer, touching the untouchable and setting people free. From the very first chapter, authority and compassion walk together in Him.

Commentary

The preparation (1:1–8). Mark wastes no time. He opens with a title that is also a confession of faith:

"The beginning of the gospel of Jesus Christ, the Son of God." — Mark 1:1 (ESV)

The word "gospel" means good news — the news a herald would run to announce after a victory in battle. This is the good news about Jesus, and it is good news from Jesus. He is "the Christ," the Anointed One Israel had been waiting for, and "the Son of God," the title that will follow Him all the way to the cross.

Before Jesus appears, John the Baptist appears. John preached in the wilderness of Judea, calling people to a baptism of repentance for the forgiveness of sins, and all the country of Judea and all Jerusalem went out to him. Mark tells us this fulfills what was written in Isaiah the prophet: "Behold, I send my messenger before your face, who will prepare your way, the voice of one crying in the wilderness: 'Prepare the way of the Lord, make his paths straight'" (Mark 1:2–3). Mark is weaving together two Old Testament promises — Malachi 3:1, about the messenger who prepares the way, and Isaiah 40:3, about the voice crying in the wilderness. The point is plain: God had promised to come to His people, and He was keeping that promise now.

John himself was a man out of the wilderness tradition — clothed with camel's hair, wearing a leather belt around his waist, eating locusts and wild honey. He looked like Elijah, and that was no accident. Israel was meant to see in him the prophet who prepares the way. And John pointed away from himself: "After me comes he who is mightier than I, the strap of whose sandals I am not worthy to stoop down and untie. I have baptized you with water, but he will baptize you with the Holy Spirit" (Mark 1:7–8). John knew his place. The water was a sign of repentance; the Spirit would be the reality of new life.

The baptism (1:9–11). Jesus comes from Nazareth of Galilee and is baptized by John in the Jordan. And as He comes up out of the water, Mark says the heavens were torn open — the Greek word is schizō, to tear or split apart. It is the same word Mark will use at the cross, when the curtain of the temple is torn in two. At the Jordan, heaven is torn open and the Spirit descends on Jesus like a dove, and a voice comes from heaven: "You are my beloved Son; with you I am well pleased." Here, in the opening chapter, the Father declares what the whole Gospel will prove. Jesus is the Son — the beloved Son of Psalm 2:7, and the Servant in whom God delights from Isaiah 42:1. And He is being sent, not to reign from a throne in Jerusalem, but to go down into the water with sinners, to be numbered with the transgressors.

The temptation (1:12–13). "The Spirit immediately drove him out into the wilderness." That word immediately is Mark's hurry-word, and here it carries force: no sooner is Jesus declared the Son than He is driven into the desert. He was in the wilderness forty days, tempted by Satan, and He was with the wild animals, and the angels were ministering to Him. Mark does not describe the temptations the way Matthew and Luke do — he gives us one stark picture instead. Forty days echoes Israel's forty years in the wilderness, and Adam's failure in the garden. Where Adam fell, and where Israel grumbled, Jesus stands. And the wild animals and the angels frame the scene: this is a new Adam, at peace in a world of danger, served by heaven itself.

The preaching begins (1:14–15). After John was arrested — Mark slips that in without comment, and the reader is meant to feel the weight of it: the forerunner's voice is being silenced, and now the One he announced must speak — Jesus came into Galilee, proclaiming the gospel of God, and saying:

"The time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God is at hand; repent and believe in the gospel." — Mark 1:15 (ESV)

This is the first sermon of Jesus in Mark, and it is only one sentence. The time is fulfilled: God's long promise has come due. The kingdom is at hand: God's reign has arrived in the person of the King. And the response is twofold — repent, turn from sin and self-rule, and believe, trust the good news. Repentance without faith is despair; faith without repentance is presumption. Jesus joins them together, and He has joined them ever since.

The first disciples (1:16–20). Passing alongside the Sea of Galilee, Jesus sees Simon and Andrew his brother casting a net into the sea, for they were fishermen. And Jesus said to them, "Follow me, and I will make you become fishers of men." And immediately they left their nets and followed Him. Going a little farther, He sees James the son of Zebedee and John his brother in the boat mending their nets, and He calls them too. They left their father Zebedee in the boat with the hired servants and followed Him. Notice what Jesus does not do: He does not recruit in the temple courts or among the scholars. He goes to working men, men with calloused hands and a living to make, and He calls them to a new kind of fishing — catching men alive for the kingdom. And they go. Immediately. That is what the call of Jesus does: it interrupts ordinary life and makes it extraordinary.

Authority in Capernaum (1:21–28). They go into Capernaum, and on the Sabbath Jesus enters the synagogue and begins to teach, and the people are astonished, "for he taught them as one who had authority, and not as the scribes." The scribes quoted other teachers; Jesus speaks with His own authority. And immediately there is a man in the synagogue with an unclean spirit, who cries out, "What have you to do with us, Jesus of Nazareth? Have you come to destroy us? I know who you are — the Holy One of God." The demons know Him before men do. But Jesus rebukes him, saying, "Be silent, and come out of him!" And the unclean spirit, convulsing him and crying out with a loud voice, comes out of him. The crowd is amazed: "What is this? A new teaching with authority! He commands even the unclean spirits, and they obey him." The news of Jesus spreads everywhere throughout the region of Galilee.

Healing in the house and the city (1:29–34). Immediately they leave the synagogue and enter the house of Simon and Andrew, with James and John. Simon's mother-in-law lies ill with a fever, and they tell Jesus about her. He comes and takes her by the hand and lifts her up, and the fever leaves her, and she begins to serve them. Mark loves this detail: the first person Jesus heals in a home gets up and serves. That is what healing is for. That evening at sundown, the whole city gathers at the door, bringing all who are sick and oppressed by demons, and He heals many who are sick with various diseases, and casts out many demons — and He would not permit the demons to speak, because they knew Him. This is Mark's pattern of the "Messianic secret": Jesus silences the demons' testimony because the time for His full unveiling has not yet come.

Prayer in the lonely place (1:35–39). Rising very early in the morning, while it is still dark, Jesus departs and goes out to a desolate place, and there He prays. And Simon and those who are with Him search for Him, and they find Him and say, "Everyone is looking for you." And He said to them, "Let us go on to the next towns, that I may preach there also, for that is why I came out." Jesus will not be captured by the crowds' demands. He knows His mission — to preach — and He goes throughout all Galilee, preaching in their synagogues and casting out demons.

The leper (1:40–45). And a leper comes to Him, imploring Him and kneeling, saying, "If you will, you can make me clean." Leprosy in that day meant more than disease: it meant uncleanness, exclusion from the community, and a living death outside the camp. The leper has no right to come near anyone, and yet he comes near to Jesus. And Jesus is moved with pity — the Greek word is splanchnizomai, a compassion that comes from the gut, from the deepest place in a person — and He stretches out His hand and touches him. Touching a leper made a man unclean by the law. Jesus touches him anyway, and says:

"I will; be clean." — Mark 1:41 (ESV)

And immediately the leprosy leaves him, and he is made clean. Jesus charges him sternly to say nothing to anyone, but to show himself to the priest and offer for his cleansing what Moses commanded. But the man goes out and begins to talk freely about it, and to spread the news, so that Jesus can no longer openly enter a town, but is out in desolate places, and people come to Him from every quarter. The Servant who touches the untouchable is now pushed outside the towns Himself. It is the first hint of the exchange that will define this Gospel: He takes the leper's place outside the camp, so that the leper can be brought inside.

Key verses

"The beginning of the gospel of Jesus Christ, the Son of God." — Mark 1:1 (ESV)

"The time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God is at hand; repent and believe in the gospel." — Mark 1:15 (ESV)

"And Jesus said to them, 'Follow me, and I will make you become fishers of men.'" — Mark 1:17 (ESV)

"Moved with pity, he stretched out his hand and touched him and said to him, 'I will; be clean.'" — Mark 1:41 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Mark 1 is that we are like the leper and the man with the unclean spirit — unclean, excluded, oppressed, and unable to make ourselves clean. No amount of washing, no ritual, no effort of our own can touch what is wrong inside us. But the good news is that Jesus comes to us in our uncleanness, and He does what no one else will do: He reaches out His hand and touches us, and He says, "I will; be clean." The kingdom of God is at hand, and the King has come to make the unclean clean.

A note from the Reformers. Mark opens his Gospel the way the Reformers said every sermon and every Christian life must open — standing on what is written. Luther said his conscience was captive to the Word of God; that is sola scriptura, Scripture alone, and it is the ground under this whole chapter.

Reflection question: Jesus calls ordinary working people to follow Him — what is He asking you to leave behind, and what is He asking you to take up, in order to follow Him today?
