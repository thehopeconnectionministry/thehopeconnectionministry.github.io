Mark 12 — The Servant Confronted: Tenants, Taxes, and the Greatest Commandment

Jesus in Mark — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Gospel of Mark
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

In Mark 12, Jesus spends His last public teaching day in the temple courts, and every group in Jerusalem takes its turn against Him — and every one leaves silenced. He tells a parable that is really Israel's whole history and His own death sentence, answers the tax question with a sentence that has ordered church and state ever since, corrects the Sadducees out of Moses' own book, and sums up the entire law in two commandments. Here Jesus reveals Himself as the rejected Cornerstone, the Teacher with no rival, and the Lord who is David's son and David's Lord at once.

Commentary

The parable of the tenants (12:1–12). And Jesus begins to speak to them in parables. "A man planted a vineyard and put a fence around it and dug a pit for the winepress and built a tower, and leased it to tenants and went into another country." Every hearer knew the song behind the story: Isaiah 5, where the vineyard is the house of Israel and the Lord of the vineyard looks for justice and finds bloodshed. When the season came, the owner sent a servant to the tenants to get from them some of the fruit of the vineyard. And they took him and beat him and sent him away empty-handed. Again he sent another servant, and they struck him on the head and treated him shamefully. And he sent another, and him they killed. And so with many others: some they beat, and some they killed. The servants are the prophets — beaten like Jeremiah, killed like Zechariah, sawn in two like Isaiah by tradition. Israel's history, told in ninety seconds.

"He had still one other, a beloved son. Finally he sent him to them, saying, 'They will respect my son.' But those tenants said to one another, 'This is the heir. Come, let us kill him, and the inheritance will be ours.' And they took him and killed him and threw him out of the vineyard." The beloved Son — the same words the Father spoke at the baptism and the Transfiguration — sent last of all, and killed, and cast out. Jesus is telling them, in their own temple, exactly what they are about to do to Him. "What will the owner of the vineyard do? He will come and destroy the tenants and give the vineyard to others." And then He quotes Psalm 118, the very psalm the crowds sang on Palm Sunday:

"Have you not read this Scripture: 'The stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone; this was the Lord's doing, and it is marvelous in our eyes'?" — Mark 12:10–11 (ESV)

The builders reject the stone; God makes it the cornerstone, the stone that holds the whole building together. Rejection by men, exaltation by God — that is the plot of the cross and resurrection in one verse. And the chief priests and scribes and elders perceive that He has told the parable against them, and they seek to arrest Him, but fear the crowd. So they leave Him and go away. The parable is already coming true as they walk off: the tenants are plotting against the Son.

Render to Caesar (12:13–17). And they send to Him some of the Pharisees and some of the Herodians, to trap Him in His talk — Pharisees and Herodians together again, the strange alliance of chapter 3. And they come and say to Him, "Teacher, we know that you are true and do not care about anyone's opinion. For you are not swayed by appearances, but truly teach the way of God. Is it lawful to pay taxes to Caesar, or not? Should we pay them, or should we not?" The flattery is bait, and the question is a snare with teeth on both sides. The poll tax was paid to Rome with Caesar's coin, and it was hated as a symbol of slavery. Say "pay it" and the crowds will call Him a collaborator; say "do not pay it" and Rome will call Him a rebel, and the Herodians standing right there will report Him. But knowing their hypocrisy, Jesus says to them, "Why put me to the test? Bring me a denarius and let me look at it." And they bring one. And He says to them, "Whose likeness and inscription is this?" They say to Him, "Caesar's." Jesus says to them:

"Render to Caesar the things that are Caesar's, and to God the things that are God's." — Mark 12:17 (ESV)

And they marvel at Him. The coin bears Caesar's image, so let Caesar have his coin. But man bears God's image — Genesis 1:27 — so let God have you. In one sentence Jesus gives the state its due and limits it forever: Caesar gets taxes; he does not get worship, conscience, or the soul. Luther would build his teaching on the two kingdoms out of this sentence — God rules the world by law and government, and He rules His church by the gospel, and neither kingdom may swallow the other. Twenty centuries of church and state have been living inside this one verse ever since.

The Sadducees and the resurrection (12:18–27). And Sadducees come to Him, who say that there is no resurrection. The Sadducees were the priestly aristocracy — wealthy, powerful, and theologically thin: they accepted only the five books of Moses and denied the resurrection, angels, and the world to come. And they ask Him a question designed to make resurrection look ridiculous: a woman married to seven brothers in turn, each dying childless, under the levirate law of Deuteronomy 25 — "in the resurrection, when they rise again, whose wife will she be?" And Jesus says to them:

"Is this not the reason you are wrong, because you know neither the Scriptures nor the power of God?" — Mark 12:24 (ESV)

Two errors, and they are the root of nearly all error: ignorance of Scripture and unbelief in God's power. "For when they rise from the dead, they neither marry nor are given in marriage, but are like angels in heaven." Resurrection life is not earthly life stretched thin; it is a new order entirely. And then Jesus defeats them out of the only books they accept — out of Exodus, the passage about the bush, where God speaks to Moses and says, "I am the God of Abraham, and the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob." Present tense, centuries after the patriarchs died. "He is not God of the dead, but of the living. You are quite wrong." God does not introduce Himself as the God of corpses and memories. Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob live, because their God lives and keeps covenant forever. Calvin loved this argument: Christ proves the greatest doctrine — the resurrection of the dead — not from a spectacular text but from the covenant faithfulness of God packed into a single present-tense verb.

The great commandment (12:28–34). And one of the scribes comes up and hears them disputing with one another, and seeing that Jesus answered them well, asks Him, "Which commandment is the most important of all?" This scribe, at least, asks honestly. Jesus answers:

"The most important is, 'Hear, O Israel: The Lord our God, the Lord is one. And you shall love the Lord your God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your mind and with all your strength.' The second is this: 'You shall love your neighbor as yourself.' There is no other commandment greater than these." — Mark 12:29–31 (ESV)

Hear, O Israel — in Hebrew, the Shema, from Deuteronomy 6:4–5, the confession every devout Jew recited morning and evening. Before the command comes the confession: the Lord is one. And the love He commands is total — heart, soul, mind, strength; nothing held back, no corner of a person unclaimed. The second commandment is Leviticus 19:18, love of neighbor, and the two cannot be separated: you cannot claim the first while breaking the second. And the scribe says to Him, "You are right, Teacher. You have truly said that he is one, and there is no other besides him. And to love him with all the heart and with all the understanding and with all the strength, and to love one's neighbor as oneself, is much more than all whole burnt offerings and sacrifices." And when Jesus sees that he answered wisely, He says to him, "You are not far from the kingdom of God." Not far — but not in. Knowing the summary of the law is a fine thing; the law's summary, honestly heard, is also its accusation, for no one in that courtyard loved God with all his heart. The scribe stands at the door. What he needs is not more information but the King Himself. And after that no one dares to ask Jesus any more questions.

Whose son is the Christ? (12:35–37). And as Jesus teaches in the temple, He says, "How can the scribes say that the Christ is the son of David? David himself, in the Holy Spirit, declared, 'The Lord said to my Lord, "Sit at my right hand, until I put your enemies under your feet."' David himself calls him Lord. So how is he his son?" Jesus quotes Psalm 110:1 — the most quoted Old Testament verse in the New Testament — and turns the scribes' own doctrine into a riddle they cannot solve. The Christ is David's son, truly human, born of David's line. But David, a thousand years earlier, called Him "my Lord." The Son of David is greater than David: He is the Lord of David. Only one person can be both — the God-man, David's descendant and David's God in one. And the throng hears Him gladly. The common people, Mark notes with pleasure, love what the scholars cannot answer.

Beware of the scribes; the widow's offering (12:38–44). And in His teaching He says, "Beware of the scribes, who like to walk around in long robes and like greetings in the marketplaces and have the best seats in the synagogues and the places of honor at feasts, who devour widows' houses and for a pretense make long prayers. They will receive the greater condemnation." Religion as a costume, prayer as a performance, and widows' houses devoured behind it all — the Lord reserves His sharpest condemnation for hypocrisy in holy dress.

And then Mark sets a widow directly in front of us, as if to show us the opposite of everything Jesus just condemned. He sits down opposite the treasury and watches the people putting money into the offering box. Many rich people put in large sums. And a poor widow comes and puts in two small copper coins, which make a penny — two lepta, the smallest coins in circulation, worth almost nothing. And He calls His disciples to Him and says to them, "Truly, I say to you, this poor widow has put in more than all those who are contributing to the offering box. For they all contributed out of their abundance, but she out of her poverty has put in everything she had, all she had to live on." God does not weigh gifts by their size but by their cost. The rich gave what they would never miss; the widow gave her life — "all she had to live on," literally, her whole living. The scribes devour widows' houses; this widow gives her house, her bread, her everything, to God. That is the kind of giving the kingdom is built on — and it points to the One who, within days, will give everything He has, all His life, for us.

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Mark 12 is that the tenants kill the Son, the scholars know neither the Scriptures nor the power of God, and the great commandment — love God with all your heart, soul, mind, and strength — stands over every one of us as a debt we have never paid. Measured by Mark 12:30, we are all lawbreakers, and the scribe's honest position, "not far from the kingdom," is as far as the law can take any man. But the good news is that the rejected Stone has become the Cornerstone. The Son who was killed and thrown out of the vineyard is the Lord who loved God wholly and loved His neighbor — us — to the death. What the law demands, Christ has done; and those who trust Him are counted in Him, and taught by His Spirit to love as they have been loved.

A note from the Reformers. Calvin insisted that the law, honestly preached, is a mirror and a schoolmaster: it shows us our sin and drives us to Christ (Galatians 3:24). Mark 12 is that mirror polished bright. The Reformers called this the law's chief use — not to make us righteous, for it cannot, but to make us run to the One who is.

Reflection question: The scribe was "not far from the kingdom of God." Is there a place where you have settled for being not far — knowing the right answers, admiring the teaching — instead of entering in through Christ Himself? What is keeping you outside?
