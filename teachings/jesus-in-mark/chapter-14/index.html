Mark 14 — The Servant Betrayed: The Table, the Garden, and the Trial

Jesus in Mark — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Gospel of Mark
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Mark 14 is the longest chapter in the Gospel, and it is the chapter of betrayal closing in from every side — plotted by the priests, priced by Judas, slept through by the disciples, denied by Peter. Against all of it stands Jesus: anointed for burial by a woman who understood, giving His body and blood at the table, surrendering in the garden with "not what I will, but what you will," and standing silent before His accusers until He confesses, openly and finally, "I am." Here Jesus reveals Himself as the Passover Lamb, the suffering Son who drinks the cup willingly, and the Christ who will not deny His identity even when the confession signs His death warrant.

Commentary

The plot and the anointing (14:1–11). It is two days before the Passover and the Feast of Unleavened Bread. And the chief priests and the scribes are seeking how to arrest Him by stealth and kill Him, for they say, "Not during the feast, lest there be an uproar from the people." God's timetable will overrule theirs: He will die at Passover, as the Lamb, whether they plan it or not. And while He is at Bethany in the house of Simon the leper, as He is reclining at table, a woman comes with an alabaster flask of ointment of pure nard, very costly, and she breaks the flask and pours it over His head. Pure nard, imported from the Himalayas, worth more than three hundred denarii — more than a year of a working man's wages — in a flask she breaks, so that nothing can be held back or used again. It is total devotion, poured out in one act. There are those who rebuke her sharply: "Why was the ointment wasted like that? For this ointment could have been sold for more than three hundred denarii and given to the poor." The arithmetic sounds so reasonable. But Jesus says, "Leave her alone. Why do you trouble her? She has done a beautiful thing to me. For you always have the poor with you, and whenever you want, you can do good for them. But you will not always have me. She has done what she could; she has anointed my body beforehand for burial. And truly, I say to you, wherever the gospel is proclaimed in the whole world, what she has done will be told in memory of her." She alone in the room seems to understand that He is going to die — and her "waste" is the one act in the chapter Jesus calls beautiful. You are reading her memorial right now: the gospel is being proclaimed, and what she did is being told, exactly as He promised.

And then Judas Iscariot, who is one of the twelve, goes to the chief priests in order to betray Him to them. Mark places the two scenes side by side without comment: a woman pours out a fortune on Jesus in love; an apostle sells Him for silver in secret. And when they hear it, they are glad and promise to give him money. And he seeks an opportunity to betray Him.

The Last Supper (14:12–25). And on the first day of Unleavened Bread, when they sacrifice the Passover lamb, His disciples say to Him, "Where will you have us go and prepare for you to eat the Passover?" And He sends two of His disciples with instructions as precise as the colt in chapter 11: a man carrying a jar of water will meet you — a man's job it was not, in that culture, so he will be easy to spot — follow him, and say to the master of the house, "The Teacher says, Where is my guest room, where I may eat the Passover with my disciples?" And he will show you a large upper room furnished and ready. The Lord who borrowed a colt borrows a room; He owns nothing and commands everything. And the disciples set out and prepare the Passover.

And when it is evening, He comes with the twelve. And as they are reclining at table and eating, Jesus says, "Truly, I say to you, one of you will betray me, one who is eating with me." They begin to be sorrowful and to say to Him one after another, "Is it I?" He says to them, "It is one of the twelve, one who is dipping bread into the dish with me. For the Son of Man goes as it is written of him, but woe to that man by whom the Son of Man is betrayed! It would have been better for that man if he had not been born." God's sovereignty and man's guilt stand side by side, unblinking: the betrayal fulfills Scripture, and Judas is still guilty of it. Then, as they are eating, Jesus takes bread, and after blessing it breaks it and gives it to them, and says:

"Take; this is my body." — Mark 14:22 (ESV)

And He takes a cup, and when He has given thanks He gives it to them, and they all drink of it. And He says to them:

"This is my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many." — Mark 14:24 (ESV)

The words reach back to Exodus 24, where Moses sprinkled the blood of the covenant on Israel at Sinai, and forward to Jeremiah 31, the new covenant in which God forgives iniquity and remembers sin no more. The Passover meal — celebrating the lamb whose blood turned away death in Egypt — is now fulfilled and transformed: this Lamb's body and blood are the meal. "Do this in remembrance" is Luke's and Paul's record of the command; Mark gives us the gift itself, plain and staggering. And Jesus adds the vow of hope: "Truly, I say to you, I will not drink again of the fruit of the vine until that day when I drink it new in the kingdom of God." The cross is not the last word; a banquet is coming, and He will drink with His people there. And when they have sung a hymn — almost certainly from the Hallel, Psalms 113–118, the psalms of the Passover — they go out to the Mount of Olives. He sings on His way to Gethsemane. Let that settle in.

The prediction of Peter's denial (14:26–31). And Jesus says to them, "You will all fall away, for it is written, 'I will strike the shepherd, and the sheep will be scattered.'" He quotes Zechariah 13:7 — and notice who does the striking: I will strike the shepherd. Behind the priests, behind Judas, behind Pilate, stands the purpose of God. "But after I am raised up, I will go before you to Galilee." Even the warning carries the promise: raised up, and going before them — the Shepherd will regather His scattered sheep. Peter says to Him, "Even though they all fall away, I will not." And Jesus says to him, "Truly, I tell you, this very night, before the rooster crows twice, you will deny me three times." Mark alone records the rooster crowing twice — Peter's memory again, precise even about his shame. But Peter says emphatically, "If I must die with you, I will not deny you." And they all say the same. Every one of them means it. Every one of them will fail. Sincerity is not the same as strength.

Gethsemane (14:32–42). And they go to a place called Gethsemane — the name means "oil press," and in this press the Son of God will be crushed. And He says to His disciples, "Sit here while I pray." And He takes with Him Peter and James and John, and begins to be greatly distressed and troubled. And He says to them, "My soul is very sorrowful, even to death. Remain here and watch." The Lord who stilled the sea is now in anguish — not play-acting, not exaggerating. Isaiah's Man of Sorrows is sorrowful unto death. And going a little farther, He falls on the ground and prays that, if it were possible, the hour might pass from Him. And He says:

"Abba, Father, all things are possible for you. Remove this cup from me. Yet not what I will, but what you will." — Mark 14:36 (ESV)

Abba — the Aramaic word a child uses for his father at home, intimate as "Papa," and no one in Judaism had dared to address God with it the way Jesus does. Mark preserves it because it is the sound of the Son's own prayer life, and Paul tells us the Spirit teaches us to cry the same word (Romans 8:15). The cup is the cup of God's wrath against sin, filled in the prophets (Isaiah 51:17; Jeremiah 25:15). Jesus does not shrink from the Father's will — but He truly shrinks from the cup, and He prays it might pass, because what is in that cup is real: the forsakenness of chapter 15. His submission is not the absence of horror; it is obedience in the full face of it. "Yet not what I will, but what you will" — the prayer Adam failed to pray in his garden, prayed perfectly in this one.

And He comes and finds them sleeping, and He says to Peter, "Simon, are you asleep? Could you not watch one hour? Watch and pray that you may not enter into temptation. The spirit indeed is willing, but the flesh is weak." Three times He prays; three times He finds them sleeping, for their eyes are very heavy, and they do not know what to answer Him. And He comes the third time and says to them, "Are you still sleeping and taking your rest? It is enough; the hour has come. The Son of Man is betrayed into the hands of sinners. Rise, let us be going; see, my betrayer is at hand." The praying One stands; the sleeping ones scatter. Whatever strength the church has ever had in a crisis, it learned here: watch and pray, because the flesh is weak even when the spirit is willing.

The arrest (14:43–52). And immediately, while He is still speaking, Judas comes, one of the twelve, and with him a crowd with swords and clubs, from the chief priests and the scribes and the elders. Now the betrayer has given them a sign, saying, "The one I will kiss is the man. Seize him and lead him away under guard." And when he comes, he goes up to Him at once and says, "Rabbi!" and kisses Him. The kiss of a disciple, used as a signal for an arrest — there is no colder detail in the Bible. And they lay hands on Him and seize Him. But one of those who stand by draws his sword and strikes the servant of the high priest and cuts off his ear — John tells us it was Peter's sword, and the servant's name was Malchus. And Jesus says to them, "Have you come out as against a robber, with swords and clubs to capture me? Day after day I was with you in the temple teaching, and you did not seize me. But let the Scriptures be fulfilled." That is the reason He gives for submitting: not their strength, but Scripture's fulfillment. And they all leave Him and flee. All of them, as He said they would.

And a young man follows Him, with nothing but a linen cloth about his naked body. And they seize him, but he leaves the linen cloth and runs away naked. Mark alone records this strange, small scene, and the church has long believed the young man is Mark himself — the author slipping his own shameful cameo into the record: I was there, and I ran too, in nothing but a sheet. If so, it is the signature of a Gospel that hides nobody's failure, not even its writer's.

The trial before the council (14:53–65). And they lead Jesus to the high priest — Caiaphas. And all the chief priests and the elders and the scribes come together. And Peter follows Him at a distance, right into the courtyard of the high priest. And he is sitting with the guards and warming himself at the fire. Mark will keep cutting between the two scenes: Jesus confessing inside, Peter denying outside. The council seeks testimony against Jesus to put Him to death, but they find none. Many bear false witness against Him, but their testimony does not agree. Some stand up and bear false witness, saying, "We heard him say, 'I will destroy this temple that is made with hands, and in three days I will build another, not made with hands.'" Yet even about this their testimony does not agree. Their case is collapsing — and under their own law, two witnesses must agree (Deuteronomy 19:15). And the high priest stands up in the midst and asks Jesus, "Have you no answer to make? What is it that these men testify against you?" But He remains silent and makes no answer — the Lamb silent before its shearers, as Isaiah 53:7 said. Again the high priest asks Him, "Are you the Christ, the Son of the Blessed?" And Jesus says:

"I am, and you will see the Son of Man seated at the right hand of Power, and coming with the clouds of heaven." — Mark 14:62 (ESV)

Everything the "Messianic secret" held back is now poured out in one sentence, to the one audience that will kill Him for it. I am — echoing the name of God. Seated at the right hand of Power — Psalm 110:1. Coming with the clouds of heaven — Daniel 7:13. Jesus fuses the two great texts and applies them to Himself, and He tells the court that will condemn Him that they will one day see Him as their Judge. And the high priest tears his garments and says, "What further witnesses do we need? You have heard his blasphemy. What is your decision?" And they all condemn Him as deserving death. And some begin to spit on Him and to cover His face and to strike Him, saying to Him, "Prophesy!" And the guards receive Him with blows. The third passion prediction is being fulfilled item by item — mocked, spit upon, struck — before the night is even over.

Peter denies his Lord (14:66–72). And as Peter is below in the courtyard, one of the servant girls of the high priest comes, and seeing Peter warming himself, she looks closely at him and says, "You also were with the Nazarene, Jesus." But he denies it, saying, "I neither know nor understand what you mean." And he goes out into the gateway, and the rooster crows. And the servant girl sees him and again says to the bystanders, "This man is one of them." But again he denies it. And after a little while the bystanders again say to Peter, "Certainly you are one of them, for you are a Galilean." But he begins to invoke a curse on himself and to swear, "I do not know this man of whom you speak." The man who said "If I must die with you, I will not deny you" is now swearing oaths that he does not even know Him — to a servant girl, by a fire. And immediately the rooster crows a second time. And Peter remembers how Jesus said to him, "Before the rooster crows twice, you will deny me three times." And he breaks down and weeps. Mark ends the chapter not with Peter's restoration — that waits for chapter 16 and one word, "and Peter" — but with his tears. And those tears matter: Peter's fall is total, but it ends in weeping, where Judas' ended in despair. The difference between the two men is not that one sinned worse. It is that one wept and turned back, and the other did not.

Key verses

"Take; this is my body... This is my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many." — Mark 14:22, 24 (ESV)

"Abba, Father, all things are possible for you. Remove this cup from me. Yet not what I will, but what you will." — Mark 14:36 (ESV)

"Watch and pray that you may not enter into temptation. The spirit indeed is willing, but the flesh is weak." — Mark 14:38 (ESV)

"I am, and you will see the Son of Man seated at the right hand of Power, and coming with the clouds of heaven." — Mark 14:62 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Mark 14 is that every human support around Jesus fails in a single night: Judas sells Him, the disciples sleep and scatter, Peter swears he never knew Him, and the religious court condemns the innocent Son of God on a charge of blasphemy for telling the truth. Left to ourselves, we are all in this chapter somewhere — betrayers, sleepers, deniers. But the good news is that Jesus walks through all of it for those very failures. He gives His body and blood "poured out for many" — for Judases who repent too late is not the story; for Peters who weep, it is. He drinks the cup so that those who denied Him can be forgiven by Him. The Shepherd is struck, and the sheep are scattered — but He has already promised: "After I am raised up, I will go before you to Galilee."

A note from the Reformers. Luther stood in awe at Gethsemane, marveling that the Son of God truly trembled — that in the garden Christ bore, in His own conscience, the terror of the wrath our sins deserved, and did not turn back. The Reformers called what happened there and at the cross solus Christus at its deepest: no saint, no disciple, no church stood with Him or helped Him. He drank the cup alone, so that He could share its blessings with many.

Reflection question: In Gethsemane, Jesus prayed, "Not what I will, but what you will." What is the hard thing in front of you right now where you need to pray that same prayer honestly — and what keeps your flesh sleeping instead of watching?
