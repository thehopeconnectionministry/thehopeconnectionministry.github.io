Mark 13 — The Servant Warns: Signs, Suffering, and Staying Awake

Jesus in Mark — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Gospel of Mark
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Mark 13 is the Lord's longest teaching in this Gospel — the Olivet Discourse, spoken on the Mount of Olives with the temple in view. Jesus foretells the destruction of the temple and Jerusalem within that generation, warns His disciples of deception, persecution, and tribulation, and looks beyond it all to His own return in glory. Here Jesus reveals Himself as the Prophet who sees the end from the beginning, the Son of Man who will come in clouds with great power and glory, and the Master who leaves His servants one standing order: stay awake.

Commentary

"Not one stone upon another" (13:1–2). And as Jesus comes out of the temple, one of His disciples says to Him, "Look, Teacher, what wonderful stones and what wonderful buildings!" The temple of Herod was one of the wonders of the world — stones of white marble and gold, some of them enormous, a building project already decades underway. It looked permanent as the hills. And Jesus says to him, "Do you see these great buildings? There will not be left here one stone upon another that will not be thrown down." Within forty years, in AD 70, the Roman general Titus will burn the temple and level the city, and Jesus' words will stand fulfilled to the letter — Josephus, the Jewish historian, describes the destruction in detail. The first lesson of the chapter is carved in rubble: everything men build, even for God, is temporary. Only the word of Christ is not.

The disciples' question and the first warnings (13:3–8). And as He sits on the Mount of Olives opposite the temple, Peter and James and John and Andrew ask Him privately, "Tell us, when will these things be, and what will be the sign when all these things are about to be accomplished?" Notice that their question mixes two horizons — the fall of the temple and the end of all things — and Jesus' answer moves between them like a man describing two mountain ranges, one near and one far, in a single view. Prophecy in Scripture often works this way: the near judgment on Jerusalem becomes a picture and pledge of the final judgment of the world.

And Jesus begins to say to them, "See that no one leads you astray. Many will come in my name, saying, 'I am he!' and they will lead many astray." The first danger of the last days is not persecution but deception — false Christs, false alarms, false teaching. "And when you hear of wars and rumors of wars, do not be alarmed. This must take place, but the end is not yet. For nation will rise against nation, and kingdom against kingdom. There will be earthquakes in various places; there will be famines. These are but the beginning of the birth pains." Birth pains: painful, real, and increasing — but they mean a birth is coming, not merely an ending. History between the cross and the return will groan like a woman in labor (Romans 8:22), and none of it takes God by surprise.

Persecution and the promise of the Spirit (13:9–13). "But be on your guard. For they will deliver you over to councils, and you will be beaten in synagogues, and you will stand before governors and kings for my sake, to bear witness before them." What Jesus promises His apostles is not comfort but a witness stand: arrest will become their pulpit. "And the gospel must first be proclaimed to all nations." Even the persecution serves the mission. "And when they bring you to trial and deliver you over, do not be anxious beforehand what you are to say, but say whatever is given you in that hour, for it is not you who speak, but the Holy Spirit." The Spirit who descended on Jesus at the Jordan will stand with His witnesses in the courtroom. "And brother will deliver brother over to death, and the father his child, and children will rise against parents and have them put to death. And you will be hated by all for my name's sake. But the one who endures to the end will be saved." Endurance is the mark: not a flash of enthusiasm, but faith that holds through hatred, betrayal, and death itself — held, in the end, by the God who holds His own (John 10:28–29).

The abomination of desolation (13:14–23). "But when you see the abomination of desolation standing where he ought not to be (let the reader understand), then let those who are in Judea flee to the mountains." The phrase comes from Daniel — Daniel 9:27, 11:31, 12:11 — where it named the desecration of the temple by Antiochus Epiphanes, who set up an altar to Zeus and sacrificed pigs on God's altar in 167 BC. Jesus takes Daniel's phrase and points it forward: a horror like that is coming again on Jerusalem. "Let the one who is on the housetop not go down, nor enter his house, to take anything out, and let the one who is in the field not turn back to take his cloak. Alas for women who are pregnant and for those who are nursing infants in those days! Pray that it may not happen in winter." When Titus' armies closed on Jerusalem in AD 70, the Christians of the city — remembering these very words, according to the early church historian Eusebius — fled across the Jordan to Pella and were saved, while the city perished in one of the most terrible sieges in history. The words of Jesus literally saved the lives of those who obeyed them. "For in those days there will be such tribulation as has not been from the beginning of the creation that God created until now, and never will be. And if the Lord had not cut short the days, no human being would be saved. But for the sake of the elect, whom he chose, he shortened the days." Even tribulation runs on God's leash, and its length is set by mercy toward His elect.

Then the warning returns: "And then if anyone says to you, 'Look, here is the Christ!' or 'Look, there he is!' do not believe it. For false christs and false prophets will arise and perform signs and wonders, to lead astray, if possible, the elect. But be on guard; I have told you all things beforehand." If possible — the elect can be troubled, shaken, even temporarily deceived, but they cannot finally be led astray, because the Shepherd keeps His sheep. Forewarned is forearmed: Jesus tells us beforehand so that when the storm comes, faith will recognize the weather and stand.

The coming of the Son of Man (13:24–27). "But in those days, after that tribulation, the sun will be darkened, and the moon will not give its light, and the stars will be falling from heaven, and the powers in the heavens will be shaken." The language is Isaiah's and Joel's — the vocabulary Scripture uses for the day of the Lord, when God shakes everything that can be shaken. "And then they will see the Son of Man coming in clouds with great power and glory." This is Daniel 7:13–14, the vision behind Jesus' favorite title for Himself: one like a son of man coming with the clouds of heaven, presented before the Ancient of Days, and given dominion and glory and a kingdom that shall not be destroyed. The Servant who came in humility will return in glory — the same Jesus, the same clouds that hid Him at His ascension (Acts 1:9–11). "And then he will send out the angels and gather his elect from the four winds, from the ends of the earth to the ends of heaven." The chapter that warns of scattering ends with gathering: not one of His elect, from any corner of earth or heaven, will be missed.

The fig tree and the unknown hour (13:28–37). "From the fig tree learn its lesson: as soon as its branch becomes tender and puts out its leaves, you know that summer is near. So also, when you see these things taking place, you know that he is near, at the very gates. Truly, I say to you, this generation will not pass away until all these things take place." The generation that heard Jesus saw the temple fall, exactly as promised — the near horizon of the prophecy. "Heaven and earth will pass away, but my words will not pass away." The temple's stones are gone; the words outlasted them. Every generation since has tested that claim and found it true.

"But concerning that day or that hour, no one knows, not even the angels in heaven, nor the Son, but only the Father." In His incarnation, the Son — who is truly God and truly man — speaks from within the limits of His human mission: the hour is the Father's to know and to appoint. This verse has humbled date-setters for two thousand years, and it is meant to. The church is not told when; the church is told what to do: "Be on guard, keep awake. For you do not know when the time will come. It is like a man going on a journey, when he leaves home and puts his servants in charge, each with his work, and commands the doorkeeper to stay awake. Therefore stay awake — for you do not know when the master of the house will come, in the evening, or at midnight, or when the rooster crows, or in the morning — lest he come suddenly and find you asleep." The Greek word is grēgoreō — to be awake, alert, on watch. Mark's readers in Rome would feel the night watches named here — evening, midnight, rooster-crow, morning — because Mark's own Gospel is structured by them: at evening Jesus was betrayed, at midnight He prayed and was arrested, at the rooster's crow Peter denied Him, and in the morning He was crucified. Watchfulness is not speculation about dates. It is faithfulness at your post, in the dark, until the Master comes. "And what I say to you I say to all: Stay awake."

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Mark 13 is sobering: deception will multiply, nations will rage, families will split over Christ, His people will be hated by all, and tribulation will come such as the world has never seen. Jesus does not promise His church a soft road through history — He promises a hard one, and He tells us beforehand so the hardness will not destroy our faith. But the good news towers over it: the Lord has cut the days short for the sake of His elect, the gospel will be proclaimed to all nations, and the Son of Man is coming in clouds with great power and glory to gather His own from the ends of the earth. The Master's return is certain even if its hour is secret. Our part is not to calculate. Our part is to stay awake.

A note from the Reformers. Calvin, teaching on this chapter, warned the church against two equal errors: prying into the secret hour God has kept for Himself, and sleeping as though the Master would never come. The Reformers' posture was Mark's own — busy at the assigned work, loose-handed with earthly things, awake. That is still the whole duty of the church in one line.

Reflection question: Jesus gives His servants one standing order for the whole age: stay awake. Where has your soul fallen asleep — in prayer, in watchfulness over your own heart, in readiness for His return? What is one change you can make this week to be found awake and at your post?
