Mark 7 — The Servant Cleanses: Tradition, the Heart, and Faith Beyond Borders

Jesus in Mark — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Gospel of Mark
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Mark 7 is about what truly makes a person clean or unclean. Jesus confronts the tradition of the elders and teaches that defilement comes not from outside but from the heart. Then He proves His point by crossing borders: a Gentile mother's faith wins His praise, and a deaf man in Gentile territory hears again. Jesus reveals Himself as the One who cleanses the heart and whose mercy will not be fenced in by man-made boundaries.

Commentary

The tradition of the elders (7:1–13). Now when the Pharisees gather to Jesus, with some of the scribes who have come from Jerusalem, they see that some of His disciples eat with hands that are defiled, that is, unwashed. Mark pauses to explain the custom for his Roman readers: the Pharisees and all the Jews do not eat unless they wash their hands properly, holding to the tradition of the elders, and there are many other traditions they observe — the washing of cups and pots and copper vessels and dining couches. This washing was ceremonial, not hygienic: a ritual of purity, layered on top of God's law by human tradition. And the Pharisees and scribes ask Him, "Why do your disciples not walk according to the tradition of the elders, but eat with defiled hands?"

And Jesus answers them with Isaiah: "Well did Isaiah prophesy of you hypocrites, as it is written, 'This people honors me with their lips, but their heart is far from me; in vain do they worship me, teaching as doctrines the commandments of men.'" Isaiah 29:13, aimed across seven centuries, lands exactly here. The charge is hypocrisy: lips near, heart far; worship offered to God, doctrines manufactured by men. "You leave the commandment of God and hold to the tradition of men."

Then Jesus gives the devastating example. "For Moses said, 'Honor your father and your mother'; and, 'Whoever reviles father or mother must surely die.' But you say, 'If a man tells his father or his mother, "Whatever you would have gained from me is Corban"' (that is, given to God) — 'then you no longer permit him to do anything for his father or mother.'" Corban is a Hebrew word for an offering dedicated to God. A man could declare his property Corban — promised to the temple — and thereby excuse himself from using it to support his aging parents, while still keeping the use of it himself. Piety as an accounting trick. "Thus making void the word of God by your tradition that you have handed down. And many such things you do." Tradition is a good servant and a terrible master. When it contradicts the clear command of God — honor your father and mother — it is not reverence. It is rebellion wearing religious clothes.

What defiles a person (7:14–23). And Jesus calls the people to Him again and says to them, "Hear me, all of you, and understand: There is nothing outside a person that by going into him can defile him, but the things that come out of a person are what defile him." This is revolutionary. The whole Levitical system of clean and unclean foods was a picture-book of holiness, teaching Israel that God is holy and sin is defiling. Jesus does not trash the picture-book; He opens it to the page it was always pointing to. And when He has entered the house and left the people, His disciples ask Him about the parable. And He says to them, "Then are you also without understanding? Do you not see that whatever goes into a person from outside cannot defile him, since it enters not his heart but his stomach, and is expelled?" And Mark adds, in a parenthesis for the church: "(Thus he declared all foods clean.)" The food laws have served their purpose; in Christ, the distinction is finished.

Then comes the diagnosis of the human heart, the darkest catalog in the Gospels:

"What comes out of a person is what defiles him. For from within, out of the heart of man, come evil thoughts, sexual immorality, theft, murder, adultery, coveting, wickedness, deceit, sensuality, envy, slander, pride, foolishness. All these evil things come from within, and they defile a person." — Mark 7:20–23 (ESV)

Thirteen evils, and every one of them is manufactured in-house. The problem is not the menu, the environment, or the company we keep. The problem is the heart — ours. No washing of hands can reach it. Only a new heart can fix it, which is exactly what God promised through Ezekiel: "I will give you a new heart, and a new spirit I will put within you" (Ezekiel 36:26).

The Syrophoenician woman (7:24–30). And from there Jesus arises and goes away to the region of Tyre and Sidon — Gentile territory, north of Israel. And He enters a house and does not want anyone to know, yet He cannot be hidden. But immediately a woman whose little daughter has an unclean spirit hears of Him and comes and falls down at His feet. Now the woman is a Gentile, a Syrophoenician by birth. And she begs Him to cast the demon out of her daughter. And He says to her, "Let the children be fed first, for it is not right to take the children's bread and throw it to the dogs." Jesus speaks of His order of mission: to the Jew first, the children of the covenant at the family table. The "dogs" is a household word — little dogs, puppies under the table — and the woman hears the softness in it and answers with one of the great replies of faith in all Scripture: "Yes, Lord; yet even the dogs under the table eat the children's crumbs."

She does not argue her worthiness. She does not demand a seat at the table. She asks for a crumb — and she asks believing that a crumb from this table is more than enough. And He says to her, "For this statement you may go your way; the demon has left your daughter." And she goes home and finds the child lying in bed and the demon gone. Faith that will take crumbs from Jesus will never go away empty. The borders are already breaking: the children's bread is reaching the Gentiles, exactly as the prophets said it would.

The deaf man healed (7:31–37). Then Jesus returns from the region of Tyre and goes through Sidon to the Sea of Galilee, in the region of the Decapolis — still Gentile country. And they bring to Him a man who is deaf and has a speech impediment, and they beg Him to lay His hand on him. And taking him aside from the crowd privately, Jesus puts His fingers into his ears, and after spitting touches his tongue. And looking up to heaven, He sighs and says to him, "Ephphatha," that is, "Be opened." Ephphatha is Aramaic — again Mark preserves the very word, and translates it for his readers. And the man's ears are opened, his tongue is released, and he speaks plainly. Jesus speaks to the man in a language the man can read: touch, gesture, a look to heaven, a sigh of compassion. And He charges them to tell no one. But the more He charges them, the more zealously they proclaim it. And they are astonished beyond measure, saying:

"He has done all things well. He even makes the deaf hear and the mute speak." — Mark 7:37 (ESV)

Their words echo Isaiah 35:5–6 — "Then the eyes of the blind shall be opened, and the ears of the deaf unstopped; then shall the lame man leap like a deer, and the tongue of the mute sing for joy." The Gentiles of the Decapolis, many of them hearing the reports of the delivered demoniac from chapter 5, are confessing what Isaiah promised: when God comes to save His people, the deaf hear and the mute speak. God has come.

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Mark 7 is that our defilement is not outside us but inside us — thirteen evils flow out of the human heart, and no ritual washing, no tradition, no religious performance can clean what is within. We can honor God with our lips while our hearts are far from Him. But the good news is that Jesus has come to deal with the heart itself, and His mercy crosses every border men draw. A Gentile mother gets her daughter delivered on a crumb of faith, and a deaf man hears because Jesus sighs over him and speaks one word: "Be opened."

A note from the Reformers. This chapter was Luther's battleground. The whole Reformation turned on Jesus' charge here: men had built traditions that made void the Word of God, and Luther insisted that every human custom, however ancient and religious it looks, must bow to Scripture. That is sola scriptura in action — the Word over the tradition, every time the two collide.

Reflection question: Is there any tradition or habit in your life — even a religious one — that you hold to more tightly than the plain command of God? And what would change if you asked Jesus to cleanse your heart, not just your hands?
