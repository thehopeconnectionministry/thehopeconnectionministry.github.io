Marriage — A History

From the Garden of Eden to the Marriage Supper of the Lamb

  • Teachings
  • Church History
  • Marriage

The Hope Connection — Lewis Fuller

1. God's Idea Before It Was Man's

Marriage was not invented by a culture, a government, or a church. It was not man's idea at all. Before there was a nation, a law court, a wedding industry, or a single human tradition, there was a garden, a man who was alone, and a God who said that was not good:

"Then the LORD God said, 'It is not good that the man should be alone; I will make him a helper fit for him.'" — Genesis 2:18 (ESV)

God made the woman from the man's own side, brought her to the man, and the man woke up singing — the first words of a man about a woman recorded in Scripture are a love poem: "This at last is bone of my bones and flesh of my flesh" (Genesis 2:23, ESV). Then comes the sentence on which every marriage in history stands:

"Therefore a man shall leave his father and his mother and hold fast to his wife, and they shall become one flesh." — Genesis 2:24 (ESV)

Notice the order of things. Marriage comes before the fall, before sin, before shame — the very next verse says the man and his wife were naked and not ashamed. Marriage is not a remedy God patched together after things went wrong. It is part of how things were right. It belongs to the "very good" of creation. Every wedding since Eden, in every culture on earth, is an echo of that first one — whether the people at it know it or not.

That matters for how we read the whole history that follows. Marriage has been honored and distorted, protected and cheapened, celebrated and redefined across five thousand years. But underneath all of it stands a design that no age invented and no age has managed to improve: one man and one woman, leaving and cleaving, one flesh, for life.

Male and female, blessed together. The first page of the Bible sets marriage inside an even larger frame. "God created man in his own image, in the image of God he created him; male and female he created them. And God blessed them. And God said to them, 'Be fruitful and multiply and fill the earth and subdue it'" (Genesis 1:27–28, ESV). Humanity bears God's image as male and female together, and the first blessing God ever spoke over human beings was spoken over that pair — fruitfulness, work, and dominion, given to them jointly. Children, then, are not an accident that sometimes happens to a marriage; in the design, they are part of its blessing, received as gifts ("Behold, children are a heritage from the LORD," Psalm 127:3, ESV), even as Scripture tenderly honors couples who bear the sorrow of barrenness — Sarah, Rebekah, Rachel, Hannah, Elizabeth, a roll call of women God loved and remembered. Companionship and fruitfulness, covenant and cradle: Genesis holds them together, and so should we.

2. Marriage in Genesis: One Flesh and a Covenant

The book of Genesis does not merely introduce marriage. It builds the pattern every later chapter of the Bible assumes.

One flesh. The union of husband and wife is not a contract for services; it is the making of a new family unit, a oneness that is physical, emotional, and spiritual at once. The man leaves his father and mother — the strongest natural bond of the ancient world — and holds fast to his wife. That phrase, "hold fast," is covenant language. It is the word used elsewhere for clinging to God Himself (Deuteronomy 10:20). Marriage, from its first page, is not a feeling that comes and goes. It is a clinging that holds.

A covenant, not merely a ceremony. Centuries later, the prophet Malachi would look back at marriages in Israel and name what made them sacred — and what made betrayal of them so serious:

"She is your companion and your wife by covenant." — Malachi 2:14 (ESV)

A companion and a wife by covenant. Both words matter. Marriage is friendship and covenant together — warmth and vow, affection and promise. God Himself, Malachi says, was the witness between a man and the wife of his youth. The covenant is made before God, and God keeps the record.

The pattern worked out in lives. Genesis shows the pattern in flesh and blood. Isaac and Rebekah: a bride brought from a far country, and a man who "took Rebekah, and she became his wife, and he loved her" (Genesis 24:67, ESV) — love following commitment, growing inside the covenant rather than being required to justify it. Jacob and Rachel: love at first sight at a well, and a bridegroom so in love that seven years of labor "seemed to him but a few days because of the love he had for her" (Genesis 29:20, ESV). Even in the garden's aftermath, with sin already in the world, Genesis holds up marriages marked by tenderness, loyalty, and desire inside the covenant — a picture of what marriage was made to be.

The pattern under strain. Genesis is equally honest that these were marriages between sinners. Abraham, the father of the faithful, twice put his wife Sarah in danger by calling her his sister to protect himself (Genesis 12 and 20) — and God protected Sarah anyway, because the covenant did not rest on Abraham's courage. Sarah laughed at God's promise; Rebekah and Jacob together deceived the blind Isaac; Rachel stole her father's household gods and lied about it. The point is not that these marriages were models in every detail. The point is that God's covenant faithfulness ran underneath flawed marriages and carried them. That is a comfort that has not expired: the strength of a biblical marriage was never the perfection of the two people in it. It was, and is, the promise of the God who witnessed it.

3. Marriage in the Old Testament: Feasts, Vows, and Hard Places

Betrothal and the mohar. In ancient Israel, a marriage began with betrothal — a binding agreement between families, sealed with a payment called the mohar, the bride price, paid by the groom or his family to the bride's father. The mohar was not the purchase of a woman; it was a serious pledge, compensation to her family, and in many cases a provision held for the bride's own security. Jacob paid his mohar in seven years of labor. David's story shows the mohar could also be demanded in dangerous deeds (1 Samuel 18). Betrothal was so binding that a betrothed woman was already called a wife, and breaking a betrothal required a divorce (Deuteronomy 22:23–29; compare Matthew 1:19, where Joseph, betrothed to Mary, resolves to divorce her quietly).

The wedding feast. When the wedding came, it came with feasting that could last a full week. When Laban tricked Jacob with Leah instead of Rachel, his excuse tells us the custom: "Complete the week of this one, and we will give you the other also" (Genesis 29:27, ESV) — the bridal week, seven days of celebration for one bride before another matter could even be discussed. Samson's wedding feast ran seven days, with thirty companions and a riddle wagered over it (Judges 14:12). Weddings were the great joy-events of village life: music, feasting, processions, the whole community drawn in. When Jeremiah wanted an image for joy restored, he reached for "the voice of mirth and the voice of gladness, the voice of the bridegroom and the voice of the bride" (Jeremiah 33:11, ESV).

The hard places, told honestly. The Old Testament also records what sin did to marriage, and it records it without blinking. The patriarchs themselves took multiple wives — Abraham with Hagar at Sarah's urging, Jacob with Leah and Rachel and their servants, and later David and Solomon on a royal scale. Scripture records polygamy; it never commands it, and it never hides its fruit. Every polygamous household in Genesis is a household of rivalry, jealousy, and tears — Sarah and Hagar, Leah and Rachel competing with mandrakes and maids, Hannah weeping before a taunting rival wife. The narrative itself preaches the sermon: this was not the design, and it never once brought peace. "From the beginning it was not so," Jesus would later say of every departure from Eden's pattern.

God's law around marriage. The Law of Moses did not only regulate marriage; it protected the vulnerable inside it. A new husband was exempt from military service for a full year "to be happy with his wife whom he has taken" (Deuteronomy 24:5, ESV) — the first year of marriage was protected time, by law. A wife's vows and standing were guarded; a husband who slandered his bride's honor faced public discipline (Deuteronomy 22:13–19). And in the law of the kinsman-redeemer, marriage became the instrument of one of the Old Testament's most beautiful stories: Ruth, a widowed Moabite with nothing, gleaning in the fields of Boaz, a godly man who redeemed her from poverty and married her — and in doing so gave her a place in the genealogy of Jesus Christ Himself (Matthew 1:5). A foreign widow, gathered in by a redeemer's marriage: the Old Testament was preaching the gospel in wedding clothes centuries before Bethlehem. Moses, governing a sinful people, permitted divorce and regulated it — a certificate, in writing, so a discarded wife would not be left without protection or recourse (Deuteronomy 24:1–4). Jesus, asked about it centuries later, explained exactly what that permission was and was not:

"He said to them, 'Because of your hardness of heart Moses allowed you to divorce your wives, but from the beginning it was not so.'" — Matthew 19:8 (ESV)

The permission was a concession to sin, not a statement of the design. Meanwhile, God used marriage itself as His great picture of His love for His people: Israel the unfaithful bride, Hosea commanded to marry a woman who would betray him, so that Israel could see its own sin — and see, in Hosea's relentless pursuing love, the heart of God who would not let His bride go (Hosea 1–3). And in the Song of Solomon, married love itself is sung without embarrassment — desire inside covenant, celebrated as God's good gift.

4. Jewish Weddings in Jesus' Day: The Bridegroom Is Coming

To understand the New Testament's wedding language — and Jesus used it constantly — you have to stand inside a first-century Jewish wedding. It unfolded in two great stages, often separated by a year or more.

The betrothal (erusin, also called kiddushin). The betrothal was not an engagement in the modern, breakable sense. It was marriage in law, lacking only the home-taking. The groom's family paid the mohar; a written marriage contract, the ketubah, spelled out the groom's obligations and the provision set aside for the bride; the couple drank from a shared cup of wine and the betrothal blessing was spoken. From that moment they were husband and wife in the eyes of the law — unfaithfulness during betrothal was adultery, and only a formal divorce could end it. This is the exact situation of Joseph and Mary: betrothed, legally bound, and Joseph facing what he believed was unfaithfulness, resolving "to divorce her quietly" before the angel spoke (Matthew 1:18–20).

The waiting year. Then the groom went home — to his father's house — to prepare the place where the couple would live, often building rooms onto the family home. The bride waited, kept herself ready, and did not know the day or the hour of his return; traditionally, the father of the groom decided when the preparations were complete and the time had come. If you have ever wondered where Jesus got the pattern of "I go to prepare a place for you" and "concerning that day and hour no one knows, not even the Son, but the Father only" (John 14:2; Matthew 24:36), you are looking at it: it is the language of a Jewish bridegroom, and every listener in Galilee would have known it.

The coming at night. When the father gave the word, the groom came for his bride — usually at night, often at midnight — with a procession of friends, torches and lamps, and a shout. A shofar, the ram's horn, could sound the announcement: the bridegroom is coming. The bride had to be ready then — dressed, lamp lit, oil in hand — because the procession would not wait. This is the living scene behind the parable of the ten virgins: five wise with oil in their lamps, five foolish knocking on a shut door, and the sobering verdict, "I do not know you" (Matthew 25:1–13). It is also behind the apostle Paul's picture of the Lord descending "with a cry of command, with the voice of an archangel, and with the sound of the trumpet of God" (1 Thessalonians 4:16, ESV). The church is a bride waiting for a midnight shout.

The home-taking and the feast (nisuin). The groom brought his bride to his father's house under the chuppah, the wedding canopy, where vows and blessings completed the union; then came the marriage feast — days of it, the best celebration a family could give. When Jesus turned water into wine at Cana, it was at such a feast, and the detail John gives is a window into the custom: the steward of the feast expected the good wine first and the poorer wine after the guests had drunk freely, which is why heaven's wine arriving last drew his astonished comment (John 2:1–10). And when John the Baptist called himself "the friend of the bridegroom" who "rejoices greatly at the bridegroom's voice" (John 3:29, ESV), he was naming a real wedding office — the groom's attendant and go-between — and placing himself gladly in it: the friend steps back, because the bride belongs to the Bridegroom.

5. Jesus on Marriage: Back to the Beginning

When the Pharisees came to Jesus with the divorce question — the great test case of their debates — Jesus refused to argue from Moses' concession. He went back behind Moses, behind the fall, to the garden:

"He answered, 'Have you not read that he who created them from the beginning made them male and female, and said, "Therefore a man shall leave his father and his mother and hold fast to his wife, and the two shall become one flesh"? So they are no longer two but one flesh. What therefore God has joined together, let not man separate.'" — Matthew 19:4–6 (ESV)

Every phrase is loaded. God joins a husband and wife — marriage is His work, not merely their arrangement. What God has joined, man must not separate — divorce is therefore not mainly a legal event but a tearing of something God Himself made one. And the standard is not the culture's practice or even Moses' regulation; it is creation itself, "from the beginning." Jesus' own disciples, hearing it, gasped that it might be better not to marry at all (Matthew 19:10) — which tells us Jesus had not lowered the bar. He had raised it back to where God set it.

Yet the same Jesus who guarded marriage so strictly also honored it with His presence and His first miracle at a wedding feast, wept with the broken, and received sinners whose marriages and lives lay in ruins — the woman at the well, with five husbands and a man who was not her husband, received from Him not a lecture first but living water (John 4). In Jesus, the creation standard and the sinner's welcome stand together. That is the pattern for everything the church should say about marriage to this day: hold the design high, and meet the broken low, right where they are.

Jesus also taught that marriage belongs to this age. In the resurrection, He said, "they neither marry nor are given in marriage, but are like angels in heaven" (Matthew 22:30, ESV) — not because marriage is small, but because it is a signpost, and signposts are not needed once you have arrived. Marriage points beyond itself, to a union it can only picture. The New Testament's last book shows us the destination.

6. Paul on Marriage: A Picture of Christ and the Church

The apostle Paul gave marriage its highest dignity and its clearest job description in one passage. After instructing wives and husbands, he turns the whole institution into a sermon:

"Husbands, love your wives, as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her, that he might sanctify her, having cleansed her by the washing of water with the word, so that he might present the church to himself in splendor, without spot or wrinkle or any such thing, that she might be holy and without blemish." — Ephesians 5:25–27 (ESV)

The husband's love is to be cross-shaped: not domination, not indulgence, but self-giving — a man laying down his life, his preferences, his pride, daily, for the good of his wife, the way Christ laid down His life for the church. The wife's part is set in the same frame: a glad, trusting response to a love that can be trusted, the way the church responds to Christ. Then Paul reaches back to Eden, quotes Genesis 2:24, and pulls back the curtain:

"'Therefore a man shall leave his father and mother and hold fast to his wife, and the two shall become one flesh.' This mystery is profound, and I am saying that it refers to Christ and the church." — Ephesians 5:31–32 (ESV)

It was always a picture. Every marriage since Eden has been a living parable of Christ and His bride — which is why marriage matters so much, and why its distortions grieve God so much. A husband's cruelty or a wife's contempt does not merely break a home; it tells a lie about the gospel in front of a watching world. And a marriage marked by sacrifice, forgiveness, and faithfulness preaches, without a word, the truest sermon some neighbors will ever hear.

Paul is honest about the rest, too. In 1 Corinthians 7 he addresses the unmarried and the widowed and calls singleness a genuine gift, not a waiting room — a life of undivided devotion that the married, with their divided interests and worldly anxieties, cannot fully give. He counsels believers married to unbelievers to stay and be a sanctifying presence where they can (1 Corinthians 7:12–16). He insists marriage is honorable and its bed undefiled (Hebrews 13:4), forbids spouses from withholding themselves from one another, and treats the whole subject with a pastor's realism: marriage is a gift, a calling, a school of holiness — and "those who marry will have worldly troubles" (1 Corinthians 7:28, ESV). Scripture never sells marriage as a fairy tale. It honors it as a covenant in a fallen world, sustained by grace.

And it all ends at a wedding. The book of Revelation closes history with the feast every Jewish bride waited for, on a scale no village could hold:

"Let us rejoice and exult and give him the glory, for the marriage of the Lamb has come, and his Bride has made herself ready; it was granted her to clothe herself with fine linen, bright and pure." — Revelation 19:7–8 (ESV)

"And the angel said to me, 'Write this: Blessed are those who are invited to the marriage supper of the Lamb.'" — Revelation 19:9 (ESV)

The Bridegroom who went to His Father's house to prepare a place is coming back — with the shout, at the hour the Father appoints — and the feast that follows will make every wedding feast in history look like a rehearsal. Because it was.

7. Marriage Through Church History: Honor, Error, and Recovery

The early church. The first Christians honored marriage in a world that often did not. Against a Roman culture of easy divorce, discarded wives, and the sexual use of slaves and children, the church insisted that marriage was holy, that husbands owed their wives exclusive faithfulness — the same standard as wives owed husbands, which was revolutionary — and that the marriage bed was honorable. Very early, Christians began marrying "in the Lord" and in the presence of the church: Ignatius of Antioch, writing around the year 110, urged that marriages be made with the bishop's knowledge and approval, so that the union would be "according to the Lord." In the centuries that followed, Christian weddings took on their own beauty — the joining of hands, the veiling of the bride, crowns placed on the heads of bride and groom as signs of victory and of the martyr's crown, and the blessing of the couple within the worship of the church. The church was saying something with all of it: marriage is not a private arrangement the church tolerates, but a holy calling it celebrates. Church fathers like Augustine defended marriage's goodness with its threefold gifts: children, faithfulness, and the sacred bond itself. Yet a shadow crept in early: an idea, borrowed partly from the surrounding philosophy, that celibacy was inherently holier than marriage, and that the married state was a concession to weakness. The apostle Paul had called singleness a gift for some; the later church slowly turned it into a rank above the rest.

The medieval church. By the Middle Ages, that shadow had hardened into system. The Western church numbered marriage among its seven sacraments — which honored it in one sense — but simultaneously forbade marriage to its clergy, a prohibition enforced from the eleventh and twelfth centuries onward (the Second Lateran Council, in 1139, declared clerical marriages invalid). The result was a two-tier church: a celibate priesthood set above ordinary believers, and marriage treated as a lesser, slightly suspect estate, permitted to the laity because they could not manage better. Many priests kept unofficial wives anyway, in open secret, and everyone knew it. The institution God called "very good" had become, in the church's own ranking, second class.

The Reformation's recovery. The Reformers attacked this at the root — not by lowering holiness, but by returning to Scripture. Marriage, they taught, is not a sacrament that confers grace; it is a creation ordinance of God, holy in itself, for all men and not a fallback for the weak. And they proved it with their own lives. In 1525, Martin Luther — a former monk — married Katharina von Bora, a former nun who had escaped her convent hidden in a fish barrel. Luther called his marriage a school of character, joked that God had given him a wife to humble his pride, and their parsonage at Wittenberg — crowded with children, students, refugees, and guests at a famous dinner table — became the model of the Protestant home: marriage not as retreat from ministry, but as a theater of it. John Calvin married Idelette de Bure and grieved her deeply at her early death; Zwingli, Knox, and the rest married likewise. The parsonage replaced the monastery as the picture of the Christian household: ordinary, open, hospitable, and holy.

The Puritan home. The Puritans carried the recovery to its warmest expression. Far from the dour caricature of later centuries, Puritan writers celebrated married love — including its physical joys — as one of God's sweetest earthly gifts, and called the Christian household "a little church": a home ordered by Scripture, prayer, catechizing of children and servants, and Sabbath rest. Writers like William Gouge, whose Of Domestical Duties appeared in 1622, gave husbands and wives detailed, tender instruction in mutual love and duty. The Puritan legacy — marriage as covenant friendship, the home as a school of faith — flowed into the evangelical tradition and shapes Christian homes to this day.

The words we still say. One more gift of that age deserves naming: the wedding service itself. The marriage liturgy Thomas Cranmer placed in the Book of Common Prayer in 1549 gave English-speaking couples the words still spoken at weddings today — vows taken "for better for worse, for richer for poorer, in sickness and in health," the declaration that marriage is "an honourable estate, instituted of God in the time of man's innocency," and the charge, drawn from Scripture, that those whom God has joined together no man should put asunder. When a couple stands at an altar today and promises themselves to each other in those cadences, they are speaking Reformation words — the recovery of biblical marriage, set to the language of ordinary people, exactly as the Reformers intended.

8. Marriage Today: Pressure, Brokenness, and Hope

Any honest history must end by looking at the present day, and the present day is hard on marriage. The age has redefined it by law and custom, treating it as a contract of personal fulfillment — kept while it satisfies, dissolved when it does not. Divorce is easy and common; many young people, having watched their parents' marriages fail, postpone marriage or avoid it altogether; loneliness is epidemic even inside marriages that survive. The covenant idea itself — a promise kept when feelings change — sounds to many modern ears like a trap rather than a shelter.

And yet the design keeps asserting itself. People still long to be known fully and loved anyway, to be clung to and not let go, to build one life out of two. They long, whether they know it or not, for Genesis 2:24. The ache itself is a witness.

Here the law must speak first, the way it always must. God's standard for marriage has not moved, and measured against it, every one of us stands guilty somewhere — in lust or in coldness, in promises thinned by selfishness, in homes we have failed or homes we have fled. Some who read this carry a failed marriage; some carry wounds inflicted by someone else's failure; some carry the quieter guilt of a marriage neglected one ordinary day at a time. Marriage law, honestly preached, leaves no one standing tall.

But the law is not the last word — it never is. The God who designed marriage also sent a Bridegroom who pursues a bride who has not been faithful. That is the Bible's own central love story: Hosea buying back his unfaithful wife; Christ giving Himself up for a church that daily gives Him reasons to leave, and does not leave. There is no marriage so broken, and no person so broken, that the grace of that Bridegroom cannot reach. Repentance is real. Forgiveness is real. New beginnings inside old covenants are real — and where a covenant lies in ruins beyond repair, the God who was witness to it remains the companion of the one left standing (Malachi 2:14). The church's word to a divorce-scarred world is not first a wagging finger. It is the same word it is to every sinner: come as you are, and be met with grace and truth in the name of Jesus.

For marriages that stand, the history in these pages is a summons and an encouragement. You are not maintaining a private arrangement; you are stewarding the oldest institution on earth and preaching the newest mercy in it. Hold fast. Forgive quickly. Keep the feast. The eyes of your children, your neighbors, and a watching world are on the picture you are painting of Christ and His church.

And for those whose story runs outside marriage — the single, the widowed, the ones who hoped and were not chosen — this history has a word too. Marriage is a picture, and no picture is the thing itself. The reality it points to, union with Christ, is not rationed to the married. The Lord who was Himself single on this earth has never once treated the unmarried as second-class citizens of His kingdom, and the church must not either. The marriage supper of the Lamb has a seat for every believer, and no one will sit at that table as anybody's leftover. In the meantime, the household of faith is a real family, and it is the church's joyful duty to make it feel like one.

9. Closing: The First Wedding and the Last

The Bible opens with a wedding in a garden and closes with a wedding in a city. Between them runs the whole story of the world: a design given, a design marred, a Bridegroom sent, a bride prepared. Every marriage in between — the feasts of Canaan and the parsonages of Wittenberg, the cottage weddings of peasants and the quiet vows spoken in hospital chapels — is a verse in that one long song.

That is why an old wedding ring, worn thin by fifty years on one hand, preaches better than most sermons. It says the design held. It says two sinners kept clinging when clinging was hard, forgave when forgiving cost something, and found — on the far side of ordinary faithfulness — that God had been keeping them both the whole time. Not every marriage is given fifty years, and no marriage is given a single day without grace. But every day of it is held by the same hand that joined the first pair in Eden and will seat the last bride at the Lamb's table.

Lewis and Rachel will mark their first wedding anniversary this December, and their marriage, like every marriage in this history, is one more small picture of the great one. That is all any of ours are, and it is no small thing to be.

A closing prayer. Father, You thought of marriage before we thought of anything, and You have kept it through every age that honored it and every age that tried to throw it away. Bless the marriages of Your people: make husbands brave in sacrifice and wives glad in trust, make homes into little churches, and make our covenants preach Christ. For the broken, be the Bridegroom who does not walk away. For the waiting, be enough. And bring us all at last to the marriage supper of the Lamb, where the picture gives way to the reality and the feast never ends. In Jesus' name, Amen.
