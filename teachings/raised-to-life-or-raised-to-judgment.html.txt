<h1 style="color:var(--navy)">Raised to Life, or Raised to Judgment</h1>
<h2 class="season-h">Eternal Life, the Second Death, and the Wrath of God</h2>
<p><em>A study piece for The Hope Connection — built from Lewis's questions, November 16, 2026. All Scripture ESV.</em></p>
<h2 class="season-h">1. Everyone exists forever — but not everyone lives</h2>
<p>It starts with an honest question: don't unbelievers live forever too, just in torment?</p>
<p>In one sense, yes. Existence does not end for anybody. Jesus said the hour is coming when <em>all</em> who are in the tombs will hear His voice and come out — "those who have done good to the resurrection of life, and those who have done evil to the resurrection of judgment" (John 5:28–29). Daniel saw the same day: "many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt" (Daniel 12:2).</p>
<p>And Matthew 25:46 puts the two destinies side by side with the same word for both: "these will go away into <strong>eternal</strong> punishment, but the righteous into <strong>eternal</strong> life." As long as the life lasts, the punishment lasts.</p>
<p>But notice: Scripture never calls the condemned man's state <em>life</em>. Eternal life is a defined thing — "this is eternal life, that they know you, the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom you have sent" (John 17:3). Life, in the Bible's language, is existence <em>with</em> God, in fellowship with Him. The condemned exist forever — but what they have is not called life. It is called death.</p>
<h2 class="season-h">2. The condemned do not die — because death was never about ceasing to exist</h2>
<p>Here is the key that unlocks the whole subject: in Scripture, death never meant <em>not existing</em>. Death means <strong>separation</strong>.</p>
<p>God told Adam, "in the day that you eat of it you shall surely die" (Genesis 2:17) — and Adam lived physically for centuries afterward. He died that day all the same: spiritually, cut off from God. Paul describes living, breathing unbelievers as "dead in the trespasses and sins in which you once walked" (Ephesians 2:1). A man can be dead while he is walking around, because death is separation from the God who is life.</p>
<p>So the unbeliever dies three ways, and none of them is the end of him:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Spiritual death</strong> — already true now, by nature (Ephesians 2:1–3).</li>
<li><strong>Physical death</strong> — the soul separated from the body.</li>
<li><strong>The second death</strong> — the final state: "the lake of fire" (Revelation 20:14), separation from God's favorable presence made permanent and unchangeable.</li>
</ul>
<p>The second death is not the end of existence. It is existence with the separation never healed. "They will suffer the punishment of eternal destruction, away from the presence of the Lord and from the glory of his might" (2 Thessalonians 1:9).</p>
<h2 class="season-h">3. And yet — God is still there</h2>
<p>That raises the next question: if hell is separation from God, how can it be separation, when God is everywhere?</p>
<p>He is still there. "If I make my bed in Sheol, you are there!" (Psalm 139:8). There is no place in creation where God is not. Revelation even says the condemned are tormented "in the presence of the holy angels and in the presence of the Lamb" (Revelation 14:10).</p>
<p>So hell is not God's absence. It is God's presence in one mode only: <strong>wrath, without grace</strong>. On earth, even the unbeliever experiences God's presence as kindness — sun and rain, food and family, and one more day to repent. In hell, the kindness is withdrawn. What remains is His presence as Judge, with nothing standing between the sinner and it. "Away from the presence of the Lord" means away from His <em>face in favor</em> — never away from His reach.</p>
<h2 class="season-h">4. The wrath of God</h2>
<p>God's wrath is His settled, holy hatred of sin. The Greek word is <em>orgē</em> — not a flare-up or a loss of temper, but a fixed, abiding opposition. God does not <em>become</em> angry the way men do; His wrath is what His holiness looks like when it faces evil. A God who could watch cruelty and rebellion and feel nothing would not be loving — He would be indifferent. Wrath is the proof He is not.</p>
<p><strong>It is revealed now.</strong> "For the wrath of God is revealed from heaven against all ungodliness and unrighteousness of men" (Romans 1:18) — present tense. In Romans 1 it works by handing men over to the sin they insist on: sometimes judgment is not God striking a man down, but God letting him have his own way and reap what grows from it. History carries the same marks — the Flood, Sodom, the plagues, the exile. "God is a righteous judge, and a God who feels indignation every day" (Psalm 7:11).</p>
<p><strong>It is slow, and it is stored.</strong> The first words God ever spoke about His own character: "The LORD, the LORD, a God merciful and gracious, <strong>slow to anger</strong>, and abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness" (Exodus 34:6). Isaiah calls judgment God's "strange work" — His <em>alien</em> work (Isaiah 28:21). Mercy is His delight; wrath is His necessity. "I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked, but that the wicked turn from his way and live" (Ezekiel 33:11). But slow is not absent. To the hard-hearted Paul says: "you are storing up wrath for yourself on the day of wrath when God's righteous judgment will be revealed" (Romans 2:5). Every unrepented sin is a deposit, and it compounds.</p>
<p><strong>It is coming in full.</strong> "Whoever does not obey the Son shall not see life, but the wrath of God <strong>remains</strong> on him" (John 3:36) — remains, because by nature we are "children of wrath" (Ephesians 2:3). Revelation calls the last day "the great day of their wrath" and asks, "who can stand?" (Revelation 6:17). And the most sobering phrase in all of Scripture: "the wrath of <strong>the Lamb</strong>" (Revelation 6:16). The gentle Jesus of the Gospels is the Judge of the last day — and His wrath is terrible precisely because His mercy was refused for so long.</p>
<h2 class="season-h">5. One resurrection, two outcomes</h2>
<p>Scripture presents one bodily resurrection with two different outcomes — not two kinds of bodies made by two different powers. God raises all the dead, and Christ Himself executes the Father's judgment.</p>
<p><strong>The resurrection of life.</strong> Christ was raised first, as the "firstfruits" (1 Corinthians 15:20–23). Those who belong to Him are raised at His coming, and their bodies are made imperishable, glorious, powerful, and immortal (1 Corinthians 15:42–54). This is physical — redemption includes the body. Believers are transformed to bear the image of the heavenly Christ, and God dwells with them in the renewed creation (1 Corinthians 15:49; Revelation 21:1–4).</p>
<p><strong>The resurrection of judgment.</strong> Paul confessed "a resurrection of both the just and the unjust" (Acts 24:15). Revelation 20 shows the dead standing before the great white throne, judged according to their works — works that do not earn the condemnation, but publicly display the guilt that was stored up all along (Romans 2:5–8). Those not found in the book of life are cast into the lake of fire, the second death.</p>
<p>The contrast is plain: believers are raised to everlasting life with God through Christ; unbelievers are raised to final judgment and everlasting punishment. Christ's own resurrection guarantees the first for His people, and His authority guarantees the second (Acts 17:30–31). For the clearest map, read John 5, 1 Corinthians 15, and Revelation 20–21 together.</p>
<h2 class="season-h">6. The cross — where the wrath fell, so it would not fall on us</h2>
<p>None of this is the end of the story, because God did not waive His wrath for sinners. He <em>spent</em> it — on His Son.</p>
<p>Christ is the "propitiation" (Romans 3:25): the sacrifice that absorbs God's wrath and turns it away from us. That is why Jesus prayed over a "cup" in Gethsemane (Matthew 26:39) — the cup of wrath the prophets described (Jeremiah 25:15). He drank it dry. "Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us" (Galatians 3:13). "It was the will of the LORD to crush him" (Isaiah 53:10). The cry from the cross — "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" — is the sound of wrath falling where it had to fall, so that it would never fall on His people.</p>
<p>So for the believer, the matter is closed: "There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus" (Romans 8:1). "God has not destined us for wrath, but to obtain salvation through our Lord Jesus Christ" (1 Thessalonians 5:9). God never punishes the same sin twice — it was borne by Christ, or it will be borne by the sinner, never both. When a believer is corrected, that is a Father's discipline (Hebrews 12), not a Judge's wrath. The gavel already fell — on the cross.</p>
<p><strong>The good news in the bad news.</strong> Wrath is real, it is present, it is coming, and it is deserved — there is no honest gospel without that. But the same God whose wrath we earned put forward His own Son to bear it. "Since, therefore, we have now been justified by his blood, much more shall we be saved by him from the wrath of God" (Romans 5:9). Salvation is entirely grace: sinners cannot rescue themselves, so God sovereignly saves His people through Christ's atoning death, and those He saves He preserves to everlasting glory (1 Peter 1:3–5).</p>
<p>The only safe place from the wrath of God is the Son of God — and the door is open now, while His anger is still slow and His patience still holds.</p>
<h2 class="season-h">Reflect — Questions &amp; Answers</h2>
<p><strong>Q: If unbelievers exist forever, why doesn't the Bible call it eternal life?</strong><br>
A: Because in Scripture, life means knowing and enjoying God (John 17:3). The condemned exist forever under judgment — a state the Bible calls the second death (Revelation 20:14), not life.</p>
<p><strong>Q: Is hell God's absence?</strong><br>
A: No. God is present everywhere (Psalm 139:8), and the torments of hell take place "in the presence of the Lamb" (Revelation 14:10). Hell is God's presence in wrath only — His favorable presence, His kindness and grace, withdrawn forever (2 Thessalonians 1:9).</p>
<p><strong>Q: If I am in Christ, is any of God's wrath left for me?</strong><br>
A: None. Christ bore it all as our propitiation (Romans 3:25), so "there is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus" (Romans 8:1), and God has "not destined us for wrath" (1 Thessalonians 5:9).</p>
