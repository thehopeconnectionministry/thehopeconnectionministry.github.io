Romans 8 — Life in the Spirit: No Condemnation, No Separation

Jesus in Romans — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Epistle to the Romans
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Jesus is the ground, the pattern, and the guarantee of everything in this greatest of chapters. Because we are in Christ Jesus, there is no condemnation (8:1). God sent His own Son in the likeness of sinful flesh and condemned sin in His flesh, so that the law's righteous requirement is fulfilled in us (8:3–4). Christ Jesus is the one who died, who was raised, and who now intercedes for us at the right hand of God (8:34) — our defense when accusations fly. We are being conformed to His image, so that He is the firstborn among many brothers (8:29). And the chapter ends with His love as the unbreakable boundary of the universe: nothing "will be able to separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord" (8:39). Romans 8 is not finally about our experience. It is about Christ — and because it is about Christ, it becomes about our experience, secure forever.

Commentary

No condemnation (8:1–4). Chapter 7 ended with a cry; chapter 8 opens with a verdict, and it is the verdict the whole letter has been traveling toward:

"There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus." — Romans 8:1 (ESV)

No condemnation — not less condemnation, not postponed condemnation, none. Now — not at death, not at the judgment, now, already, in the present tense. For those who are in Christ Jesus — the location is everything; outside Him the condemnation stands, in Him it is gone forever. "For the law of the Spirit of life has set you free in Christ Jesus from the law of sin and death" (8:2) — one law overruled by a greater law, the way lift overrules gravity for the eagle: gravity is not abolished, but it is answered. Then Paul gives the gospel's engine room in two verses: "For God has done what the law, weakened by the flesh, could not do. By sending his own Son in the likeness of sinful flesh and for sin, he condemned sin in the flesh, in order that the righteous requirement of the law might be fulfilled in us, who walk not according to the flesh but according to the Spirit" (8:3–4). God did what the law could not. He sent His own Son — truly God, truly man, in the likeness of sinful flesh but without sin — and on the cross He condemned sin, not the sinner who believes: the sentence fell on the sin in Christ's flesh so that it would never fall on us. And the purpose is positive, not merely negative: the law's righteous requirement fulfilled in us — the justified become people who actually walk in the Spirit's way. Romans 8 does not lower chapter 7's standard; it supplies chapter 7's missing power.

The mind set on the Spirit (8:5–11). Two mindsets divide mankind: "For those who live according to the flesh set their minds on the things of the flesh, but those who live according to the Spirit set their minds on the things of the Spirit. For to set the mind on the flesh is death, but to set the mind on the Spirit is life and peace" (8:5–6). The mind is the battlefield of Romans 7, and here is its occupation. The fleshly mind "is hostile to God, for it does not submit to God's law; indeed, it cannot. Those who are in the flesh cannot please God" (8:7–8). But — and the turn is personal — "You, however, are not in the flesh but in the Spirit, if in fact the Spirit of God dwells in you. Anyone who does not have the Spirit of Christ does not belong to him" (8:9). The Spirit's indwelling is the mark of belonging. And the indwelling Spirit is resurrection power on layaway: "If the Spirit of him who raised Jesus from the dead dwells in you, he who raised Christ Jesus from the dead will also give life to your mortal bodies through his Spirit who dwells in you" (8:11). The body that wars in Romans 7 will be raised by the same Spirit who wars for us now. The war has an expiration date.

Sons, heirs, and Abba (8:12–17). So we are debtors — not to the flesh. "For if you live according to the flesh you will die, but if by the Spirit you put to death the deeds of the body, you will live" (8:13). Mortification — putting sin to death — is real work, but it is work done by the Spirit, not by the flesh whipping itself. "For all who are led by the Spirit of God are sons of God" (8:14). And here Paul gives us one of the tenderest verses in the Bible: "For you did not receive the spirit of slavery to fall back into fear, but you have received the Spirit of adoption as sons, by whom we cry, 'Abba! Father!'" (8:15). The Greek word is huiothesia — adoption, the legal placing of a child into a family with full rights. Slavery's spirit made us cringe; adoption's Spirit makes us cry Abba — the family word, the child's word, the word Jesus Himself used in Gethsemane. "The Spirit himself bears witness with our spirit that we are children of God, and if children, then heirs — heirs of God and fellow heirs with Christ, provided we suffer with him in order that we may also be glorified with him" (8:16–17). Heirs — of God Himself, alongside Christ. And the suffering is not an exception to the inheritance; it is the road to its glory.

The groaning creation (8:18–25). Paul now lifts his eyes to the whole creation. "For I consider that the sufferings of this present time are not worth comparing with the glory that is to be revealed to us" (8:18). Creation itself waits "with eager longing for the revealing of the sons of God" — subjected to futility at the fall, groaning "in the pains of childbirth" (8:20–22). And we groan too: "we ourselves, who have the firstfruits of the Spirit, groan inwardly as we wait eagerly for adoption as sons, the redemption of our bodies" (8:23). The adoption is already ours and not yet complete — the papers are signed, the homecoming is coming. "For in this hope we were saved... But if we hope for what we do not see, we wait for it with patience" (8:24–25). Groaning, in Romans 8, is not the opposite of hope. It is hope's sound in a world not yet remade.

The Spirit intercedes, and God purposes (8:26–30). And when the groaning goes deeper than words, we are not left alone in it: "Likewise the Spirit helps us in our weakness. For we do not know what to pray for as we ought, but the Spirit himself intercedes for us with groanings too deep for words" (8:26). The believer who cannot pray is prayed for — by the Spirit, in groans God the Father understands perfectly, since He "who searches hearts knows what is the mind of the Spirit" (8:27). Then the promise that has carried more Christians through more nights than perhaps any other: "And we know that for those who love God all things work together for good, for those who are called according to his purpose" (8:28). Not all things are good; all things work together for good — the way ingredients work together in a loaf, some of them bitter alone. And behind the promise stands the unbreakable chain: "For those whom he foreknew he also predestined to be conformed to the image of his Son, in order that he might be the firstborn among many brothers. And those whom he predestined he also called, and those whom he called he also justified, and those whom he justified he also glorified" (8:29–30). Foreknown, predestined, called, justified, glorified — five links, no gaps, and the last one spoken in the past tense, as good as done. The goal of it all is not merely our rescue but our resemblance: conformed to the image of His Son.

Nothing shall separate (8:31–39). Paul closes the chapter — and the whole doctrinal heart of the letter — with a courtroom turned into a hymn. "What then shall we say to these things? If God is for us, who can be against us?" (8:31). The argument of verse 32 is unanswerable: "He who did not spare his own Son but gave him up for us all, how will he not also with him graciously give us all things?" God has already given the costliest thing; the rest is small change. Who shall bring a charge? "It is God who justifies." Who condemns? "Christ Jesus is the one who died — more than that, who was raised — who is at the right hand of God, who indeed is interceding for us" (8:33–34). The Judge has become the defense attorney, and He pleads His own wounds. Then the final roll call of every power that might separate: tribulation, distress, persecution, famine, nakedness, danger, sword — and the confession of the martyrs, "for your sake we are being killed all the day long" — answered by the verdict: "No, in all these things we are more than conquerors through him who loved us" (8:35–37). More than conquerors — not merely surviving the battle but coming out ahead of it, since even the sword works for us now. And the last word:

"For I am sure that neither death nor life, nor angels nor rulers, nor things present nor things to come, nor powers, nor height nor depth, nor anything else in all creation, will be able to separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord." — Romans 8:38–39 (ESV)

Paul ransacks the universe for a separator — death and life, angels and demons, present and future, height and depth — and closes the search with a catch-all: nor anything else in all creation. There is nothing left outside the list. The love of God in Christ Jesus is the one thing in existence that cannot be lost.

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Romans 8 is that the flesh is hopeless: the mind set on the flesh is death, hostile to God, unable to submit and unable to please Him. The law, weakened by that flesh, could show us the standard and condemn our failure, but it could not free us, change us, or raise us. Left there, chapter 7's cry is the last word. But the good news is the greatest news in the letter so far: God has done what the law could not do. There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus — the sentence fell on sin in His flesh instead. The Spirit of life has set us free, adopted us as sons, taught us to cry Abba, intercedes for us when we cannot pray, and works all things — all things — together for good for those called according to His purpose. And nothing in all creation, ransacked end to end, will be able to separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord. The chapter does not end with our grip on God. It ends with His grip on us, and His grip does not slip.

A note from the Reformers. Spurgeon called Romans 8 the believer's treasure chest and said that if he were a dying man with only one chapter to be read to him, this would be it — because it begins with no condemnation and ends with no separation, and in between it leaves no ground for a justified sinner to stand on but Christ. Read it that way: from the first verse to the last, it is all of grace.

Reflection question: Verse 1 says no condemnation, and verse 39 says no separation. Which of the two do you struggle to believe for yourself right now — and what specific accusation or fear will you answer today with God's verdict instead of your own?
