Romans 2 — Judged by the Same Standard: God's Righteous Judgment

Jesus in Romans — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Epistle to the Romans
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Jesus appears in this chapter in a role we do not often linger on: He is the Judge. "God judges the secrets of men by Christ Jesus" (2:16) — the secrets, the hidden things, the real record, judged by Christ Himself. But the same chapter shows the Savior's heart in advance: it is God's kindness, forbearance, and patience — the kindness of the Father who sent this Judge first as a Savior — that is meant to lead us to repentance (2:4). And at the chapter's end, Jesus is the one who works the true circumcision, the circumcision of the heart, by the Spirit (2:29). Judge, kindness, and heart-changer: Romans 2 will not let us have a small Christ.

Commentary

The moralist condemned by his own mouth (2:1–5). Chapter 1 ended with the debased world, and somewhere a respectable reader was nodding along — the kind of person who condemns the gutter and keeps his own record clean. Paul turns on him in the first verse: "Therefore you have no excuse, O man, every one of you who judges. For in passing judgment on another you condemn yourself, because you, the judge, practice the very same things" (2:1). The trap of chapter 1 was baited for the man of chapter 2. We know God's righteous decree against those who practice such things — and then we practice them, in politer forms: the same covetousness with better manners, the same malice in a quieter voice. "We know that the judgment of God rightly falls on those who practice such things. Do you suppose, O man — you who judge those who practice such things and yet do them yourself — that you will escape the judgment of God?" (2:2–3). There is no gallery at God's judgment, no spectator seats. And then comes one of the most searching verses in the letter:

"Or do you presume on the riches of his kindness and forbearance and patience, not knowing that God's kindness is meant to lead you to repentance?" — Romans 2:4 (ESV)

God's patience is not permission, and His kindness is not indifference. Every day God does not strike is a day He is leading — leading toward repentance, the way a shepherd leads, not the way a flood carries. To presume on that kindness, to spend patience like it is approval, is to store up wrath: "But because of your hard and impenitent heart you are storing up wrath for yourself on the day of wrath when God's righteous judgment will be revealed" (2:5). A treasury can be filled with wrath as surely as with treasure. The account is kept either way.

Judgment according to works (2:6–11). Now Paul states the principle of the coming judgment with perfect evenhandedness: God "will render to each one according to his works" (2:6). To those who by patience in well-doing seek glory and honor and immortality, He will give eternal life; to the self-seeking who do not obey the truth but obey unrighteousness, wrath and fury. Tribulation and distress for every human being who does evil, glory and honor and peace for everyone who does good — "the Jew first and also the Greek. For God shows no partiality" (2:9–11). No partiality: the Judge cannot be lobbied, and ancestry is not a defense. Is Paul teaching salvation by works? No — he is describing the standard of the judgment, the same standard he will show every mouth stopped under in chapter 3. The judgment will be according to works because works are the evidence of the heart. When chapter 3 arrives, the verdict on all flesh under this standard will be unanimous: guilty. The principle stands so that no one can claim the trial was rigged. It was not rigged. It was lost.

Law and conscience (2:12–16). All who have sinned without the law will perish without the law, and all who have sinned under the law will be judged by the law. For it is not the hearers of the law who are righteous before God, but the doers of the law who will be justified. Then Paul makes a remarkable admission about the Gentiles: when Gentiles who do not have the law by nature do what the law requires, they show that the work of the law is written on their hearts, "while their conscience also bears witness, and their conflicting thoughts accuse or even excuse them" (2:15). Conscience is God's courtroom witness planted in every chest — accusing, excusing, arguing the case in advance. And all of it points to a day: "on that day when, according to my gospel, God judges the secrets of men by Christ Jesus" (2:16). The secrets. Not the public record, not the reputation — the secrets. And the Judge of the secrets is Jesus Christ, which means the judgment is in the hands of the One who knows what it is to be human, and who — wonder of wonders — is first preached to us as Savior before He is met as Judge.

The Jew's boast examined (2:17–24). Now Paul addresses his own people directly: "But if you call yourself a Jew and rely on the law and boast in God and know his will and approve what is excellent, because you are instructed from the law..." (2:17–18). The privileges were real — the law, the instruction, the knowledge of God's will. The failure was the gap between the classroom and the life: "You who teach others, do you not teach yourself? While you preach against stealing, do you steal? You who say that one must not commit adultery, do you commit adultery? You who abhor idols, do you rob temples?" (2:21–22). And the verdict lands with a quotation from the prophets: "For, as it is written, 'The name of God is blasphemed among the Gentiles because of you'" (2:24, quoting Isaiah 52:5). The worst advertisement against God has always been the hypocrisy of His professed people. Possession of the truth is not obedience to the truth, and a Bible on the shelf judges the man who will not open it with his life.

True circumcision (2:25–29). Circumcision was the covenant sign, Israel's badge. Paul honors it and then strips it of all false confidence: "For circumcision indeed is of value if you obey the law, but if you break the law, your circumcision becomes uncircumcision" (2:25). The sign without the obedience is worse than no sign at all — it is a testimony against its bearer. And conversely, the uncircumcised man who keeps the law will be counted as circumcised, and will condemn the letter-keeping lawbreaker. Then the chapter's great redefinition: "For no one is a Jew who is merely one outwardly, nor is circumcision outward and physical. But a Jew is one inwardly, and circumcision is a matter of the heart, by the Spirit, not by the letter. His praise is not from man but from God" (2:28–29). God has always wanted the heart. The outward sign pointed to an inward work that only the Spirit can do — a cut-away heart, a heart made new. That is the true badge of God's people, in Moses' day, in Paul's day, and in ours.

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Romans 2 is that respectability is no refuge. The man who judges others condemns himself, because he practices the very same things; God's kindness, presumed upon, becomes wrath stored up; and on the coming day God will judge the secrets of men — the real record, not the reputation — by Christ Jesus, with no partiality for anyone's ancestry, church membership, or outward signs. The circumcision that matters is of the heart, and none of us can perform that surgery on ourselves. But the good news is hidden in the chapter's sharpest verse: God's kindness is meant to lead you to repentance. The patience that exposes the hypocrite is the same patience that woos the sinner. God has not yet struck because He is still leading — and the Judge of the secrets is the Savior who already knows every secret and went to the cross with them. Repentance is not the price of His kindness; it is the path His kindness is leading us down, and it is open today.

A note from the Reformers. Calvin pressed Romans 2:4 on the comfortable church of his day: God's patience is not a pillow for sinners to sleep on but a hand stretched out to draw them back — and the man who treats forbearance as permission is, in Paul's dreadful bookkeeping, making deposits in an account of wrath. Kindness despised is the surest way to be hardened.

Reflection question: Where have you been presuming on God's patience — treating His kindness as if it were approval — and what is the repentance His kindness is leading you to, by name, today?
