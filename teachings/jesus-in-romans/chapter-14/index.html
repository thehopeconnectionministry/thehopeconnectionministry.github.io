Romans 14 — The Weak and the Strong: One Table, One Lord

Jesus in Romans — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Epistle to the Romans
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Jesus is the Lord of both the weak and the strong in this chapter — the Lord who died and lived again precisely "that he might be Lord both of the dead and of the living" (14:9). He is the one for whom the brother was bought: "the brother for whom Christ died" (14:15) — the price tag on every believer at the table. He is the Judge before whose seat we shall all stand (14:10), which is exactly why we may stop standing in judgment over each other now. And He is the kingdom's center: "the kingdom of God is not a matter of eating and drinking but of righteousness and peace and joy in the Holy Spirit" (14:17) — the righteousness He earned, the peace He made, the joy His Spirit gives.

Commentary

Welcome, but not to quarrel (14:1–4). The transformed community now faces its most ordinary test: dinner. "As for the one who is weak in faith, welcome him, but not to quarrel over opinions" (14:1). The weak brother in Rome is likely a believer — many of them Jewish — whose conscience still binds him to the food laws and the calendar; the strong eat everything, knowing the gospel has cleansed all foods. Paul's first command is not "straighten him out" but welcome him — and welcome him without making the welcome an ambush, a debate society over his scruples. "One person believes he may eat anything, while the weak person eats only vegetables. Let not the one who eats despise the one who abstains, and let not the one who abstains pass judgment on the one who eats, for God has welcomed him" (14:2–3). Both directions of contempt are named: the strong man's despising and the weak man's judging are equally forbidden. And the ground is decisive: God has welcomed him. If God has received a man, the church's only remaining option is to receive him too. "Who are you to pass judgment on the servant of another? It is before his own master that he stands or falls. And he will be upheld, for the Lord is able to make him stand" (14:4). He will be upheld — not because his opinions are all correct, but because his Master is able.

Days, and being fully convinced (14:5–9). The calendar gets the same treatment: "One person esteems one day as better than another, while another esteems all days alike. Each one should be fully convinced in his own mind" (14:5). On matters Scripture has not bound, Paul commands conviction without contempt: the observer observes "in honor of the Lord," the eater eats "in honor of the Lord," and both give thanks to God (14:6). Thanksgiving is the test of the conscience — what can be done with thanks can be done in faith. Then Paul lifts the whole matter to its true altitude: "For none of us lives to himself, and none of us dies to himself. For if we live, we live to the Lord, and if we die, we die to the Lord. So then, whether we live or whether we die, we are the Lord's" (14:7–8). Food and days are small; lordship is everything. And the lordship is grounded in the cross and resurrection: "For to this end Christ died and lived again, that he might be Lord both of the dead and of the living" (14:9). The man who belongs to that Lord, in life and in death, can afford to let his brother belong to Him too.

The judgment seat (14:10–12). Then Paul removes the gavel from our hands permanently: "Why do you pass judgment on your brother? Or you, why do you despise your brother? For we will all stand before the judgment seat of God; for it is written, 'As I live, says the Lord, every knee shall bow to me, and every tongue shall confess to God.' So then each of us will give an account of himself to God" (14:10–12). Each of us — an account of himself, not a report on his brother. The judgment seat is real, it is God's, and it is occupied — which means it is not vacant for us to climb into. Every act of contempt for a brother is a rehearsal for a job we will never hold. The knee that will bow then can afford to unbend the neck now.

Do not put a stumbling block (14:13–18). The strong now receive the chapter's weightiest charge. "Therefore let us not pass judgment on one another any longer, but rather decide never to put a stumbling block or hindrance in the way of a brother" (14:13). Paul states his own conviction without flinching: "I know and am persuaded in the Lord Jesus that nothing is unclean in itself, but it is unclean for anyone who thinks it unclean" (14:14). The strong are right about the food — and their rightness is not the point. "For if your brother is grieved by what you eat, you are no longer walking in love. By what you eat, do not destroy the one for whom Christ died" (14:15). The one for whom Christ died — Paul prices the brother at the cross, and against that price, a plate of meat is nothing. Freedom that destroys a brother is not freedom exercised but love abandoned: "So do not let what you regard as good be spoken of as evil" (14:16). And then the kingdom's true menu: "For the kingdom of God is not a matter of eating and drinking but of righteousness and peace and joy in the Holy Spirit. Whoever thus serves Christ is acceptable to God and approved by men" (14:17–18). Righteousness, peace, joy — the gospel's own triad from chapter 5 and chapter 8 — that is what the kingdom tastes like.

Pursue peace (14:19–23). The conclusion gathers the whole ethic: "So then let us pursue what makes for peace and for mutual upbuilding" (14:19). Pursue — peace is chased, not stumbled into. "Do not, for the sake of food, destroy the work of God" (14:20) — the brother is God's work, still under construction, and no appetite is worth demolishing him. Paul gives the strong a rule for their liberty: it is good not to eat meat or drink wine or do anything that causes a brother to stumble (14:21) — freedom is most free when it is laid down voluntarily. And the private side: "The faith that you have, keep between yourself and God. Blessed is the one who has no reason to pass judgment on himself for what he approves" (14:22). Not every liberty needs advertising. The chapter closes with the conscience principle that governs all of it: "But whoever has doubts is condemned if he eats, because the eating is not from faith. For whatever does not proceed from faith is sin" (14:23). To act against conscience is to act without faith — and whatever is without faith, however small the matter, is sin. Conscience is not God, but it is God's appointed governor in the small provinces, and it is not to be overridden — not by appetite, and not by pressure from the strong.

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Romans 14 is that the church's worst wounds are often self-inflicted at the table. The strong despise, the weak judge, and both forget that the brother they are wounding is a man for whom Christ died. We climb into a judgment seat that is not ours, we prize our liberties above God's work in another soul, and we call it conviction. Left to ourselves, the family of God divides over vegetables while the kingdom is righteousness, peace, and joy in the Holy Spirit. But the good news is that one Lord holds the whole table together. God has welcomed the weak brother and the strong one alike; Christ died and lived again to be Lord of both; and each of us will give an account of himself to a Judge who is also our Savior. Because He has welcomed us, we can welcome each other. Because He will make His servants stand, we can stop trying to prop up or knock down the servants of Another — and pursue, together, what makes for peace.

A note from the Reformers. Calvin treated Romans 14 as the church's charter for handling everything Scripture leaves free: where God has not bound the conscience, no man may bind it — and where love requires restraint, no liberty may plead its rights. The Reformers called these things adiaphora, matters indifferent — and insisted they stop being indifferent the moment a brother's soul is at stake.

Reflection question: Who is the "weak brother" — or the "strong" one — you have been despising or judging over a matter of opinion? What would it cost you this week to welcome them the way God has welcomed you?
