Romans 10 — The Righteousness That Is by Faith: Christ the End of the Law

Jesus in Romans — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Epistle to the Romans
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Jesus is the end of the law and the nearness of the gospel in this chapter. He is the goal to which the whole law was running: "Christ is the end of the law for righteousness to everyone who believes" (10:4). He is the Lord confessed with the mouth and believed on in the heart — "if you confess with your mouth that Jesus is Lord and believe in your heart that God raised him from the dead, you will be saved" (10:9). He is the Lord of all, bestowing riches on all who call on Him (10:12), the one of whom Isaiah said, "How beautiful are the feet of those who preach the good news!" (10:15). And He is the Christ who all day long holds out His hands to a disobedient and contrary people (10:21) — pursuing the very people who will not pursue Him.

Commentary

Zeal without knowledge (10:1–4). Chapter 10 opens where chapter 9 did — in Paul's heart: "Brothers, my heart's desire and prayer to God for them is that they may be saved" (10:1). The theologian of election is the evangelist of prayer; Paul holds both in one heart, and so must we. His testimony about Israel is honest and sad: "For I bear them witness that they have a zeal for God, but not according to knowledge. For, being ignorant of the righteousness of God, and seeking to establish their own, they did not submit to God's righteousness" (10:2–3). Zeal is not enough. A man can be fervent, sincere, sacrificial — and lost, if his zeal is aimed at establishing his own righteousness instead of submitting to God's. That word submit is the crux: God's righteousness must be received with bowed head and empty hands, and pride will not bow. Then the sentence that sums up the law's whole career:

"For Christ is the end of the law for righteousness to everyone who believes." — Romans 10:4 (ESV)

End — both goal and termination. Christ is what the law was always aiming at, the One in whom its shadows become substance; and for everyone who believes, Christ is the end of the law as a way of righteousness — that road is closed, because its destination has arrived. No believer will ever again be asked to climb it.

Two righteousnesses (10:5–8). Paul sets the two ways side by side from Moses' own writings. The righteousness based on the law says: "The person who does the commandments shall live by them" (10:5, quoting Leviticus 18:5) — do, and live; a true statement and, for sinners, a death sentence, since none of us does. But the righteousness based on faith speaks differently — and Paul borrows Deuteronomy 30 to let it speak: "Do not say in your heart, 'Who will ascend into heaven?' (that is, to bring Christ down) or 'Who will descend into the abyss?' (that is, to bring Christ up from the dead). But what does it say? 'The word is near you, in your mouth and in your heart' (that is, the word of faith that we proclaim)" (10:6–8). The gospel requires no impossible journeys. You do not have to climb to heaven — Christ has come down. You do not have to raid the grave — Christ has come up. The word is near: as near as your mouth, as near as your heart.

Confess and believe (10:9–13). And so Paul states the way of salvation as simply as it can be stated, in the verses more sinners have entered the kingdom through than perhaps any others in the letter:

"because, if you confess with your mouth that Jesus is Lord and believe in your heart that God raised him from the dead, you will be saved. For with the heart one believes and is justified, and with the mouth one confesses and is saved." — Romans 10:9–10 (ESV)

Heart and mouth, inward and outward, one faith with two expressions. Belief is not a vague optimism — it has an object and a content: that God raised Jesus from the dead. Confession is not a work added to faith — it is faith with its mouth open: Jesus is Lord, the earliest creed of the church, spoken by people who knew Caesar claimed the same title. And the promise stands on Scripture: "everyone who believes in him will not be put to shame" (10:11). Then the door swings to its full width: "For there is no distinction between Jew and Greek; for the same Lord is Lord of all, bestowing riches on all who call on him. For 'everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved'" (10:12–13, quoting Joel 2:32). Everyone. The richest word in the chapter. The Lord of all bestows riches on all who call — and calling is all the qualification there is.

Beautiful feet (10:14–17). But calling requires hearing, and hearing requires sending — so Paul builds the great chain of missions: "How then will they call on him in whom they have not believed? And how are they to believe in him of whom they have never heard? And how are they to hear without someone preaching? And how are they to preach unless they are sent? As it is written, 'How beautiful are the feet of those who preach the good news!'" (10:14–15, quoting Isaiah 52:7). God has ordained the means as surely as the end: sent preachers, preached words, hearing ears, believing hearts, calling mouths. Sovereignty in Romans never cancels missions; it fuels them. "So faith comes from hearing, and hearing through the word of Christ" (10:17). Faith is not worked up; it is born, and its birthplace is the preached word of Christ. That is why this letter, and every sermon ever preached from it, matters eternally.

Israel heard — and did not obey (10:18–21). Paul closes by clearing God of every charge of unfairness. Did Israel not hear? "But I ask, have they not heard? Indeed they have, for 'Their voice has gone out to all the earth, and their words to the ends of the world'" (10:18). Did Israel not understand? Moses and Isaiah both foretold it: God would provoke Israel to jealousy by a foolish nation, and be found by those who did not seek Him (10:19–20). The gospel's advance to the Gentiles was not an accident; it was prophesied. And the last word of the chapter belongs neither to Paul's argument nor to Israel's failure, but to God's posture: "But of Israel he says, 'All day long I have held out my hands to a disobedient and contrary people'" (10:21, quoting Isaiah 65:2). All day long. Hands held out. That is how chapter 10 ends — not with God turning away from rebels, but with God standing with open hands toward them, from morning to night.

Key verses

"For Christ is the end of the law for righteousness to everyone who believes." — Romans 10:4 (ESV)

"The word is near you, in your mouth and in your heart" — Romans 10:8 (ESV)

"because, if you confess with your mouth that Jesus is Lord and believe in your heart that God raised him from the dead, you will be saved." — Romans 10:9 (ESV)

"For 'everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved.'" — Romans 10:13 (ESV)

"So faith comes from hearing, and hearing through the word of Christ." — Romans 10:17 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Romans 10 is that zeal cannot save. Israel had zeal for God in abundance — and missed salvation, because their zeal ran toward establishing their own righteousness and would not submit to God's. Sincerity is not the test; submission is. A man can work himself to exhaustion on the law's road and never arrive, because the road was never meant to carry him — it was meant to point him to its end. But the good news is that Christ is the end of the law for righteousness to everyone who believes, and the gospel He finished is near — not up in heaven, not down in the grave, but in your mouth and in your heart. Confess that Jesus is Lord, believe that God raised Him from the dead, and you will be saved. Everyone — everyone — who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved. And the chapter's last picture is God's hands, held out all day long to a contrary people. They are still held out.

A note from the Reformers. Lloyd-Jones insisted that Romans 10:17 is the charter of preaching: God has chosen to give faith through the heard word, and so the preacher's task is not to entertain or to impress but to preach Christ until faith is born. Spurgeon lived on verse 13 — "everyone" was his favorite word in the Bible, he said, because it let no sinner exclude himself. Between the two of them, that is this chapter: preach to everyone, because everyone who calls will be saved.

Reflection question: The word is near — in your mouth and in your heart. Is there a confession you have believed privately but never spoken aloud? What keeps your mouth closed, and who needs to hear "Jesus is Lord" from you this week?
