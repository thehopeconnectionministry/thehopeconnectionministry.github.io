Romans 7 — The War Within: The Law, the Flesh, and the Cry for a Deliverer

Jesus in Romans — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Epistle to the Romans
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Jesus is mostly offstage in Romans 7 — and that is exactly the point. This is the one chapter where Paul shows us the Christian life described in terms of "I" — I, I, I, dozens of times — with Christ hardly mentioned, and the result is defeat. The chapter is a valley between two mountain ranges named Christ (chapter 6) and the Spirit (chapter 8). But Jesus appears at the two moments that matter most: at the start, as the Husband to whom we now belong, having died to the law through His body (7:4), and at the end, as the Deliverer named in the cry of a desperate man: "Thanks be to God through Jesus Christ our Lord!" (7:25). Romans 7 teaches us by absence what chapters 6 and 8 teach by presence: apart from Christ and His Spirit, even a renewed man cannot win. With Him, the cry of defeat becomes a cry of thanks.

Commentary

Dead to the law, married to Christ (7:1–6). Paul begins with an illustration from marriage law, addressed to those who know the law. A married woman is bound to her husband while he lives; if he dies, she is released from the law of marriage and free to belong to another. "Likewise, my brothers, you also have died to the law through the body of Christ, so that you may belong to another, to him who has been raised from the dead, in order that we may bear fruit for God" (7:4). Notice the direction of the death: we did not kill the law; we died — to the law, through the body of Christ crucified. The old marriage is over because a death has occurred, and the new union is with a risen Husband, for a purpose: fruit for God. The old way is described in one clause: "For while we were living in the flesh, our sinful passions, aroused by the law, were at work in our members to bear fruit for death" (7:5). The new way: "But now we are released from the law, having died to that which held us captive, so that we serve in the new way of the Spirit and not in the old way of the written code" (7:6). Released — not to lawlessness, but to a new service, in the Spirit's way rather than the letter's. The rest of the chapter will show why the letter's way, by itself, cannot produce that fruit.

Is the law sin? (7:7–13). The question comes naturally: if the law aroused sinful passions, is the law itself sin? "By no means! Yet if it had not been for the law, I would not have known sin. For I would not have known what it is to covet if the law had not said, 'You shall not covet'" (7:7). Paul reaches for the tenth commandment deliberately — the commandment against an inward desire, the one no Pharisee could claim to have kept by outward performance. The law did its diagnostic work in Paul: it named the cancer. "But sin, seizing an opportunity through the commandment, produced in me all kinds of covetousness" (7:8) — the commandment, meant for life, became the occasion for sin to multiply, the way a "wet paint" sign draws the very touching it forbids. "I was once alive apart from the law, but when the commandment came, sin came alive and I died. The very commandment that promised life proved to be death to me" (7:9–10). Paul is describing his own Pharisee's story: self-confident once, then slain by the law's true meaning. And he is careful to acquit the law itself: "So the law is holy, and the commandment is holy and righteous and good" (7:12). The killer is not the law but sin, which used the good law as its weapon — "in order that sin might be shown to be sin" (7:13). The law is God's holy standard. It was never the disease. It was the X-ray, and X-rays do not heal.

The war within (7:14–20). Now the chapter turns confessional, and generations of Christians have either found themselves here or argued about whose experience it is. Read it as what it most plainly is: the honest diary of a man who loves God's law and cannot keep it — the believer's war with indwelling sin. "For we know that the law is spiritual, but I am of the flesh, sold under sin. For I do not understand my own actions. For I do not do what I want, but I do the very thing I hate" (7:14–15). This is not the speech of a man who loves sin. A man who loves sin does not hate what he does. "Now if I do what I do not want, I agree with the law, that it is good. So now it is no longer I who do it, but sin that dwells within me" (7:16–17). Paul is not excusing himself — he is locating the enemy: a squatter, indwelling sin, still resident in a house whose ownership has changed. "For I know that nothing good dwells in me, that is, in my flesh. For I have the desire to do what is right, but not the ability to carry it out. For I do not do the good I want, but the evil I do not want is what I keep on doing" (7:18–19). Desire without ability — that is the anatomy of the war. The will is renewed; the flesh is not yet. Every honest believer has stood exactly here, between the wanting and the doing.

A law, a war, and a cry (7:21–25). Paul sums up his discovery as a principle: "So I find it to be a law that when I want to do right, evil lies close at hand. For I delight in the law of God, in my inner being, but I see in my members another law waging war against the law of my mind and making me captive to the law of sin that dwells in my members" (7:21–23). I delight in the law of God — no unregenerate man talks this way; this is a renewed heart describing its own civil war. Two laws, two captivities, one man. And out of the war comes the most honest cry in the Bible:

"Wretched man that I am! Who will deliver me from this body of death?" — Romans 7:24 (ESV)

Who will deliver me — not what technique, not how much effort, but who. The question is a person-shaped question, and it gets a Person for an answer: "Thanks be to God through Jesus Christ our Lord!" (7:25). The deliverance is not self-improvement; it is rescue, and the Rescuer is named. Then the honest summary, neither triumphant nor despairing: "So then, I myself serve the law of God with my mind, but with my flesh I serve the law of sin" (7:25). That is where chapter 7 leaves us — serving God with the mind, warring in the flesh, crying out for deliverance. Chapter 8 will open with the deliverance itself, and it will not disappoint: there is therefore now no condemnation.

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Romans 7 is that justification does not end the war. The believer delights in God's law in his inner being and still finds another law in his members, waging war, taking him captive; he has the desire to do right and not the ability to carry it out. The law itself is holy and good, but it cannot rescue — it can only show the sin, stir the conscience, and slay the self-confidence. Left to the resources of "I," the chapter ends in a cry: "Wretched man that I am!" But the good news is that the cry has an answer, and the answer is a Person. Who will deliver me? "Thanks be to God through Jesus Christ our Lord!" Deliverance was never going to come from a better technique or a stronger resolve; it comes from the Deliverer — the risen Husband to whom we now belong, who died to free us from the old marriage and lives to bear fruit through us. Romans 7 is the valley, but it is a valley with a name cried out in it, and the next chapter is the mountain on the other side.

A note from the Reformers. Luther knew this chapter from the inside — his monastery years were Romans 7 without Romans 8, desire without deliverance, confession without peace. His summary of the Christian life, simul iustus et peccator — at the same time righteous and a sinner — is Romans 7 and Romans 8 held together: fully righteous in Christ, still warring in the flesh, and never, ever excused from the war or condemned by it.

Reflection question: Paul's cry is "Who will deliver me?" — a person, not a plan. Where have you been answering the war within with techniques and resolutions instead of running to the Deliverer — and what would it mean to cry out to Jesus Christ Himself about that specific battle today?
