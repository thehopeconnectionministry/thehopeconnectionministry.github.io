The Hope Connection
Lewis Fuller

"For I am not ashamed of the gospel, for it is the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes, to the Jew first and also to the Greek. For in it the righteousness of God is revealed from faith for faith, as it is written, 'The righteous shall live by faith.'" — Romans 1:16–17 (ESV)

If Mark's Gospel is the good news in action, Romans is the good news explained. Here the apostle Paul sits down — the only time in his letters where he does this at full length — and lays out the whole gospel in order: what is wrong with us, what God has done about it in Christ, what changes when a sinner believes, what God is doing with Israel and the nations, and how a justified people actually live on Monday morning. Every doctrine that matters most is here: sin, wrath, justification, faith, union with Christ, adoption, election, the new life, and the love of God from which nothing can separate us. Christians have called Romans the greatest letter ever written. They are not wrong. A man does not have to take anyone's word for that — he only has to read it slowly, the way this commentary will walk through it, chapter by chapter.

Who wrote it. Paul the apostle wrote Romans — Paul, the Pharisee from Tarsus who had once hunted the church and tried to destroy it, stopped on the Damascus road by the risen Christ Himself (Acts 9). The persecutor became the preacher, and the man who had approved the stoning of Stephen became the man who would write, "I am not ashamed of the gospel." Paul dictated the letter to a scribe named Tertius, who slips his own greeting into the last chapter — "I Tertius, who wrote this letter, greet you in the Lord" (Romans 16:22) — and the letter was most likely carried to Rome by Phoebe, a servant of the church at Cenchreae, whom Paul commends in the warmest terms (Romans 16:1–2). So even the delivery of Romans is a picture of the gospel it carries: an apostle, a scribe, and a faithful woman, none of them famous in the world's eyes, carrying the most important letter in the world.

How to Use This Commentary

This commentary was built to be used, not just read. Here is how to get the most out of it.

The order of every session. Work through each chapter the same way, in this order:

  1. Read the chapter in your own Bible first. Open your Bible to the chapter of Romans and read it all the way through before you read a word of commentary. The commentary serves the Scripture; it never replaces it. Read it out loud if you can — Romans was written to be read aloud to a gathered church, and Paul's arguments land with their full weight when they are heard in sequence, the way the Romans first heard them.
  2. Then read the commentary section for that chapter. Keep your Bible open beside it and look up the verses as they come. Start with "Jesus in this chapter" — before anything else, fix your eyes on how Christ is revealed there. Charles Spurgeon said a passage is not finished until Christ has been seen in it, and that is the aim of every section here. Romans is an argument, and arguments are followed step by step: do not skip ahead to chapter 8's comfort before you have felt chapter 1's weight. Paul builds; build with him.
  3. Then sit with the reflection question. Each chapter ends with one reflection question and a "bad news and good news" paragraph. Do not rush past them. Read the bad news honestly — let the law do its searching work, the way it searched Luther — and then read the good news slowly, out loud if you can. Answer the reflection question in prayer, out loud to God, not just in your head.
  4. Write in a notebook. Keep a simple notebook with this study. Write down your answer to the reflection question, your answer to the study guide question for that chapter (see the Romans Study Guide below), and the memory verse for the chapter. A truth is not really yours until you have written it, prayed it, and lived it. You do not need anything fancy — a dollar notebook and a pen will do the whole letter.

The rhythm: three sessions a week. This fits the study rhythm used across The Hope Connection: three sessions a week, one book at a time, until the book is done. At one chapter per session, three sessions a week, you will walk through all sixteen chapters of Romans in about five and a half weeks. If you want to go slower — and with Romans, slower is often better — spread one chapter across the whole week: session one, read the chapter in your Bible and the "Jesus in this chapter" opening; session two, read the commentary; session three, return to the chapter, work through the questions, and pray it back to God. Either pace is fine. What matters is the steadiness, not the speed.

No catch-up piles. This rule is not negotiable, because it protects the whole habit: if you miss a day, you do not owe anybody two chapters. You simply pick up at the next chapter at the next session. Life happens — sickness, family, work, hard weeks. A study rhythm that piles up guilt every time life interrupts will not survive a month. This one bends and keeps going. Missed a week? Start again at the next chapter. The Lord is not keeping a tally, and neither are we.

Using it alone. Everything above is written for one reader, one Bible, one notebook, and the Lord. Pray before you open the book — a short, real prayer: "Lord, open my eyes, and let this Word search me." Pray again when you finish, turning the chapter's good news into thanksgiving and its bad news into confession.

Using it with a spouse or a friend. Read the chapter out loud together at the start of the session — take turns reading. Then read the commentary, or take turns reading it aloud too. Talk through the reflection question and the study guide question together, honestly. Pray for each other out of what the chapter showed you. Romans raises the deepest questions a Christian can ask — what God has done, why He has done it, how then to live — and two people working through it together will steady each other in chapters that might shake a reader alone.

Using it in a small group. One chapter per meeting. Have someone read the chapter aloud from the Bible to open — Romans especially rewards this, since the whole letter was first received by hearing. Walk through the commentary section together, stopping wherever the text raises a question. Save real time at the end for the reflection question and the study guide question — that is where study becomes life. Close in prayer, and let the chapter's memory verse be the group's verse for the week: read it together at the start of the next meeting and see who can say it from memory. Keep the group small enough that everybody talks, and keep the door open — Romans explains the gospel more fully than any other book, and every chapter here is written plain enough for a brand-new believer to follow.

The memory verses. The study guide below gives one memory verse for each chapter — sixteen verses, the spine of the letter. If you take one verse a week alongside the chapter rhythm, by the time you finish the book you will carry its whole argument in your heart: the gospel's power, God's kindness leading to repentance, all have sinned, faith counted as righteousness, peace with God, the free gift of eternal life, the cry for a Deliverer, no condemnation, mercy that does not depend on man, the confession that saves, glory to God forever, the living sacrifice, the Lord Jesus put on, the life that is the Lord's, the God of hope, and glory to the only wise God. That is treasure no one can take from you.

Study Questions — One per Chapter

Work each question in your notebook after the chapter's reflection question. Many of them follow the Bible's own pattern of putting off the old and putting on the new (Ephesians 4:22–24; Colossians 3:8–10).

  1. Romans 1 — Do not be ashamed. Paul was eager to preach the gospel in the capital of the world. Put off shame about the gospel — the places where you go quiet about Christ because of what people might think. Put on boldness: name one setting this week where you will speak of Christ plainly, and pray for it by name.
  2. Romans 2 — Let kindness lead you. God's kindness is meant to lead us to repentance, not to presumption. Put off one sin you have been excusing because "God is patient." Put on repentance today: confess it by name, and take one concrete step away from it this week.
  3. Romans 3 — Stop defending yourself. "None is righteous, no, not one" — and every mouth is stopped. Put off one excuse you habitually make for your sin. Put on the stopped mouth and the open hand: confess your sin without defense, and receive justification as what it is — a gift.
  4. Romans 4 — Believe like Abraham. Abraham believed God against every visible fact — a dead body and a barren womb — and it was counted to him as righteousness. Put off trusting what you can see. Name one promise of God that looks impossible in your life right now, and put on Abraham's faith: write the promise down and thank God for it daily this week.
  5. Romans 5 — Rejoice in the peace. Justification is not a courtroom technicality; it is peace with God and hope that does not put us to shame. Put off one fear that treats God as still against you. Put on Romans 5:8: write out "while we were still sinners, Christ died for us" and put it where you will see it every day this week.
  6. Romans 6 — Count yourself dead. You cannot live off a baptism you treat as a past event only; Paul says consider — reckon — yourself dead to sin daily. Put off one sin's claim that it still owns you. Put on the reckoning: each morning this week, say Romans 6:11 out loud and name the sin you are dead to.
  7. Romans 7 — Bring the war into the open. The Christian life has an honest war inside it, and hiding it only feeds it. Put off pretending you have no struggle. Put on honesty: tell one trusted brother or sister the true state of your war this week, and ask them to pray Romans 7:25 with you — thanks be to God, through Jesus Christ.
  8. Romans 8 — Live uncondemned. "There is therefore now no condemnation" — present tense, settled fact. Put off one condemnation you keep carrying that Christ has already carried. Put on the Spirit's cry: pray "Abba, Father" out loud this week, and when the accuser comes, answer him with Romans 8:1 and 8:33–34, not with your record.
  9. Romans 9 — Trust the mercy you cannot manage. Salvation "depends not on human will or exertion, but on God, who has mercy." Put off one place where you are trying to manage God's mercy — for yourself or for someone you love. Put on prayer: commit that person to God's mercy by name every day this week, and rest the outcome with Him.
  10. Romans 10 — Open your mouth. Salvation is as near as the word in your mouth and heart — and "how are they to believe in him of whom they have never heard?" Put off silence about Christ with one specific person. Put on confession: tell that person this week, in plain words, that Jesus is Lord and God raised Him from the dead — and leave the results with God.
  11. Romans 11 — Stay humble at the tree. Gentile believers stand at the olive tree by grace alone, and Paul warns: "Do not be proud, but fear." Put off spiritual pride — every trace of looking down on those who do not yet believe. Put on gratitude and awe: spend one prayer time this week doing nothing but thanking God that you were grafted in.
  12. Romans 12 — Present your body. The mercies of God call for a whole-body response: a living sacrifice, a renewed mind. Put off one conformity to this world — one pattern of thinking or spending or speaking you borrowed from the age. Put on one act of presented-body worship this week: a concrete service to the body of Christ that costs your time and strength.
  13. Romans 13 — Put on Christ in public. Love is the debt we never finish paying, and the night is far gone. Put off one provision for the flesh — one arrangement you keep in place that feeds a sin. Put on the Lord Jesus Christ at that exact point: remove the provision this week, and tell a brother or sister what you have done.
  14. Romans 14 — Welcome, do not quarrel. The strong and the weak sit at one table because one Lord died for both. Put off one quarrel over opinions — food, days, customs — that has been costing fellowship. Put on welcome: share a meal or a conversation this week with a believer you differ with, and refuse to pass judgment on their conscience.
  15. Romans 15 — Abound in hope for others. The God of hope fills us so that hope overflows toward the nations and the neighbor. Put off a hope kept only for yourself. Put on overflow: name one person who has little hope, and bring them a concrete encouragement this week — a visit, a call, a verse — the way Christ welcomed you.
  16. Romans 16 — Name the family. Paul ends the greatest letter ever written with names — ordinary saints, greeted one by one. Put off anonymous Christianity. Put on the Romans 16 habit: this week, greet by name and thank three people who have labored beside you in the Lord, and tell them what their faithfulness has meant.

Introduction — Romans at a Glance

If Mark's Gospel is the good news in action, Romans is the good news explained. Here the apostle Paul sits down — the only time in his letters where he does this at full length — and lays out the whole gospel in order: what is wrong with us, what God has done about it in Christ, what changes when a sinner believes, what God is doing with Israel and the nations, and how a justified people actually live on Monday morning. Who wrote it. Paul the apostle wrote Romans — Paul, the Pharisee from Tarsus who had once hunted the church and tried to destroy it, stopped on the Damascus road by the risen Christ Himself (Acts 9). The persecutor became the preacher, and the man who had approved the stoning of Stephen became the man who would write, "I am not ashamed of the gospel." So even the delivery of Romans is a picture of the gospel it carries: an apostle, a scribe, and a faithful woman, none of them famous in the world's eyes, carrying the most important letter in the world.

Where and when it was written. Paul wrote from Corinth, near the end of his third missionary journey, around AD 57. He says so himself in the letter: he has finished his work from Jerusalem around to Illyricum, he is about to carry a relief offering to the poor saints in Jerusalem, and then — at last — he hopes to come to Rome on his way to Spain (Romans 15:19–29). That matters for how we read it. Paul is not writing in a hurry between crises, the way he wrote some of his letters. He is writing with the whole of his apostolic teaching gathered and settled, laying down the gospel in its full order before he travels. Romans is Paul's fullest, calmest, most complete statement of the faith — the nearest thing he ever wrote to a systematic account of the whole counsel of God.

Who it was written for. Paul wrote to a church he had not planted and had never visited — the believers in Rome itself, the capital of the empire. The church there had likely begun with Jews who were in Jerusalem at Pentecost and carried the faith home (Acts 2:10), and by the time Paul wrote it was a mixed congregation of Jews and Gentiles, meeting in houses across the city. Paul greets twenty-six people by name in the last chapter, and he had never been there — that is how wide his love and his information ran. He writes to strengthen them (Romans 1:11), to explain the gospel they share, to settle the questions that arise when Jewish and Gentile believers sit at one table, and to ask them to pray for him and join his mission westward. He writes, in other words, to ordinary Christians in an extraordinary city — people living as a small, watched minority in the capital of the world that would soon burn some of them for the name of Christ. Romans is a letter for people who need to know that the gospel is strong enough for where they live. It still is.

The theme: the righteous shall live by faith. Every letter has a hinge, and Paul hangs Romans on two verses in the first chapter:

"For I am not ashamed of the gospel, for it is the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes, to the Jew first and also to the Greek. For in it the righteousness of God is revealed from faith for faith, as it is written, 'The righteous shall live by faith.'" — Romans 1:16–17 (ESV)

Unpack that and you have the whole letter. The gospel is power — God's own power, not a suggestion. It is for salvation — a rescue, not a self-improvement plan. It is for everyone who believes — the only door is faith, and the door stands open to Jew and Greek alike. And in it the righteousness of God is revealed — not only the righteousness by which God is righteous, but the righteousness He gives, the righteous standing He credits to the sinner who believes. "The righteous shall live by faith" — Paul is quoting the prophet Habakkuk (Habakkuk 2:4), and that one line will detonate through church history: it is the verse that broke open Luther's conscience, the verse at the head of the Reformation, and the thread Paul himself will follow through Abraham (chapter 4), through the life of the justified (chapters 5–8), and all the way to the mercy that swallows the nations (chapter 11).

The great arc of the letter. Romans moves like a river with a clear course, and it helps to see the whole map before we walk it. Sin (chapters 1–3): the bad news first — God's wrath against all unrighteousness, the Gentile world guilty, the Jewish world guilty, the whole world held accountable to God, and not one righteous, no, not one. Justification (chapters 3–5): the turn — God puts Christ forward as a propitiation, the sinner is justified by grace as a gift through faith alone, Abraham is the pattern, and peace with God floods in. Sanctification (chapters 6–8): the new life — dead to sin and alive to God in Christ, the war within, and then the mountain peak of chapter 8: no condemnation, adoption, the Spirit's help, God's unbreakable purpose, and a love from which nothing in all creation can separate us. God's purposes for Israel (chapters 9–11): the hard and glorious questions — has God's word failed? No: He has mercy on whom He has mercy, a remnant is chosen by grace, the Gentiles are grafted in, and God's gifts and calling are irrevocable. The transformed life (chapters 12–15): therefore — living sacrifices, love that fulfills the law, welcome at one table, and the God of hope filling His people with joy and peace in believing. Greetings (chapter 16): the gospel's family, named one by one, and a doxology that lifts the whole letter into worship. Sin, salvation, sovereignty, service. That is the arc. Walk it in order — Paul built it in order, and the power of the letter is in the sequence.

Luther's road through Romans. No book of the Bible has a conversion story attached to it like Romans does. Martin Luther was an Augustinian monk who feared God — feared Him the way a guilty man fears a judge. He confessed for hours, fasted, punished his body, and still found no peace, because he read the phrase "the righteousness of God" in Romans 1:17 as the righteousness by which God punishes sinners. "I hated that word 'righteousness of God,'" he wrote later. Then, meditating day and night on the connection of the words — "in it the righteousness of God is revealed, as it is written, 'The righteous shall live by faith'" — the light broke. He saw that the righteousness of God is the righteousness He gives: that the righteous live by a gift of God, namely by faith. Luther said that then he felt altogether born again, and that he had entered paradise itself through open gates. The verse he had hated became, in his words, a gateway to heaven. Out of that one breakthrough in Romans came the Reformation — and out of the Reformation came the recovery of the gospel for ordinary people in their own language. When you read this letter, you are reading the book that broke a monk's chains. It has been breaking chains ever since.

Read in the Reformation way. Romans is the Reformation's own book, and this commentary reads it the way the Reformers taught the church to read it — with their five watchwords held high and the law and the gospel kept distinct. Sola scriptura — Scripture alone is our rule: Paul builds every argument in this letter on "as it is written," and so does this commentary; every claim stands or falls by the text. Sola gratia — grace alone saves: Romans will say it until we cannot miss it — "justified by his grace as a gift" (3:24), "a remnant, chosen by grace" (11:5), "by grace you have been saved" is the air this letter breathes. Sola fide — faith alone receives that grace: "one is justified by faith apart from works of the law" (3:28); faith is the empty hand, never the earning hand. Solus Christus — Christ alone is the Savior: the propitiation in His blood, the one act of righteousness, the last Adam, the root of Jesse — every road in Romans runs through Him. Soli Deo gloria — glory to God alone: the letter ends where all true theology ends, in doxology — "To him be glory forever. Amen" (11:36). And the distinction the Reformers guarded with their lives runs through every chapter that follows: the law tells us what God requires and shows us our sin — that is the bad news, and Romans preaches it without flinching for three chapters. The gospel tells us what God has done in Christ and gives us His righteousness — that is the good news, and Romans preaches it until it sings. Mix law and gospel and you lose both: you will either despair under a gospel turned back into requirements, or grow careless under a law with its teeth pulled. Keep them distinct, in Paul's own order — law first, then gospel, then the gratitude that obeys — and Romans will do to you what it did to Luther.

How to use this commentary. Each chapter section begins with "Jesus in this chapter" — because Romans is a letter about Christ from its first line ("the gospel of God... concerning his Son," Romans 1:1–3) to its last. Then the commentary walks the chapter in order, movement by movement, explaining the text plainly and fully. Key verses are quoted in full from the English Standard Version. Each chapter closes with "The bad news and the good news," a brief note from the Reformers where one genuinely fits, and one reflection question to carry into prayer. Read it slowly. Read it with your Bible open. And remember, as you read, that you are not studying an argument in the abstract — you are being led, step by step, from the wrath of God against your sin to a love from which nothing can separate you. That journey is the Christian life itself, and Paul is the finest guide the church has ever had for walking it.
