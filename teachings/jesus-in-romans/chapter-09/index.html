Romans 9 — God's Sovereign Mercy: Has the Word of God Failed?

Jesus in Romans — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Epistle to the Romans
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Jesus is the stone of chapter 9 — the stone laid in Zion, over whom men stumble and on whom believers rest: "whoever believes in him will not be put to shame" (9:33). He is the goal Israel pursued wrongly: they chased righteousness as if it were by works and stumbled over Christ, the one place righteousness is actually given. He is also the measure of Paul's love: Paul could wish himself accursed and cut off from Christ for the sake of his brothers (9:3) — the measure only makes sense because being in Christ is everything. And behind the whole chapter stands Christ as the promised Seed through whom the children of promise are counted (9:6–8) — the offspring through whom God's word to Abraham has never once failed.

Commentary

Paul's anguish (9:1–5). Romans 9 opens not with an argument but with a broken heart. "I am speaking the truth in Christ — I am not lying; my conscience bears me witness in the Holy Spirit — that I have great sorrow and unceasing anguish in my heart" (9:1–2). Paul piles up the solemnities because what he says next is almost beyond belief: "For I could wish that I myself were accursed and cut off from Christ for the sake of my brothers, my kinsmen according to the flesh" (9:3). Paul would trade his own salvation — if such a trade were possible — for Israel's. It echoes Moses interceding: blot me out of Your book for their sake (Exodus 32:32). Only a man secure in the love of chapter 8 can even wish such a thing. Then he lists Israel's privileges, and the list is staggering: "They are Israelites, and to them belong the adoption, the glory, the covenants, the giving of the law, the worship, and the promises. To them belong the patriarchs, and from their race, according to the flesh, is the Christ, who is God over all, blessed forever. Amen" (9:4–5). The adoption, the glory, the covenants — and crowning all of it, the Christ Himself, who is God over all. One of the clearest statements of Christ's deity in the New Testament sits in the middle of Paul's grief for Israel. The privileges were real and enormous. Which raises the chapter's question with real force: if Israel had all this, and Israel has largely not believed — has God's word failed?

Not all Israel is Israel (9:6–13). Paul's answer comes like a hammer: "But it is not as though the word of God has failed. For not all who are descended from Israel belong to Israel, and not all are children of Abraham because they are his offspring" (9:6–7). God's promises were never made to mere bloodline. From the beginning, God worked through a chosen line within the line: not Ishmael but Isaac — "Through Isaac shall your offspring be named" — and not Esau but Jacob. "This means that it is not the children of the flesh who are the children of God, but the children of the promise are counted as offspring" (9:8). And the Jacob-Esau case is chosen precisely because it excludes every human factor: "though they were not yet born and had done nothing either good or bad — in order that God's purpose of election might continue, not because of works but because of him who calls — she was told, 'The older will serve the younger.' As it is written, 'Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated'" (9:11–13, quoting Genesis 25:23 and Malachi 1:2–3). Before birth, before works, before deserving — God's purpose of election stood on His call alone. This is hard ground, and Paul knows it. He does not soften it. The point is foundational for everything that follows: salvation has always run on God's promise and God's choice, never on man's bloodline or man's record. Chapter 4's Abraham — justified by faith, father of all who believe — is the same doctrine in a warmer light.

Is God unjust? (9:14–18). The objection rises instantly, in Paul's day and in ours: "What shall we say then? Is there injustice on God's part?" And the answer: "By no means!" — and the proof is God's own self-revelation to Moses: "For he says to Moses, 'I will have mercy on whom I have mercy, and I will have compassion on whom I have compassion'" (9:15, quoting Exodus 33:19). Mercy, by definition, cannot be owed. If God owed it, it would be wages, not mercy. "So then it depends not on human will or exertion, but on God, who has mercy" (9:16). Not on human will — the runner does not win God's mercy by running; the mercy precedes the running. Pharaoh stands as the dark mirror: God raised him up to show His power and proclaim His name in all the earth, and hardened the heart that had hardened itself against every warning. "So then he has mercy on whomever he wills, and he hardens whomever he wills" (9:18). Paul does not explain the mystery away. He states God's freedom — the freedom without which mercy would not be mercy at all.

The potter and the clay (9:19–24). One more objection, the sharpest: "You will say to me then, 'Why does he still find fault? For who can resist his will?'" (9:19). Paul's answer is not an explanation but a re-location: "But who are you, O man, to answer back to God? Will what is molded say to its molder, 'Why have you made me like this?' Has the potter no right over the clay, to make out of the same lump one vessel for honorable use and another for dishonorable use?" (9:20–21). The creature does not sit in judgment on the Creator; the clay does not cross-examine the potter. And then Paul gives the deepest reason he will give: "What if God, desiring to show his wrath and to make known his power, has endured with much patience vessels of wrath prepared for destruction, in order to make known the riches of his glory for vessels of mercy, which he has prepared beforehand for glory — even us whom he has called, not from the Jews only but also from the Gentiles?" (9:22–24). God's patience with the vessels of wrath serves the display of glory to the vessels of mercy — and those vessels are us, Jew and Gentile together, called out of both. The doctrine of election, in Paul's hands, does not end in speculation. It ends in a called people, gathered from everywhere, prepared beforehand for glory.

The remnant and the stone (9:25–33). Paul now proves the calling of the Gentiles from the prophets themselves, quoting Hosea — spoken originally about Israel's restoration and applied here to the nations: "Those who were not my people I will call 'my people,' and her who was not beloved I will call 'beloved'" (9:25). And Isaiah on Israel: though the number of Israel be as the sand of the sea, "only a remnant of them will be saved" (9:27) — and were it not for the remnant, Israel would have become like Sodom, utterly gone. The chapter closes with the diagnosis of Israel's stumbling, and it is a diagnosis of method, not of sincerity: "Gentiles who did not pursue righteousness have attained it, that is, a righteousness that is by faith; but... Israel who pursued a law that would lead to righteousness did not succeed in reaching that law. Why? Because they did not pursue it by faith, but as if it were based on works. They have stumbled over the stumbling stone" (9:30–32). Israel ran hard — in the wrong direction, with the wrong reliance: works instead of faith. And the stone they struck is a Person: "Behold, I am laying in Zion a stone of stumbling and a rock of offense; and whoever believes in him will not be put to shame" (9:33). The same stone is stumbling or foundation depending on one thing: whether a man comes to Christ by faith or crashes into Him with his works in his hands.

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Romans 9 is that no one can put God in his debt. Privileges do not save — Israel had the adoption, the covenants, the law, and the patriarchs, and stumbled. Bloodline does not save — not all Israel is Israel. Effort does not save — Israel pursued righteousness as if by works and did not reach it. Salvation depends not on human will or exertion, and the clay has no standing to answer back to the Potter. If mercy could be managed, claimed, or deserved, sinners like us would manage to lose it. But the good news is the same truth read from the other side: mercy depends on God, and God is merciful. His word has not failed — it never ran on our strength. He calls vessels of mercy from Jews and Gentiles alike, people who were not His people, and makes them beloved. And the stone that trips the proud is a foundation for everyone who believes: whoever believes in Him will not be put to shame. Election is not a wall to keep sinners out. It is the reason any sinner is in.

A note from the Reformers. Calvin approached Romans 9 the way a man approaches holy ground, and warned his readers to do the same: where Scripture stops, our curiosity must stop too — God has revealed His freedom to have mercy, not a map of His hidden counsel for us to audit. The Reformers held this chapter with both hands: sovereignty that humbles every boast, and mercy offered to every believer. Neither hand may let go.

Reflection question: Paul begins this hard chapter with unceasing anguish for people who do not believe. Whose salvation do you grieve over — and will you let that grief drive you to prayer and witness this week, trusting the mercy of God that does not depend on your will or exertion?
