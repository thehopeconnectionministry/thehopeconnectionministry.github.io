Romans 6 — Dead to Sin, Alive to God: The New Life in Christ

Jesus in Romans — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Epistle to the Romans
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Jesus is the place where the believer's old life ended and new life began. We were baptized into Christ Jesus, baptized into His death, buried with Him, and raised with Him to walk in newness of life (6:3–4). His crucifixion is where our old self was crucified (6:6); His resurrection is the pattern and the guarantee of our new walk; His death to sin, once for all, and His life to God are the very shape of the life we now live (6:10–11). And at the chapter's end He is the one in whom the free gift is given: "eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord" (6:23). Romans 6 will not let us think of salvation as a ticket out of hell that leaves the life unchanged. We are united to a Person, and the union changes everything.

Commentary

The question grace always provokes (6:1–2). Chapter 5 ended with grace abounding over increasing sin, and a certain logic pipes up at once — Paul heard it in his own day, and every preacher of free grace has heard it since: "What shall we say then? Are we to continue in sin that grace may abound?" (6:1). If grace super-abounds where sin increases, why not sin more and get more grace? Paul's answer is the strongest negative in his vocabulary: "By no means! How can we who died to sin still live in it?" (6:2). Notice he does not answer with a threat but with an identity. The question is not "how dare you, after all God has done" but "how can you — you died." Grace does not merely forgive the old life; it ends it. A dead man cannot go back to his old job.

Baptized into His death (6:3–11). The ground of that death is baptism — not the water as magic, but the union with Christ that baptism signifies and seals. "Do you not know that all of us who have been baptized into Christ Jesus were baptized into his death? We were buried therefore with him by baptism into death, in order that, just as Christ was raised from the dead by the glory of the Father, we too might walk in newness of life" (6:3–4). Buried with Him — the old life is not reformed, it is interred. Raised with Him — the new life is not our old life with better habits; it is resurrection life, His life, walked out in us. "For if we have been united with him in a death like his, we shall certainly be united with him in a resurrection like his" (6:5). Then the fact to be known: "We know that our old self was crucified with him in order that the body of sin might be brought to nothing, so that we would no longer be enslaved to sin. For one who has died has been set free from sin" (6:6–7). The old self was crucified — not is being crucified, not will be, but was, at the cross, in Christ. Sin's claim on the believer is a dead man's claim on a corpse: it has no legal standing. "For the death he died he died to sin, once for all, but the life he lives he lives to God" (6:10) — once for all, never to be repeated, and His risen life is a life lived entirely to God. Then comes the chapter's great imperative, built entirely on the indicative: "So you also must consider yourselves dead to sin and alive to God in Christ Jesus" (6:11). Consider — it is logizomai again, Abraham's bookkeeping word from chapter 4. Reckon it true. Count on the ledger what God has already entered: dead to sin, alive to God. Sanctification begins not with trying harder but with counting correctly.

Do not present your members to sin (6:12–14). Because the fact is settled, the fight can be fought: "Let not sin therefore reign in your mortal body, to make you obey its passions. Do not present your members to sin as instruments for unrighteousness, but present yourselves to God as those who have been brought from death to life, and your members to God as instruments for righteousness" (6:12–13). The members — hands, eyes, tongue, feet — are instruments, and instruments are presented to a master. Every day they are offered somewhere. The believer offers them to God, "as those who have been brought from death to life" — that phrase is the motive: we fight sin not to become alive but because we have been brought from death to life. And the promise under the whole battle: "For sin will have no dominion over you, since you are not under law but under grace" (6:14). Dominion — lordship. Sin remains present, but it is a deposed king raiding a country it no longer rules. And the reason is a change of regime: not under law, which commands without giving power, but under grace, which gives what it commands.

Slaves of the one you obey (6:15–23). The second objection comes: "What then? Are we to sin because we are not under law but under grace? By no means!" (6:15). Paul answers with the slavery principle: "Do you not know that if you present yourselves to anyone as obedient slaves, you are slaves of the one whom you obey, either of sin, which leads to death, or of obedience, which leads to righteousness?" (6:16). Everyone serves someone; the only question is whom. There is no unowned life. Then thanksgiving breaks in: "But thanks be to God, that you who were once slaves of sin have become obedient from the heart to the standard of teaching to which you were committed, and, having been set free from sin, have become slaves of righteousness" (6:17–18). Set free from sin — and the freedom is a transfer of ownership, from a master who pays in death to a Master whose service is life. Paul apologizes for the slavery metaphor even as he uses it — "I am speaking in human terms, because of your natural limitations" (6:19) — and then draws the contrast of the two harvests. The old slavery's fruit: "the things of which you are now ashamed... For the end of those things is death." The new: "the fruit that leads to sanctification and its end, eternal life" (6:21–22). And the whole gospel of the chapter lands in the most famous summary verse in the letter:

"For the wages of sin is death, but the free gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord." — Romans 6:23 (ESV)

Wages against gift. Sin pays what is earned — death, fairly, in full. God gives what is not earned — eternal life, freely, in Christ. No one will ever stand before God and complain the accounts were unclear.

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Romans 6 is that there is no neutral ground and no unowned life. We were slaves of sin, and sin pays its wages honestly: death. Even the memory of the old slavery bears fruit we are now ashamed of, and its end never changes. And sin does not retire when we believe — it raids, it calls, it presents itself for our members every day. But the good news is that the believer's relation to sin has been settled at the cross before it is ever fought in the life: our old self was crucified with Christ, we were buried with Him and raised with Him, and sin's dominion is legally broken because we are not under law but under grace. The fight of Romans 6 is not a fight to become free; it is the fight of the free. Consider yourselves dead to sin and alive to God in Christ Jesus — and present your members, day by day, to the Master whose gift is eternal life.

A note from the Reformers. Luther's famous summary of the Christian — simul iustus et peccator, at the same time righteous and a sinner — breathes the air of this chapter: righteous completely, by the gift; still fighting sin daily, in the members. That is why Luther told believers to remember their baptism every day: you have already died the only death that finally matters, in Christ, and you have already been raised to the life that cannot end.

Reflection question: Paul says the fight begins with counting: "consider yourselves dead to sin and alive to God." Which specific sin still speaks to you as if it owned you — and what would change this week if you answered it from Romans 6:11 instead of from your feelings?
