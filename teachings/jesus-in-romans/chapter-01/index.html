Romans 1 — The Gospel of God, and the Wrath of God Revealed

Jesus in Romans — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Epistle to the Romans
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Jesus stands at the head of this letter before He is ever argued for. He is the promised Son — descended from David according to the flesh, declared to be the Son of God in power by His resurrection from the dead (1:3–4). He is the Lord through whom Paul received grace and apostleship, the One whose name the nations are called to obey in faith (1:5). And He is the heart of the gospel Paul is not ashamed of: the righteousness of God is revealed in Him, and the righteous live by faith in Him. But chapter 1 also shows us Jesus as Judge on the horizon — the gospel is preached against the backdrop of wrath revealed from heaven, and the Christ who saves is the same Christ before whom a truth-suppressing world stands without excuse.

Commentary

Paul and his gospel (1:1–7). Paul opens with the longest, richest introduction in any of his letters, and every word is loaded. He calls himself "a servant of Christ Jesus" — in Greek, a doulos, a bondslave. The man who once held the coats at a stoning now glories in being owned. He is "called to be an apostle, set apart for the gospel of God" — set apart, the same root idea as his old name for himself, a Pharisee, one of the separated ones. He was once separated to traditions; now he is separated to the gospel. And that gospel is not new: God "promised beforehand through his prophets in the holy Scriptures" — the gospel is as old as the promises. It concerns God's Son:

"concerning his Son, who was descended from David according to the flesh and was declared to be the Son of God in power according to the Spirit of holiness by his resurrection from the dead, Jesus Christ our Lord," — Romans 1:3–4 (ESV)

Two stages of the Son's life are held together: David's descendant in the flesh — the true King of Israel, born into the royal line — and declared Son of God in power by the resurrection. The resurrection did not make Jesus the Son; it declared Him, publicly vindicated Him, installed Him in power. Through this Lord, Paul received "grace and apostleship to bring about the obedience of faith for the sake of his name among all the nations" (1:5). Hold that phrase — the obedience of faith — because Paul will end the whole letter with it (16:26). Faith is not mere agreement; true faith obeys, and true obedience flows from faith. The greeting closes with grace and peace "from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ" — the letter's whole message in two words: grace is the source, peace is the result.

Paul's longing for Rome (1:8–15). Paul thanks God for these believers, "because your faith is proclaimed in all the world" — the faith of the Roman church was famous before Paul ever got there. He serves God with his spirit in the gospel, he prays for them without ceasing, and he has longed for years to come, hoping to impart some spiritual gift to strengthen them — and, he adds with an apostle's humility, to be mutually encouraged by each other's faith. He has often intended to come and been hindered. Then the obligation that drives him: "I am under obligation both to Greeks and to barbarians, both to the wise and to the foolish. So I am eager to preach the gospel to you also who are in Rome" (1:14–15). A debt he did not borrow and cannot repay except by preaching. The gospel is not Paul's possession; it is his trust, and he owes it to everyone.

The theme of the letter (1:16–17). Now Paul states the theme that the next fifteen chapters will unfold, and this commentary's introduction has already quoted it in full. Do not rush past its pieces. Not ashamed — in Rome, of all places, where the gospel sounded like news about a crucified Jew from a backwater province, Paul plants his flag: I am not ashamed. Why not? Because the gospel is the power of God — not a power among powers, not advice about power, but God's own power at work — for salvation, a rescue from sin and wrath and death, to everyone who believes — the condition is faith, and faith alone, and the offer runs to the Jew first and also to the Greek, God's own order of history. And the reason the gospel is power: "For in it the righteousness of God is revealed from faith for faith."

Here one Greek word carries the whole letter on its back: dikaiosynē — righteousness. Paul will use it again and again, and it means more than God's own righteous character, though it includes that. In Romans, the righteousness of God is also the righteous standing God gives — the verdict of "righteous" that He pronounces over the believing sinner and the righteous life He works in him. It is revealed "from faith for faith" — faith from first to last, faith all the way down. And Paul anchors it in the prophet: "as it is written, 'The righteous shall live by faith.'" Habakkuk spoke it in a day of coming judgment; Paul takes it as the charter of the gospel. The righteous — those declared righteous by God — live, now and forever, by faith and by nothing else.

The wrath of God revealed (1:18–23). Then, without a pause, Paul turns the page from gospel to wrath, and the turn is deliberate. You cannot understand the good news until you feel the weight of the bad news:

"For the wrath of God is revealed from heaven against all ungodliness and unrighteousness of men, who by their unrighteousness suppress the truth." — Romans 1:18 (ESV)

God's wrath is not a temper tantrum; it is His settled, holy opposition to evil — a judge's righteous sentence, revealed from heaven. And it falls on men who suppress the truth. That is the root sin: not ignorance, but suppression. "For what can be known about God is plain to them, because God has shown it to them. For his invisible attributes, namely, his eternal power and divine nature, have been clearly perceived, ever since the creation of the world, in the things that have been made. So they are without excuse" (1:19–20). Creation is a sermon no one can unhear. God's power and deity are on display in everything made, and so no human being will stand before God and plead that he never knew. The problem is not that men cannot see; it is that, seeing, "they did not honor him as God or give thanks to him, but they became futile in their thinking, and their foolish hearts were darkened. Claiming to be wise, they became fools, and exchanged the glory of the immortal God for images resembling mortal man and birds and animals and creeping things" (1:21–23). There is the great exchange of Romans 1: glory traded for images, the Creator traded for the creature, truth traded for a lie. Every idolatry since Eden is this exchange repeated.

God gave them up (1:24–32). Three times the dreadful sentence falls: "Therefore God gave them up" (1:24, 26, 28). This is wrath in its present form — not lightning from heaven, but the judicial handing-over of sinners to the sins they chose. God gives them up to impurity and the dishonoring of their bodies; He gives them up to dishonorable passions, to relations contrary to nature, men and women exchanging the natural for the unnatural; and He gives them up to a debased mind, to do what ought not to be done. Then Paul catalogs the wreckage: "all manner of unrighteousness, evil, covetousness, malice... envy, murder, strife, deceit, maliciousness... gossips, slanderers, haters of God, insolent, haughty, boastful, inventors of evil, disobedient to parents, foolish, faithless, heartless, ruthless" (1:29–31). And the last depth is the worst: "Though they know God's righteous decree that those who practice such things deserve to die, they not only do them but give approval to those who practice them" (1:32). Sin practiced is terrible; sin applauded is the bottom of the pit. Chapter 1 ends there — deliberately. Paul has not yet mentioned the church, the moralist, or the Jew. He is building a courtroom, and every human being will be brought into it before he is finished.

Key verses

"Paul, a servant of Christ Jesus, called to be an apostle, set apart for the gospel of God," — Romans 1:1 (ESV)

"For I am not ashamed of the gospel, for it is the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes, to the Jew first and also to the Greek. For in it the righteousness of God is revealed from faith for faith, as it is written, 'The righteous shall live by faith.'" — Romans 1:16–17 (ESV)

"For the wrath of God is revealed from heaven against all ungodliness and unrighteousness of men, who by their unrighteousness suppress the truth." — Romans 1:18 (ESV)

"For his invisible attributes, namely, his eternal power and divine nature, have been clearly perceived, ever since the creation of the world, in the things that have been made. So they are without excuse." — Romans 1:20 (ESV)

"Though they know God's righteous decree that those who practice such things deserve to die, they not only do them but give approval to those who practice them." — Romans 1:32 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Romans 1 is as bad as news gets: God's wrath is revealed against all ungodliness, and we are without excuse. We have seen His eternal power and divine nature in everything He made, and instead of honoring Him and giving thanks, we suppressed the truth and made the great exchange — His glory for our images, His truth for our lies — until He gave us up to the very sins we chose. That is not a description of some other people in some other place. That is the human race, and it is us. But the good news stands at the head of the chapter before the wrath is ever mentioned, and it stands there on purpose: the gospel is the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes. The same chapter that says "without excuse" opens by saying "not ashamed" — because there is a righteousness of God revealed, received by faith alone, for people exactly like the ones this chapter condemns. The righteous shall live by faith — and faith is a hand even a Romans 1 sinner can open.

A note from the Reformers. Luther called Romans "really the chief part of the New Testament, and the purest gospel of all" — and it was this chapter, this verse 17, that broke his chains. He wrote that when he finally saw that the righteousness of God is the gift by which the righteous live by faith, he felt altogether born again and entered paradise through open gates. Read Romans 1 the way Luther finally did: wrath is real, and so is the gate.

Reflection question: Paul says the root sin of the world is suppressing a truth it can plainly see. What truth about God have you been most tempted to suppress or trade away — and what would it look like to honor Him and give Him thanks for it today?
