Romans 13 — Love Fulfills the Law: Authorities, Neighbors, and the Coming Day

Jesus in Romans — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Epistle to the Romans
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Jesus stands at this chapter's end as the garment of the new day: "put on the Lord Jesus Christ" (13:14) — the Christian's whole public and private life is a wearing of Christ. He stands behind the chapter's opening too: the authorities that exist have been instituted by God (13:1), and the Christ who submitted to Pilate's unjust authority and answered Pilate that his power was given from above (John 19:11) is the model of the believer's posture — submission without worship, obedience without idolatry. And He is the coming One whose day is nearer than when we first believed (13:11), the Lord whose return turns ethics into urgency: the night is far gone, and we dress for the day He brings.

Commentary

Subject to the authorities (13:1–5). The transformed life of chapter 12 now steps into the public square, and its first word is submission: "Let every person be subject to the governing authorities. For there is no authority except from God, and those that exist have been instituted by God" (13:1). Paul writes this under Nero — early Nero, before the madness, but Nero nonetheless — to a church in the empire's capital. The principle is not that every ruler is good; it is that every ruler is under God, holding an office God instituted, answerable to God for it. "Therefore whoever resists the authorities resists what God has appointed, and those who resist will incur judgment" (13:2). The ruler's role is real and God-given: "he is God's servant for your good... he does not bear the sword in vain. For he is the servant of God, an avenger who carries out God's wrath on the wrongdoer" (13:4). The sword — capital and coercive authority — is delegated, not seized. And the believer's submission runs deeper than fear of punishment: "Therefore one must be in subjection, not only to avoid God's wrath but also for the sake of conscience" (13:5). Conscience, not convenience, is the ground. The Reformers held this chapter together with the rest of Scripture: submission is the rule; obedience to God rather than men is the boundary (Acts 5:29); and no authority on earth is God.

Pay what is owed (13:6–7). Submission gets practical at the tax office: "For because of this you also pay taxes, for the authorities are ministers of God, attending to this very thing. Pay to all what is owed to them: taxes to whom taxes are owed, revenue to whom revenue is owed, respect to whom respect is owed, honor to whom honor is owed" (13:6–7). Four debts, all paid without grumbling: taxes, revenue, respect, honor. The Christian is not a freeloader on the order God provides, and his respect for office-holders does not rise and fall with his opinion of the office-holder. Honor owed is honor paid — because the office is God's idea even when the man is not ours.

The debt that never clears (13:8–10). Then Paul turns the language of debt inside out: "Owe no one anything, except to love each other, for the one who loves another has fulfilled the law" (13:8). Every debt can be discharged except one — love is the perpetual obligation, the mortgage that never pays off, because the neighbor's need never runs out. And love is not a mood set against the commandments; it is their sum: "For the commandments, 'You shall not commit adultery, You shall not murder, You shall not steal, You shall not covet,' and any other commandment, are summed up in this word: 'You shall love your neighbor as yourself.' Love does no wrong to a neighbor; therefore love is the fulfilling of the law" (13:9–10). The second table of the law, gathered into one word. Love does no wrong — it does not merely feel kindly; it refrains, it protects, it provides. This is chapter 12's genuine love in its street clothes.

The hour has come (13:11–14). The chapter closes with a clock and a wardrobe. "Besides this you know the time, that the hour has come for you to wake from sleep. For salvation is nearer to us now than when we first believed. The night is far gone; the day is at hand" (13:11–12). Every day the believer lives is one day nearer the end of the story — salvation, in its full and final sense, is nearer now, and nearer still by the time you finish this page. Urgency, in Paul, is not panic; it is wakefulness. "So then let us cast off the works of darkness and put on the armor of light. Let us walk properly as in the daytime, not in orgies and drunkenness, not in sexual immorality and sensuality, not in quarreling and jealousy" (13:12–13). The list is a nightclub and a boardroom at once — the darkness works in both. And the final command gathers the whole Christian life into one act of dressing:

"But put on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make no provision for the flesh, to gratify its desires." — Romans 13:14 (ESV)

Put on the Lord Jesus Christ — as a man puts on his clothes in the morning, deliberately, completely, for the day. And its negative twin: make no provision for the flesh — no stocking the pantry for the old appetite, no keeping the key to the old room. It was this verse, read in a garden in Milan in the year 386 — "take up and read," a child's voice had said — that broke Augustine's chains and turned the great sinner into the great doctor of the church. Romans 13:14 has been ending old lives ever since.

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Romans 13 is that we are sleepwalkers by nature. We chafe at authority as if God had not appointed it, we nurse debts of love unpaid while paying every other bill, and we make quiet provision for the flesh — keeping the old appetites supplied while professing the new life. The night suits us; darkness is where the old man is comfortable. But the good news is that the night is far gone and the day is at hand — salvation is nearer now than when we first believed, and the nearness is mercy, not threat, to those who are in Christ. God has instituted authority so the world is not chaos, summed up the law in a love He Himself supplies, and given us the only garment the new day requires: the Lord Jesus Christ Himself, to be put on every morning. We do not have to manufacture daylight. We only have to wake up, dress in Christ, and walk as what we already are — children of the coming day.

A note from the Reformers. Luther taught this chapter with his doctrine of God's two governments: God rules the world through the law and the sword to restrain evil, and rules His church through the gospel alone — and the Christian lives in both kingdoms at once, subject to the magistrate in body and goods, free in conscience before God. Confuse the two kingdoms, he warned, and you lose both the gospel and the peace of society.

Reflection question: "Make no provision for the flesh" is a logistics command — it is about supply lines. What provision have you been keeping in place for a sin you profess to be fighting — an arrangement, a habit, a kept key — and what would cutting that supply line look like this week?
