Romans 15 — The God of Hope: Christ the Servant of Jews and Gentiles

Jesus in Romans — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Epistle to the Romans

Jesus in this chapter

Jesus is the pattern of the whole chapter: the strong are to bear with the weak because "Christ did not please himself" (15:3). He is the servant of the circumcised who confirmed the promises to the patriarchs, and the root of Jesse in whom the Gentiles hope (15:8–12) — one Savior with two directions of mercy, to Israel according to promise and to the nations according to prophecy. He is the source of the chapter's benediction: the God of hope fills us with joy and peace in believing through Him, and Paul's whole ambition is to preach Him where He has not been named (15:20). Romans 15 is the letter turning to leave — and Christ is still the subject of every sentence.

Commentary

Bear with the weak, as Christ did (15:1–6). Chapter 14's ethic now reaches its summit: "We who are strong have an obligation to bear with the failings of the weak, and not to please ourselves. Let each of us please his neighbor for his good, to build him up" (15:1–2). Obligation — strength is not a privilege but a duty, and its duty is carrying, not criticizing. And the ground is Christ Himself: "For Christ did not please himself, but as it is written, 'The reproaches of those who reproached you fell on me'" (15:3, quoting Psalm 69:9). The strongest One who ever lived used His strength to absorb reproach that was aimed at God and at us. Self-pleasing is the one luxury the cross permanently retired. Then Paul states the purpose of the whole Old Testament for the church: "For whatever was written in former days was written for our instruction, that through endurance and through the encouragement of the Scriptures we might have hope" (15:4). The Scriptures are hope's supply line. And the benediction that follows names God's two gifts to a divided church: "May the God of endurance and encouragement grant you to live in such harmony with one another, in accord with Christ Jesus, that together you may with one voice glorify the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ" (15:5–6). With one voice — Jew and Gentile, weak and strong, one mouth glorifying God. That is the destination of the whole letter's argument.

Welcome, as Christ welcomed (15:7–13). The command that gathers chapters 14–15 into one sentence: "Therefore welcome one another as Christ has welcomed you, for the glory of God" (15:7). The measure of our welcome is Christ's welcome of us — and He welcomed us while we were sinners, enemies, weak, ungodly. Then Paul shows the two directions of that welcome in Christ's own ministry: "For I tell you that Christ became a servant to the circumcised to show God's truthfulness, in order to confirm the promises given to the patriarchs, and in order that the Gentiles might glorify God for his mercy" (15:8–9). To Israel, Christ came as promise-keeper; to the Gentiles, as mercy-giver — and both end in the same place: glory to God. Then a choir of Old Testament voices sings the Gentiles in: "Rejoice, O Gentiles, with his people" (Deuteronomy 32:43); "Praise the Lord, all you Gentiles, and let all the peoples extol him" (Psalm 117:1); and Isaiah's root of Jesse, who "shall come... in him will the Gentiles hope" (Isaiah 11:10). The inclusion of the nations was never plan B. And the section closes with the letter's most beloved benediction:

"May the God of hope fill you with all joy and peace in believing, so that by the power of the Holy Spirit you may abound in hope." — Romans 15:13 (ESV)

The God of hope — hope is not a temperament; it is a Person's gift, and He gives it in filling measure: joy and peace, in believing, abounding.

Paul's ambition and plans (15:14–29). Paul now turns personal, and the personal section is a window into an apostle's heart. He is satisfied about the Romans — full of goodness, filled with knowledge, able to instruct one another — yet he has written boldly "by way of reminder," because of the grace given him "to be a minister of Christ Jesus to the Gentiles in the priestly service of the gospel of God, so that the offering of the Gentiles may be acceptable, sanctified by the Holy Spirit" (15:15–16). Paul sees his evangelism as priesthood and the converted nations as the offering. He glories only in what Christ has accomplished through him — by word and deed, by signs and wonders, by the Spirit's power — from Jerusalem around to Illyricum, the whole eastern world evangelized (15:17–19). And the ambition that drove it: "I make it my ambition to preach the gospel, not where Christ has already been named, lest I build on someone else's foundation" (15:20). The pioneer spirit, aimed at the unreached. His plans follow: hindered often, now coming at last — after delivering the relief collection to Jerusalem's poor (the Gentile churches' gift to the Jewish saints, the letter's theology in an act of money and love), then Rome, then Spain (15:22–29). But under the plans runs an honest fear: he asks them to strive together in prayer "that I may be delivered from the unbelievers in Judea, and that my service for Jerusalem may be acceptable to the saints, so that by God's will I may come to you with joy and be refreshed in your company" (15:30–32). The apostle of the sovereign God begs for prayer as if everything depended on it — because in God's ordering, it does. He will come to Rome, though not as he planned: in chains, under guard, through shipwreck — and refreshed, exactly as promised, by means he never chose.

The God of peace be with you (15:30–33). The chapter closes with the blessing that chapter 16 will echo: "The God of peace be with you all. Amen" (15:33). Peace has been the letter's thread — peace with God (5:1), the God of peace who will crush Satan (16:20), and here the God of peace present with His people. The doctrinal giant ends its practical section the way a pastor ends a visit: with a blessing.

"and thus I make it my ambition to preach the gospel, not where Christ has already been named, lest I build on someone else's foundation," — Romans 15:20 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Romans 15 is that self-pleasing is the natural religion of the strong. We use our strength to criticize the weak rather than carry them, we treat our welcome as a reward for people who agree with us, and our hope runs dry because we try to fill ourselves. Even the chapter's divisions are ours: one voice glorifying God is the goal, and our voices are usually raised at each other. But the good news is that Christ did not please Himself. He became a servant to the circumcised to confirm the promises, He absorbed the reproaches that fell on us, He welcomed us at our worst — and from that welcome flows everything: a church that welcomes as it was welcomed, Scriptures that fill us with encouragement, and a God of hope who fills His people with all joy and peace in believing until hope abounds. The chapter's benediction is not a wish. It is a description of what the God of hope does.

A note from the Reformers. Spurgeon preached Romans 15:13 as the believer's birthright: hope is not a scarce ration for the strong in faith but the filling of the God of hope for everyone who believes — joy and peace are the Spirit's ordinary work, and a hope that abounds is meant to overflow onto the hopeless. He would have added what verse 4 teaches: the way to that filling runs through the Scriptures, read and believed.

Reflection question: Paul says the God of hope fills us in believing — hope abounds in the direction of trust. Where has your hope run dry, and which promise from this letter will you deliberately believe — out loud, in prayer — until joy and peace begin to refill it?
