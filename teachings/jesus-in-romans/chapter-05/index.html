Romans 5 — Peace with God: The Fruits of Justification and the Two Adams

Jesus in Romans — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Epistle to the Romans
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Jesus is the One through whom everything in this chapter flows — Paul says "through our Lord Jesus Christ" the way a river says "downhill." Through Him we have peace with God; through Him we have obtained access into grace; in Him we rejoice in hope of the glory of God. He is the proof of God's love: "while we were still sinners, Christ died for us" (5:8). And He is the last Adam, the second Head of the human race, whose one act of righteousness leads to justification and life for all who are in Him — the obedience that answers Adam's disobedience, the gift that abounds over the trespass. Romans 5 is where the justified sinner stops looking at the courtroom and starts looking at Christ, and finds that Christ is better than he dared hope.

Commentary

Peace, access, and hope (5:1–5). The chapter opens with the most restful word in the letter — therefore — and the first fruit of justification:

"Therefore, since we have been justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ." — Romans 5:1 (ESV)

Peace with God — not first the feeling of peace, but the fact of it: the war is over. The hostility that chapters 1–3 described — wrath above, rebellion below — has been ended by justification, and ended God's way, through our Lord Jesus Christ. "Through him we have also obtained access by faith into this grace in which we stand" (5:2) — access, the word for being ushered into a king's presence. We do not visit grace on occasion; we stand in it, permanently stationed. "And we rejoice in hope of the glory of God" (5:2). And then the chain that turns suffering inside out: "we rejoice in our sufferings, knowing that suffering produces endurance, and endurance produces character, and character produces hope, and hope does not put us to shame, because God's love has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us" (5:3–5). The justified man is not promised a life without suffering; he is promised a suffering that goes somewhere — endurance, character, hope — and a hope that will never shame him, because it rests on love already poured into his heart by the Spirit. Justification is not the end of the Christian's story. It is the ground the whole story stands on.

Christ died for the ungodly (5:6–11). Now Paul proves the love of God from the timing of the cross. "For while we were still weak, at the right time Christ died for the ungodly" (5:6). Weak — helpless, unable to help ourselves. And scarce is the man who would die even for a righteous man, though for a good man someone might dare to die. "But God shows his love for us in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us" (5:8). Not while we were improved. Not while we were promising. While we were still sinners. The cross is God's love demonstrated in the past tense, at our worst, before we had done one thing to deserve it — which is exactly why it can be trusted in the present. And if God did the greater then, He will surely do the lesser now: "Since, therefore, we have now been justified by his blood, much more shall we be saved by him from the wrath of God. For if while we were enemies we were reconciled to God by the death of his Son, much more, now that we are reconciled, shall we be saved by his life" (5:9–10).

The Greek word for that reconciling is katallagē — reconciliation, the exchange of hostility for friendship. We did not reconcile ourselves; we were reconciled, passive, by the death of His Son, while we were still enemies. And the saved life that follows is by His life — the risen Christ keeps those the crucified Christ reconciled. "More than that, we also rejoice in God through our Lord Jesus Christ, through whom we have now received reconciliation" (5:11). The chapter that began with peace ends with joy: the justified do not merely escape wrath; they rejoice in God.

Adam and Christ (5:12–21). Now Paul steps back to the widest view in the letter: the whole human race under two heads. "Therefore, just as sin came into the world through one man, and death through sin, and so death spread to all men because all sinned" (5:12). Adam's trespass in Eden was not a private fall. In Adam — our representative, our head — the race fell, and death spread to all: the cemeteries of the world are the evidence of Eden. Death reigned from Adam to Moses even over those whose sinning was not like Adam's transgression — even over infants who broke no command — because death is not only the wage of personal sins but the inheritance of a fallen headship. Adam, Paul says, "was a type of the one who was to come" (5:14) — a pattern, a foreshadowing. And then the glorious how much more begins. The free gift is not like the trespass: "For if many died through one man's trespass, much more have the grace of God and the free gift by the grace of that one man Jesus Christ abounded for many" (5:15). One trespass brought condemnation; the gift, following many trespasses, brings justification (5:16) — grace does not merely match our sin; it outruns it, trespass after trespass. "For if, because of one man's trespass, death reigned through that one man, much more will those who receive the abundance of grace and the free gift of righteousness reign in life through the one man Jesus Christ" (5:17). Reign in life — the slaves of death become kings of life, not by their grip on grace but by receiving its abundance.

The conclusion gathers it all: "Therefore, as one trespass led to condemnation for all men, so one act of righteousness leads to justification and life for all men. For as by the one man's disobedience the many were made sinners, so by the one man's obedience the many will be made righteous" (5:18–19). Two men, two acts, two humanities. Every person who has ever lived is in Adam by birth or in Christ by faith — there is no third country. And the final word belongs to grace: "Now the law came in to increase the trespass, but where sin increased, grace abounded all the more, so that, as sin reigned in death, grace also might reign through righteousness leading to eternal life through Jesus Christ our Lord" (5:20–21). Sin reigned — past tense for the believer. Grace reigns now. And grace does not reign by ignoring righteousness but through it, all the way to eternal life.

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Romans 5 is that we were born into a lost race. Sin came into the world through one man, and death through sin, and death spread to all — we were weak, ungodly, sinners, and enemies, every word of it true at once. We did not merely commit sins; we inherited a kingdom of death and lived as its loyal subjects. But the good news outruns the bad at every point, and Paul says "much more" until we believe it. While we were still sinners, Christ died for us. Justified by His blood, we have peace with God — the war is over, and God ended it. Reconciled as enemies, we are saved by His life. And the second Adam has undone the first: where sin increased, grace abounded all the more, and those who receive the abundance of grace reign in life through the one man Jesus Christ. In Adam, death. In Christ, life — and life is the stronger of the two.

A note from the Reformers. Martyn Lloyd-Jones preached through Romans at Westminster Chapel in London for over a decade, and he lingered over chapter 5 because, he said, it is where the Christian's assurance is built — not on feelings, but on an argument: if God did the greater thing when we were enemies, He will not fail in the lesser now that we are His children. Assurance in Romans 5 is not a mood. It is a conclusion.

Reflection question: Paul argues from the greater to the lesser: God saved you when you were His enemy, so He will surely keep you now that you are His child. What present fear about God's commitment to you does that argument answer — and will you let the cross, not your feelings, have the last word on it today?
