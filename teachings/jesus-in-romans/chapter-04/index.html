Romans 4 — Abraham Believed God: Justified by Faith, Counted Righteous

Jesus in Romans — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Epistle to the Romans
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Jesus is the Seed and the substance of Abraham's faith in this chapter. Abraham believed the God "who gives life to the dead and calls into existence the things that do not exist" (4:17) — and the supreme proof of that life-giving power is the resurrection of Jesus, through whom Abraham's blessing reaches the world. At the chapter's end, Jesus stands in His full saving work in a single verse: He "was delivered up for our trespasses and raised for our justification" (4:25). The cross answers our trespasses; the resurrection secures our justification. Abraham looked forward to Him by promise; we look back at Him accomplished — and both stand righteous the same way: by faith.

Commentary

The question of Abraham (4:1–3). Paul has claimed that justification is by faith apart from works, and he knows exactly where his Jewish readers will go: what about Abraham, the father of the nation, the friend of God? "What then shall we say was gained by Abraham, our forefather according to the flesh? For if Abraham was justified by works, he has something to boast about, but not before God" (4:1–2). If works justify, boasting lives. Paul answers with Genesis itself:

"For what does the Scripture say? 'Abraham believed God, and it was counted to him as righteousness.'" — Romans 4:3 (ESV)

Genesis 15:6 — and notice the timing, because Paul's whole argument leans on it. Abraham believed and was counted righteous in Genesis 15. The covenant sign of circumcision does not arrive until Genesis 17, and the law will not come for another four hundred years after that. Abraham was justified as an uncircumcised believer, by faith, before a single work of the law existed to perform. The father of the Jews is the pattern of the Gentile Christian: faith first, and everything else after.

Counted: the great bookkeeping word (4:4–8). Now Paul unpacks the word counted, and it is one of the load-bearing words of the gospel. The Greek is logizomai — a bookkeeping term: to reckon, to credit to an account, to enter in the ledger. Paul will use it eleven times in this chapter. "Now to the one who works, his wages are not counted as a gift but as his due. And to the one who does not work but believes in him who justifies the ungodly, his faith is counted as righteousness" (4:4–5). Wages and gifts are opposite ledgers: wages are owed; gifts are given. God justifies — and linger on the scandal of the phrase — the ungodly. Not the improved, not the promising, not the almost-there. The ungodly, who bring nothing but their sin and their believing. And the faith itself is counted as righteousness: the righteousness credited to Abraham's account was not Abraham's own; it was entered by God's gracious reckoning when Abraham believed. Then Paul brings David as a second witness, quoting Psalm 32: "Blessed are those whose lawless deeds are forgiven, and whose sins are covered; blessed is the man against whom the Lord will not count his sin" (4:7–8). Forgiveness, covering, non-counting — the negative side of the same ledger. God does not count the believer's sin against him, because He has counted it against Another, and He counts the believer righteous, because Another's righteousness stands in his place.

Before circumcision (4:9–12). Is this blessing for the circumcised only? Paul presses the chronology: "How then was it counted to him? Was it before or after he had been circumcised? It was not after, but before he was circumcised" (4:10). Circumcision came afterward, as "a sign and seal of the righteousness that he had by faith while he was still uncircumcised" (4:11) — a seal stamps what already exists; it does not create it. And the purpose was universal: "that he might be the father of all who believe without being circumcised... and likewise the father of the circumcised" who walk in the footsteps of his faith (4:11–12). Abraham is too big for one nation. He is the father of everyone who believes — which is to say, the father of the whole church of Jesus Christ, Jew and Gentile together, all justified the way he was.

Promise, not law (4:13–17). "For the promise to Abraham and his offspring that he would be heir of the world did not come through the law but through the righteousness of faith" (4:13). Heir of the world — the promise outgrew Canaan long ago; Romans will show it swallowing the nations. Law and promise work on opposite principles: "For if it is the adherents of the law who are to be the heirs, faith is null and the promise is void. For the law brings wrath, but where there is no law there is no transgression" (4:14–15). That is why the inheritance "depends on faith, in order that the promise may rest on grace and be guaranteed to all his offspring" (4:16). Grace is the only foundation that can guarantee anything to sinners. And the God Abraham believed is named in one of the great descriptions of God in the Bible: the God "who gives life to the dead and calls into existence the things that do not exist" (4:17). Resurrection power and creation power — the two things Abraham's situation required, since what God had promised him was, humanly speaking, a resurrection and a creation: a son from a dead body and a barren womb.

Against hope, in hope (4:18–22). "In hope he believed against hope, that he should become the father of many nations" (4:18). Abraham did the math honestly — "he considered his own body, which was as good as dead (since he was about a hundred years old), or when he considered the barrenness of Sarah's womb" (4:19) — and believed anyway. Faith is not pretending the facts are other than they are; it is reckoning God greater than the facts. "No unbelief made him waver concerning the promise of God, but he grew strong in his faith as he gave glory to God, fully convinced that God was able to do what he had promised. That is why his faith was 'counted to him as righteousness'" (4:20–22). He grew strong as he gave glory to God — faith strengthens in worship, not in self-examination. And note what Abraham's faith rested on: not his own sincerity, but God's ability. "Fully convinced that God was able" — the object of faith is what makes faith strong.

Written for us (4:23–25). Then Paul turns the whole ancient story toward the reader, and the turn is the point of the chapter: "But the words 'it was counted to him' were not written for his sake alone, but for ours also. It will be counted to us who believe in him who raised from the dead Jesus our Lord, who was delivered up for our trespasses and raised for our justification" (4:23–25). Abraham's faith had a promise to believe; ours has an accomplished Savior. We believe in the same life-giving God, now known by His supreme act: He raised Jesus from the dead. And the last verse gathers cross and empty tomb into the doctrine of justification itself:

"who was delivered up for our trespasses and raised for our justification." — Romans 4:25 (ESV)

Delivered up for our trespasses — our sins were the cause of His death. Raised for our justification — our acquittal is the fruit of His resurrection. The empty tomb is God's receipt, stamped and published, that the payment was accepted in full.

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Romans 4 is that the ledger of works is closed to us. If justification were wages, we would have to earn it — and what we have actually earned, chapter 3 has already told us. Abraham himself, the greatest name in Israel's history, had nothing to boast about before God. But the good news is that God keeps a different ledger. He justifies the ungodly — the one who does not work but believes — and counts his faith as righteousness. He does not count the believer's sin against him, because He counted it against His Son, delivered up for our trespasses; and He counts the believer righteous, because His Son was raised for our justification. Abraham believed God against hope, with a dead body and a barren womb in front of him, and it was counted to him. The same counting stands open today to everyone who believes in the God who raised Jesus from the dead.

A note from the Reformers. Calvin said Abraham is set before us as a mirror in which every believer may see himself: justified before the sign, before the law, before any work — by faith alone, resting on a God who gives life to the dead. The Reformers loved this chapter because it proves justification by faith is not a New Testament invention; it is the way God has saved sinners since Genesis 15.

Reflection question: Abraham "considered" the hopeless facts and believed God anyway. What dead-looking situation in your life are you being asked to consider honestly — and then trust the God who gives life to the dead with it, by name, today?
