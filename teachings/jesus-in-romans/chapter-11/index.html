Romans 11 — The Olive Tree: Remnant, Grafting, and the Mercy of God

Jesus in Romans — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Epistle to the Romans
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Jesus is the root of the olive tree in this chapter — the holy root that bears the branches, not the other way around (11:16–18). He is the Deliverer who will come from Zion, who will banish ungodliness from Jacob and take away their sins, according to the covenant God will keep (11:26–27). He is the one through whom God's gifts and calling to Israel remain irrevocable (11:29), and He is the center of the great doxology that ends the doctrinal half of the letter: all things are from Him and through Him and to Him (11:36). Romans 11 will not let the church boast against Israel, because the church's life is sap drawn from a root that is not its own — and the root has a name.

Commentary

Has God rejected His people? (11:1–6). Paul asks the question chapters 9–10 have raised and answers it with his own biography: "I ask, then, has God rejected his people? By no means! For I myself am an Israelite, a descendant of Abraham, a member of the tribe of Benjamin" (11:1). Exhibit A is the apostle himself — a Hebrew of Hebrews, standing in the pulpit, saved. "God has not rejected his people whom he foreknew" (11:2). Then Elijah is summoned: in the darkest hour of Israel's apostasy, Elijah prayed against Israel and believed himself alone — "I have been very jealous for the LORD... and I, even I only, am left." God's answer corrected the prophet's arithmetic: "I have kept for myself seven thousand men who have not bowed the knee to Baal" (11:2–4, quoting 1 Kings 19:18). God's remnant is always larger than the discouraged prophet thinks. "So too at the present time there is a remnant, chosen by grace. But if it is by grace, it is no longer on the basis of works; otherwise grace would no longer be grace" (11:5–6). That last sentence is a fence around the whole gospel: grace and works are mutually exclusive currencies. Mix them and you have destroyed both — a grace that must be supplemented is no longer grace at all.

Hardening and jealousy (11:7–12). The summary is blunt: "Israel failed to obtain what it was seeking. The elect obtained it, but the rest were hardened" (11:7) — hardened, as Scripture warned, with a spirit of stupor, eyes that would not see and ears that would not hear, "down to this very day" (11:8). David's table became a snare. But the hardening is neither total nor final, and Paul asks the chapter's second great question: "So I ask, did they stumble in order that they might fall? By no means! Rather, through their trespass salvation has come to the Gentiles, so as to make Israel jealous" (11:11). Israel's stumble became the world's doorway — the gospel, refused in the synagogue, went out to the nations, exactly as the pattern of Acts shows city by city. And the purpose circles back: to make Israel jealous — to provoke the elder brother by the sight of the feast. "Now if their trespass means riches for the world, and if their failure means riches for the Gentiles, how much more will their full inclusion mean!" (11:12). If Israel's failure enriched the world, what will Israel's fullness do? Paul's logic runs uphill: the story of Israel is not a tragedy trailing off; it is a river dammed for a season, and the release will flood the world.

Paul's ministry and the dead raised (11:13–16). Paul now addresses the Gentile readers directly — "Now I am speaking to you Gentiles" — and explains his own strategy: he magnifies his ministry to Israel's people "in order somehow to make my fellow Jews jealous, and thus save some of them" (11:13–14). The apostle to the Gentiles never stopped being an evangelist to the Jews; his Gentile mission was his Jewish mission, run by the long road of providence. "For if their rejection means the reconciliation of the world, what will their acceptance mean but life from the dead?" (11:15). Life from the dead — resurrection language, and Paul chooses it deliberately: Israel's acceptance will be like a resurrection morning for the world. Then the figures of root and lump: "If the dough offered as firstfruits is holy, so is the whole lump, and if the root is holy, so are the branches" (11:16). The patriarchs are the firstfruits and the root; the holiness of the root reaches the branches. Israel's story is consecrated at its source — and the church is about to be warned not to forget whose root it is growing from.

The olive tree: warning to the grafted (11:17–24). Now the chapter's famous picture. Some natural branches — unbelieving Israel — were broken off, "and you, although a wild olive shoot, were grafted in among the others and now share in the nourishing root of the olive tree" (11:17). Gentile believer: you are a wild shoot in a cultivated tree, living on sap you did not grow. So: "do not be arrogant toward the branches. If you are, remember it is not you who support the root, but the root that supports you" (11:18). The warning tightens: branches were broken off because of unbelief; "you stand fast through faith. So do not become proud, but fear. For if God did not spare the natural branches, neither will he spare you" (11:20–21). And then the verse that holds the whole chapter in balance: "Note then the kindness and the severity of God: severity toward those who have fallen, but God's kindness to you, provided you continue in his kindness. Otherwise you too will be cut off" (11:22). Kindness and severity — the two hands of God in history, and the church is to note both. Perseverance is not presumption; standing is by faith, daily. But severity is not the last word for the broken branches either: "And even they, if they do not continue in their unbelief, will be grafted in, for God has the power to graft them in again" (11:23). God has the power. And how much more readily will the natural branches be grafted into their own olive tree (11:24)!

The mystery and the mercy (11:25–32). "Lest you be wise in your own sight, I do not want you to be unaware of this mystery, brothers: a partial hardening has come upon Israel, until the fullness of the Gentiles has come in. And in this way all Israel will be saved" (11:25–26). Partial hardening — not all Israel, and not forever. Until — the hardening has an expiration date, set by the fullness of the Gentiles coming in. And then: "all Israel will be saved," as the prophets promised: "The Deliverer will come from Zion, he will banish ungodliness from Jacob; and this will be my covenant with them when I take away their sins" (11:26–27). Whatever debates surround the details, Paul's direction is unmistakable: God's story with Israel ends in mercy, by covenant, through the Deliverer. "As regards the gospel, they are enemies for your sake. But as regards election, they are beloved for the sake of their forefathers. For the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable" (11:28–29). Irrevocable — God does not take back His gifts or unsay His call. And then the great leveler, which folds chapters 9–11 into one sentence: "For God has consigned all to disobedience, that he may have mercy on all" (11:32). Jew and Gentile, the whole human race, shut up together in disobedience — so that the whole human race might be met by one thing: mercy. The prison has one door, and the door is mercy.

The doxology (11:33–36). Argument ends in worship, the way all true theology must: "Oh, the depth of the riches and wisdom and knowledge of God! How unsearchable are his judgments and how inscrutable his ways! 'For who has known the mind of the Lord, or who has been his counselor?' 'Or who has given a gift to him that he might be repaid?' For from him and through him and to him are all things. To him be glory forever. Amen" (11:33–36). Paul has spent three chapters tracing God's sovereignty and mercy through the hardest questions in the Bible, and he does not end with a system. He ends on his knees. From Him — the source. Through Him — the means. To Him — the goal. All things. And the glory is His forever. Every commentary on Romans 9–11 should end where Paul ends: not with the reader congratulating himself for understanding, but with the reader worshiping a God whose ways are beyond tracing out — and whose mercy has reached even him.

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Romans 11 is that branches can be broken off. Unbelief is not a safe condition for anyone — not for Israel with all her privileges, and not for the Gentile believer who grows arrogant toward the root that bears him. God is severe toward those who fall, His hardening is real, and "continue in his kindness" is a warning as much as a comfort. Standing is by faith, or it is not standing at all. But the good news towers over the chapter: there is a remnant chosen by grace; the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable; the broken branches can be grafted in again because God has the power; and over the whole human race — consigned, all of it, to disobedience — God has written one purpose: that He may have mercy on all. The chapter that begins with hardening ends with the deepest doxology in the New Testament. Kindness and severity are both true, but mercy is the last word, and the last word belongs to God.

A note from the Reformers. Calvin ended his commentary on this chapter the way Paul ends the chapter itself — in adoration, remarking that the more we understand of God's purposes, the more we should be led not to pry but to worship. The Reformers' rule for Romans 9–11 is written across verse 33: where the depth is unfathomable, the right response is not a ladder but a bowed head — to him be glory forever.

Reflection question: Paul warns the grafted branches: "it is not you who support the root, but the root that supports you." Where has spiritual pride — toward unbelievers, toward other believers, toward Israel — crept into your heart, and what would humble gratitude for the root look like in your prayers this week?
