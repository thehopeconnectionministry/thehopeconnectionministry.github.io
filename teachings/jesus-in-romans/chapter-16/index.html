Romans 16 — Greetings and Glory: The Family of the Gospel

Jesus in Romans — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Epistle to the Romans
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Jesus is the bond of every name in this chapter. These twenty-six people are not Paul's contacts; they are people "in Christ" — Paul says it of them again and again: in Christ Jesus, in the Lord, approved in Christ, chosen in the Lord. He is the one in whom Phoebe serves, in whom Prisca and Aquila risked their necks, in whom a household believes, in whom slaves and senators' families alike are one. He is the Christ of the gospel that strengthens the nations (16:25–26), the one through whom glory is given to the only wise God forever (16:27). And He is the coming crusher behind the chapter's promise: "The God of peace will soon crush Satan under your feet" (16:20) — Genesis 3:15's Seed of the woman, finishing His war under the feet of His church.

Commentary

Phoebe and the letter itself (16:1–2). The chapter begins with the letter's own courier, and Paul commends her the way every believer deserves to be commended: "I commend to you our sister Phoebe, a servant of the church at Cenchreae, that you may welcome her in the Lord in a way worthy of the saints, and help her in whatever she may need from you, for she has been a patron of many and of myself as well" (16:1–2). A woman carries the greatest letter ever written across the empire, and Paul calls her sister, servant — the word is diakonos, deacon — and patron, a benefactor who has stood by many, including the apostle himself. The gospel's history runs on such people: unnamed in the world's histories, named forever in God's.

Prisca and Aquila, and the roll of honor (16:3–16). Then the names come like a drumbeat of grace. Prisca and Aquila, "my fellow workers in Christ Jesus, who risked their necks for my life, to whom not only I give thanks but all the churches of the Gentiles give thanks as well" (16:3–4) — tentmakers who once housed Paul in Corinth, now hosting a church in their house in Rome. Epaenetus, "my beloved... the first convert to Christ in Asia." Mary, "who has worked hard for you." Andronicus and Junia, kinsmen and fellow prisoners, "well known to the apostles," in Christ before Paul was. Ampliatus, Urbanus, Stachys, Apelles "approved in Christ," the household of Aristobulus, Herodion, the household of Narcissus, Tryphaena and Tryphosa "workers in the Lord," Persis "the beloved" who worked hard, Rufus "chosen in the Lord" and his mother — who had been a mother to Paul too. Asyncritus, Phlegon, Hermes, Patrobas, Hermas, Philologus and Julia, Nereus and his sister, Olympas, "and all the saints who are with them." Read the list slowly and see what the gospel had built in the capital of the world within twenty-five years of the resurrection: slaves and free, Jews and Gentiles, men and women, households and singles, prisoners and patrons — one family, greeted by name. Paul ends the greetings the way the family behaves: "Greet one another with a holy kiss. All the churches of Christ greet you" (16:16).

The warning (16:17–20). Into the warmth comes one sharp paragraph, and its sharpness is pastoral: "I appeal to you, brothers, to watch out for those who cause divisions and create obstacles contrary to the doctrine that you have been taught; avoid them. For such persons do not serve our Lord Christ, but their own appetites, and by smooth talk and flattery they deceive the hearts of the naive" (16:17–18). Division-makers are marked by two things: doctrine contrary to what was taught, and service of their own appetites dressed in smooth talk. The church that welcomes everyone Christ welcomed (15:7) must still watch the door — welcome and watchfulness are the same love in two directions. The Romans' obedience is famous, Paul says, and he wants them "wise as to what is good and innocent as to what is evil" (16:19). Then the promise that reaches back to Eden: "The God of peace will soon crush Satan under your feet" (16:20). The serpent who crushed man's heel will be crushed under the church's feet by the God of peace — peace, in Romans, is not the absence of war but the presence of the winning God. "The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with you" (16:20) — the letter's signature blessing, grace from first to last.

The senders and the doxology (16:21–27). Paul's companions add their greetings — Timothy, Lucius, Jason, Sosipater — and Tertius, the scribe, signs his own line: "I Tertius, who wrote this letter, greet you in the Lord" (16:22). Gaius, host to Paul and to the whole church; Erastus, the city treasurer of Corinth — the gospel had reached the city hall; Quartus, "the brother." And then the letter that began "Paul, a servant of Christ Jesus" ends not with Paul at all, but with God:

"Now to him who is able to strengthen you according to my gospel and the preaching of Jesus Christ, according to the revelation of the mystery that was kept secret for long ages but has now been disclosed and through the prophetic writings has been made known to all nations, according to the command of the eternal God, to bring about the obedience of faith — to the only wise God be glory forevermore through Jesus Christ! Amen." — Romans 16:25–27 (ESV)

Everything in the letter is gathered here. The gospel strengthens. The mystery — Christ for the nations, Jew and Gentile in one body — was kept secret for long ages and is now disclosed, through the prophetic writings, made known to all nations. The goal is the letter's first phrase come home: the obedience of faith (1:5; 16:26), among all the nations, for the sake of His name. And the end of the end is glory: to the only wise God, through Jesus Christ, forevermore. Paul signs off the way the whole Bible signs off — with the glory going where it belongs.

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Romans 16 is that the family has enemies — smooth talkers who cause divisions, serve their own appetites, and deceive the naive — and that Satan is still, for now, under no one's feet but at large in the world. A church that only welcomes and never watches will be devoured; the last chapter of the greatest letter does not end in naivety. But the good news is the chapter's whole atmosphere: the gospel makes a family. Twenty-six names — servants and patrons, prisoners and mothers, the approved and the beloved — bound in one Lord in the heart of a hostile empire. And over that family stands the promise and the doxology: the God of peace will soon crush Satan under your feet, the mystery is disclosed to all nations for the obedience of faith, and to the only wise God be glory forevermore through Jesus Christ. Romans ends where it began — grace and glory, God's from first to last.

A note from the Reformers. The Reformers loved this chapter because it proves their doctrine of the church: the true church is not a hierarchy of the impressive but a communion of saints — Phoebes and Priscas, slaves and treasurers, each named, each in Christ. Luther said the church is found wherever the gospel is preached and believed; Romans 16 is what that looks like with its sleeves rolled up — ordinary people, eternally named.

Reflection question: Paul ends the letter by naming twenty-six ordinary people and their faithfulness. Who are the "Romans 16" names in your life — the fellow workers, risk-takers, and mothers-in-the-faith God has given you — and which one will you greet by name and thank this week?

Closing — Romans in One Sentence

Sixteen chapters, and they can be carried in a single sentence:

The gospel of God concerning His Son is the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes — for in it the righteousness of God is revealed, given to sinners by grace alone through faith alone in Christ alone, so that the justified live uncondemned, inseparable from God's love, and transformed into a people who glorify Him forever.

In the "Jesus in Every Book of the Bible" walkthrough, this is Romans' testimony: Jesus in Romans is the righteousness of God revealed — the propitiation for our sins, the last Adam, the Deliverer from Zion, in whom the ungodly are justified by faith and from whose love nothing in all creation can separate us. Mark showed us the Servant's hands; Romans shows us what those pierced hands accomplished in the courtroom of heaven and the ledger of God: our trespasses counted to Him, His righteousness counted to us, peace where wrath stood, and a life that begins at the verdict and runs all the way to glory. The walkthrough of Scripture has many mountains, and Romans is among the highest — the place where the gospel is not only told but explained, defended, and sung.

If you have walked through this commentary chapter by chapter, you have heard the five watchwords of the Reformers sound on every page, because they are Romans' watchwords first: Scripture alone, as Paul builds every argument on "as it is written"; grace alone, as justification comes "by his grace as a gift" and the remnant is "chosen by grace"; faith alone, as the ungodly are justified by believing and Abraham is counted righteous for his faith; Christ alone, as the propitiation, the one act of righteousness, and the end of the law; and glory to God alone, as the letter breaks into doxology — "To him be glory forever. Amen." Hold those five, keep the law and the gospel distinct, and you will read not only Romans but the whole Bible the way it asks to be read.

The last word belongs to the letter's own theme, the verse that opened Luther's paradise and can open yours: The righteous shall live by faith. Not by record, not by ancestry, not by effort, not by feeling — by faith, in the Christ who was delivered up for our trespasses and raised for our justification. Live there. That is the whole letter, and it is enough.

A Closing Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, our righteousness and our peace,

We thank You for this letter, and for the apostle who wrote it, and for the God who breathed it. We thank You that the gospel is the power of God for salvation — that You were delivered up for our trespasses and raised for our justification, that in You there is now no condemnation, and that nothing in all creation can separate us from the love of God in You.

We confess that we are the people of these chapters: suppressors of truth, judgers of others, stopped mouths and wretched hearts, strong when we should bear and weak when we should stand. Thank You that You justify the ungodly who believe. Teach us to count ourselves dead to sin and alive to God, to put to death by the Spirit what still wars in us, to cry Abba with confidence, and to present our bodies as living sacrifices — not to earn the mercies of God, but because they are already ours in You.

Make us a Romans 16 people: fellow workers, risk-takers, welcomers, named and known in Your family. And keep us in the obedience of faith until the day the God of peace crushes Satan under our feet.

To the only wise God be glory forevermore, through You.

Christ Proclaimed. Jesus is Lord.

Amen.
