Baptism — A History

The Sign of the Covenant Through the Centuries

  • Teachings
  • Church History
  • Baptism

The Hope Connection — Lewis Fuller

1. Christ's Own Ordinance

Baptism was not man's idea. It was not invented by a church council, and it did not grow up slowly out of human custom. It was commanded by the risen Lord Jesus Christ Himself, in some of the last words He spoke on this earth before He ascended into heaven. Standing on a mountain in Galilee, with all authority in heaven and on earth given to Him, Jesus said to His disciples:

"Go therefore and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, teaching them to observe all that I have commanded you. And behold, I am with you always, to the end of the age." — Matthew 28:19–20 (ESV)

Notice what that means. Baptism belongs to Jesus. It is His ordinance, given to His church, to be carried out in the name of the Triune God — Father, Son, and Holy Spirit — until the end of the age. It is one of only two ordinances Christ gave His church, the other being the Lord's Supper. And notice too where it stands in the sentence: making disciples and baptizing them go together. From the very beginning, coming to Christ and being baptized have belonged together like a door belongs to a house.

And yet baptism did not appear out of nowhere on that Galilean mountain. Long before Jesus spoke those words, God had been preparing His people to understand what water, washing, and covenant signs mean. The story of baptism reaches back into the Old Testament, runs through the Jordan River with a camel-haired preacher, passes through the baptism of Jesus Himself, explodes outward at Pentecost, and then runs through two thousand years of church history — praised, practiced, argued over, and even died for. It is a story worth knowing, because baptism is not a small thing. Men and women have been imprisoned and killed over the questions this history raises. And every believer who has ever stepped into the water, or had the water poured over them, stands in this story.

So let us walk through it together, from the beginning.

2. Roots in the Old Testament: Washings, Water, and the Covenant Sign

The Old Testament never uses the word "baptism," but it is full of water. Again and again, God taught His people that sin makes a person unclean, and that cleansing is something God Himself must provide.

Under the law of Moses, washing with water ran through the whole life of Israel. The priests had to wash their hands and feet at the bronze basin before they could serve at the altar, on pain of death (Exodus 30:17–21). Lepers who were healed were washed and sprinkled. People who touched a dead body were cleansed with water mixed with the ashes of the red heifer (Numbers 19). The whole book of Leviticus is threaded through with washings — "he shall bathe his flesh in water" comes again and again. None of these washings could finally take away sin, and the book of Hebrews says so plainly. But they were sermons in water, preaching a truth Israel needed to learn deep in their bones: you cannot come near a holy God while you are unclean, and you cannot make yourself clean by your own power.

The prophets picked up the picture and pointed it forward. God promised through Ezekiel:

"I will sprinkle clean water on you, and you shall be clean from all your uncleannesses, and from all your idols I will cleanse you." — Ezekiel 36:25 (ESV)

Through Zechariah He promised, "On that day there shall be a fountain opened for the house of David and the inhabitants of Jerusalem, to cleanse them from sin and uncleanness" (Zechariah 13:1). And Isaiah called the sinner to the water: "Wash yourselves; make yourselves clean" (Isaiah 1:16). The water of the Old Testament always pointed beyond itself — to a cleansing only God could give, in a day that was still coming.

There are even moments where the water nearly becomes baptism itself. When Naaman the Syrian leper came down to the Jordan at the word of Elisha, the Bible says he dipped himself seven times in the river and was healed (2 Kings 5:14). In the old Greek translation of the Old Testament, the word used there is a form of the very word the New Testament uses for baptize. And the apostle Paul looks back at Israel passing through the Red Sea and says, in so many words, that it was a kind of baptism: "Our fathers were all under the cloud, and all passed through the sea, and all were baptized into Moses in the cloud and in the sea" (1 Corinthians 10:1–2). Peter likewise sees the flood in Noah's day — where eight people were brought safely through water — as a picture pointing forward to baptism (1 Peter 3:20–21).

By the time of Jesus, ritual washing was woven into Jewish life. Households and synagogues used the mikveh, a pool of water built for ceremonial immersion, and stepped pools of this kind have been dug up all around Jerusalem and the land of Israel from that period. A person who had become unclean went down into the water and came up clean. Rabbinic sources also describe Gentile converts to Judaism being immersed as part of their entrance into the covenant people. So when a preacher appeared at the Jordan calling Israel to a baptism of repentance, nobody asked what water had to do with God. They already knew. What shocked them was who he said needed it — a matter we will come to shortly.

But the deepest root of baptism in the Old Testament is not the washings. It is the covenant sign.

When God made His covenant with Abraham, He gave him a sign to mark it — a sign placed on the flesh of every male in Abraham's household, including infants eight days old:

"This is my covenant, which you shall keep, between me and you and your offspring after you: Every male among you shall be circumcised. You shall be circumcised in the flesh of your foreskins, and it shall be a sign of the covenant between me and you." — Genesis 17:10–11 (ESV)

That sign mattered enormously. It said: this child belongs to the covenant people; the promises of God are spoken over this child before the child can speak a word. Paul tells us what the sign meant for Abraham himself: "He received the sign of circumcision as a seal of the righteousness that he had by faith while he was still uncircumcised" (Romans 4:11). A sign and a seal — the sign pointed to the promise, and the seal confirmed it.

Then, in the New Testament, Paul draws the line from that old sign straight to baptism. Writing to the Colossians about what Christ has done, he says:

"In him also you were circumcised with a circumcision made without hands, by putting off the body of the flesh, by the circumcision of Christ, having been buried with him in baptism, in which you were also raised with him through faith in the powerful working of God, who raised him from the dead." — Colossians 2:11–12 (ESV)

Circumcision and baptism stand side by side in that passage — the sign of the old covenant and the sign of the new, both pointing to the same reality: God cutting away sin and raising His people to new life, received by faith in the powerful working of God. Hold on to that connection. It will matter greatly when we reach the great debates of church history, because the question of who should be baptized cannot be answered honestly without asking whether God's covenant with His people has changed in the way it embraces their households.

3. John the Baptist: The Baptism of Repentance

The first man in Scripture to actually baptize — to make baptizing his God-given work — was John, the son of Zechariah and Elizabeth, the last and greatest of the old-covenant prophets. He came preaching in the wilderness of Judea, dressed in camel's hair, eating locusts and wild honey, and his message was a hammer: "Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand."

Mark describes his ministry this way:

"John appeared, baptizing in the wilderness and proclaiming a baptism of repentance for the forgiveness of sins. And all the country of Judea and all Jerusalem were going out to him and were being baptized by him in the river Jordan, confessing their sins." — Mark 1:4–5 (ESV)

Think about how startling this was. Ritual washing was for the unclean, and proselyte immersion was for Gentiles coming into Israel. But John stood in the Jordan and told the children of Abraham themselves — the covenant people, the circumcised — that they needed to be washed. Descent from Abraham was not enough. Being outwardly in the covenant was not enough. The axe was laid to the root of the trees, and God's own people needed to repent and be cleansed as surely as any outsider. Crowds streamed out of Jerusalem to hear him, and they went down into the Jordan confessing their sins.

John was also clear that his baptism was not the end of the story. He pointed beyond himself with both hands: "After me comes he who is mightier than I, the strap of whose sandals I am not worthy to stoop down and untie. I have baptized you with water, but he will baptize you with the Holy Spirit" (Mark 1:7–8). John's baptism was a baptism of preparation. It washed people in repentance and aimed their eyes at the Coming One.

That distinction matters, and the book of Acts proves it. Years after Pentecost, Paul came to Ephesus and found about twelve disciples who had only ever received John's baptism. Paul explained the difference, and Luke records it:

"And Paul said, 'John baptized with the baptism of repentance, telling the people to believe in the one who was to come after him, that is, Jesus.' On hearing this, they were baptized in the name of the Lord Jesus." — Acts 19:4–5 (ESV)

So John's baptism and Christian baptism are close kin, but they are not the same thing. John's baptism looked forward to a Christ who was coming. Christian baptism looks to a Christ who has come — crucified, risen, and reigning — and it is administered in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, with the gift of the Spirit Himself given to the church. John himself would have been the first to tell you: his water prepared the way; Jesus is the way.

4. The Baptism of Jesus

Then came the day that stands at the head of all Christian baptism — the day the sinless Son of God Himself went down into the Jordan.

Matthew tells the story:

"Then Jesus came from Galilee to the Jordan to John, to be baptized by him. John would have prevented him, saying, 'I need to be baptized by you, and do you come to me?' But Jesus answered him, 'Let it be so now, for thus it is fitting for us to fulfill all righteousness.' Then he consented. And when Jesus was baptized, immediately he went up from the water, and behold, the heavens were opened to him, and he saw the Spirit of God descending like a dove and coming to rest on him; and behold, a voice from heaven said, 'This is my beloved Son, with whom I am well pleased.'" — Matthew 3:13–17 (ESV)

Everything about this scene preaches. John objected, and no wonder: his baptism was for sinners confessing their sins, and here stood the one man who had no sins to confess. But Jesus answered with the reason: "It is fitting for us to fulfill all righteousness." Jesus did not need cleansing. He came to stand in the place of those who do. From the very first step of His public ministry, He identified Himself with sinners — He took His place in line with the guilty, in the water of repentance, because He had come to take their place at the cross as well. His baptism was a picture, at the start, of what His death would be at the end: the righteous One counted among the transgressors, for us.

And heaven answered. The heavens were opened. The Holy Spirit descended on Him like a dove and rested on Him. And the Father spoke aloud, declaring over His Son the words that stand behind every Christian baptism since: "This is my beloved Son, with whom I am well pleased." Father, Son, and Holy Spirit — all three persons of the Godhead are present and active at the baptism of Jesus. It is no accident that when Jesus later commanded baptism, He commanded it in that same threefold name. Every baptism since that day echoes the Jordan: the Father adopting, the Son cleansing, the Spirit descending and resting.

There is something else here for the believer who has not yet been baptized, or who wonders whether it matters. Your Lord did not stand on the bank and wave you into the water from a distance. He went in first. The water you enter in baptism is water your Savior entered before you, for you. Obedience to Him in baptism is simply following Him where He has already gone.

5. Baptism in the Book of Acts

When the Holy Spirit came at Pentecost, baptism moved from the Jordan into the life of the church — and it moved fast. Peter preached Christ crucified and risen to the crowds in Jerusalem, and when the hearers were cut to the heart and cried out, "Brothers, what shall we do?", Peter answered with the pattern that would mark the church from that day forward:

"And Peter said to them, 'Repent and be baptized every one of you in the name of Jesus Christ for the forgiveness of your sins, and you will receive the gift of the Holy Spirit.'" — Acts 2:38 (ESV)

And they did. Luke records: "So those who received his word were baptized, and there were added that day about three thousand souls" (Acts 2:41). Three thousand baptisms in a day. From then on, wherever the gospel went in the book of Acts, baptism went with it — not months later, not as an afterthought, but right there, on the spot, as the God-appointed response of faith and the doorway into the visible fellowship of believers.

Walk through the book and watch it happen. The Samaritans who believed Philip's preaching were baptized, men and women both (Acts 8:12). The Ethiopian eunuch, riding home in his chariot reading Isaiah, heard the gospel from Philip, and as soon as they came to water he said, "See, here is water! What prevents me from being baptized?" — and "he commanded the chariot to stop, and they both went down into the water, Philip and the eunuch, and he baptized him" (Acts 8:36, 38). Saul of Tarsus, struck blind on the Damascus road, was told by Ananias, "And now why do you wait? Rise and be baptized and wash away your sins, calling on his name" (Acts 22:16).

Then the gospel broke the Jewish boundary, and baptism broke it too. In the house of Cornelius, the Roman centurion at Caesarea, the Holy Spirit fell on Gentiles who had not yet been baptized at all — and Peter's response was not to wait but to baptize: "'Can anyone withhold water for baptizing these people, who have received the Holy Spirit just as we have?' And he commanded them to be baptized in the name of Jesus Christ" (Acts 10:47–48). The order is instructive. Normally in Acts, faith and baptism come together and the Spirit is given with them; here God reversed the visible order to prove to Jewish believers that the Gentiles truly belonged. Either way, water followed faith without delay. Baptism was not optional equipment for the Christian life. It was the front door.

And then there are the households — and honesty requires us to name them plainly, because they have stood at the center of the church's great baptism debate ever since. When Lydia, a seller of purple goods in Philippi, believed the word Paul preached, Luke writes: "And after she was baptized, and her household as well, she urged us, saying, 'If you have judged me to be faithful to the Lord, come to my house and stay'" (Acts 16:15). Days later in the same city, the Philippian jailer, trembling before Paul and Silas at midnight, believed — "and he was baptized at once, he and all his family" (Acts 16:33). The same pattern appears with Crispus, the synagogue ruler at Corinth, who "believed in the Lord, together with his entire household" and was baptized (Acts 18:8), and Paul mentions baptizing the household of Stephanas (1 Corinthians 1:16).

What do these household baptisms prove? Baptists have long argued, fairly, that the texts say the households believed — the jailer's house rejoiced "that he had believed in God" (Acts 16:34) — and that no infant is ever named in any of them. Reformed believers answer, also from the text, that Scripture consistently speaks of God's covenant dealings running through households, that Peter at Pentecost declared, "For the promise is for you and for your children and for all who are far off, everyone whom the Lord our God calls to himself" (Acts 2:39), and that the pattern of whole houses receiving the covenant sign matches exactly how God dealt with Abraham's house under the old covenant sign. We will weigh this more fully in the section on the great debates. For now, simply notice what nobody in Acts ever did: nobody treated baptism as a small or postponable thing. It belonged to the first hours of the Christian life.

6. What Baptism Means

History tells us what the church has done. Scripture tells us what God is doing in it. And the heart of the matter is union with Christ — being joined to Jesus in His death and His resurrection. Paul's words in Romans 6 are the deepest waters in the whole doctrine of baptism:

"Do you not know that all of us who have been baptized into Christ Jesus were baptized into his death? We were buried therefore with him by baptism into death, in order that, just as Christ was raised from the dead by the glory of the Father, we too might walk in newness of life." — Romans 6:3–4 (ESV)

Baptism is a burial and a resurrection acted out. The old life goes down; the new life comes up. The water says what the gospel says: the person who trusts in Christ has died with Him and risen with Him, and now walks in newness of life. Paul says the same thing with different words in Galatians: "For as many of you as were baptized into Christ have put on Christ" (Galatians 3:27) — baptized believers are clothed with Christ Himself. And again: "For in one Spirit we were all baptized into one body — Jews or Greeks, slaves or free — and all were made to drink of one Spirit" (1 Corinthians 12:13). Baptism joins a person not only to Christ but to His body, the church, across every line the world draws.

Baptism also speaks of washing — the real, inner washing that only God performs. Paul writes that Christ "saved us, not because of works done by us in righteousness, but according to his own mercy, by the washing of regeneration and renewal of the Holy Spirit" (Titus 3:5). Hebrews invites believers to draw near "with our hearts sprinkled clean from an evil conscience and our bodies washed with pure water" (Hebrews 10:22). Peter, reaching back to the flood, writes the sentence that holds the whole truth in careful balance: "Baptism, which corresponds to this, now saves you, not as a removal of dirt from the body but as an appeal to God for a good conscience, through the resurrection of Jesus Christ" (1 Peter 3:21).

Read Peter closely, because here the Reformed faith speaks with precision. Baptism is a sign and a seal of the gospel promise — it pictures the washing, it confirms the promise, it marks the person as Christ's. But the water itself is not the Savior. Peter says so in the very verse where he says baptism "saves": not as a removal of dirt from the body — the power is not in the water or the act — but as an appeal to God for a good conscience, through the resurrection of Jesus Christ. The saving is God's, through Christ's resurrection, received by faith. Baptism without faith is a wet body and a dry soul. And faith without baptism is a disobedient faith that refuses the Lord's own appointed sign — which is why the New Testament knows nothing of a believer who simply declines to be baptized.

This is grace alone and faith alone, preached in water. The Reformed confessions summarize it by calling baptism a sign and seal of the covenant of grace — God's visible word, His promise put in a form you can see and feel and remember on your darkest day. When your feelings accuse you, your baptism answers: God has spoken His promise over you in Christ, and He does not take it back. That is why Paul, arguing with baptized believers who were falling back into sin, does not say "remember your experience." He says, "Do you not know what your baptism means?" (Romans 6:3). Baptism is meant to preach to the believer for the rest of his life.

And it is one baptism, as it is one faith and one Lord: "There is one body and one Spirit — just as you were called to the one hope that belongs to your call — one Lord, one faith, one baptism, one God and Father of all, who is over all and through all and in all" (Ephesians 4:4–6). Whatever else has divided Christians through history — and baptism has divided them more than almost anything — the Scriptures confess one baptism, because there is one Christ into whom believers are baptized.

7. Baptism in the Early Church

What did the church do with baptism after the apostles died? We know a good deal, because the early Christians wrote about it, and what they wrote shows a church that took baptism with utmost seriousness.

The earliest church manual we have outside the New Testament is a little book called the Didache — "The Teaching of the Twelve Apostles" — written in the late first century, within living memory of the apostles themselves. Its chapter on baptism is practical and plain. Baptize in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, in living — that is, running — water if it can be had. If not, use other water, cold if possible, warm if not. And then comes the line that has been quoted in mode debates ever since: if you have neither, "pour water three times on the head, in the name of Father and Son and Holy Spirit." So in the earliest church manual we possess, immersion in running water was the honored norm, but pouring was expressly permitted where water was scarce. The mode served the ordinance; the ordinance never served the mode.

Around the year 200, the North African teacher Tertullian wrote the first surviving book devoted entirely to baptism, called simply On Baptism. It shows a church baptizing at Easter and Pentecost especially, preparing candidates carefully, and wrestling with real pastoral questions. Tertullian himself urged caution about baptizing young children — not because the church around him refused to do it, but because he feared people treating the ordinance lightly; he advised waiting until children could know Christ for themselves. His caution is worth noting honestly: it tells us that by around 200 the baptism of children was common enough that a teacher felt the need to push back on haste, even though Tertullian's own counsel did not carry the day in the wider church.

Not long after, the Roman church order known as the Apostolic Tradition, associated with Hippolytus (early third century), describes the practice in detail: candidates prepared over a period of instruction — sometimes as long as three years — called catechumens; a final examination of their lives; baptism at dawn after a night of prayer, in water deep enough for the purpose; and infants brought by their parents, with the parents answering on the child's behalf where the child could not yet speak. In Carthage, around the year 253, Bishop Cyprian and a council of bishops ruled that infants need not even wait until the eighth day, as with circumcision, but could be baptized within days of birth — arguing from the grace of God that no one should be hindered from the covenant sign. Augustine, the great teacher of the church in the following centuries, defended the baptism of infants as the church's ancient and apostolic practice, grounding it in original sin and the covenant: the children of believers belong to the covenant people and receive its sign, as they always had since Abraham.

The buildings tell the same story. Christians began building baptisteries — separate rooms or buildings housing a baptismal pool — and one of the earliest surviving examples, in a house church at Dura-Europos on the Euphrates (from about the 240s), still has its font. The great baptisteries of the fourth and fifth centuries, like the one at the Lateran in Rome, had pools a person could step down into, with steps on either side — down into the water, and up out of it again, burial and resurrection in stone and water.

But the early church also shows us warnings. In the centuries after Constantine made Christianity legal (and himself was baptized only on his deathbed in 337), many delayed baptism as long as possible, reasoning that since baptism washed away sins, a late baptism was a safer one. Preachers like John Chrysostom rebuked the practice sharply. The church's instinct had been right at the start: baptism belongs at the beginning of the Christian life, not at its end. Delay turned a gift into a calculation, and the best teachers of the age said so.

One more thing the early centuries settle: nobody in the ancient church — nobody — treated baptism as unimportant, optional, or a mere human ceremony. They prepared for it for years. They built buildings for it. They sang over it through the night at Easter. Whatever the church has argued about since, it has never argued about whether baptism matters. It has only argued about what it means, who should receive it, and how the water should be applied. Those arguments now take us to the hardest and most honest part of this history.

8. The Great Debates: Who, and How

Two questions have divided sincere, Bible-loving Christians over baptism: who should be baptized — the children of believers, or only those who profess faith themselves — and how should the water be applied? Let us walk through them fairly, stating each side in words its own defenders would recognize, and then say plainly where the Reformed faith stands, and why.

The Reformed and covenant view. The historic Reformed position — held also by Lutherans, Anglicans, Methodists, and the Roman Catholic and Orthodox churches, though with different understandings of what baptism does — is that the infants of believers should be baptized. The argument is not tradition alone. It is an argument from the covenant. God made His covenant with Abraham and said it was with him and his offspring (Genesis 17:7), and He commanded the covenant sign to be given to infants eight days old. The New Testament never repeals the place of believers' children in the covenant; instead, Peter opens the church's first sermon by declaring, "the promise is for you and for your children" (Acts 2:39). Paul connects circumcision and baptism directly (Colossians 2:11–12) and calls the children of even one believing parent "holy" — set apart to God (1 Corinthians 7:14). Whole households were baptized in Acts. So Reformed believers conclude: the sign of the covenant now, baptism, belongs where the sign of the covenant always belonged — to believers and their children. The child is baptized not because anyone presumes the child is regenerated, and not because the water saves, but because God's promise runs to households, and the sign of that promise is placed on the child in hope and in obedience, to be claimed by the child's own faith as he grows. This is the position this ministry confesses, and we hold it gladly.

The Baptist view, stated fairly. Our Baptist brothers and sisters answer with arguments any honest reader must weigh. They point out that every baptism described in detail in the New Testament follows a personal profession of faith: repent and be baptized; those who received the word were baptized; the eunuch believed and was baptized. No text, they say, explicitly commands or describes the baptism of an infant, and the household texts speak of households believing. They hold that the new covenant is made with those who all know the Lord (Jeremiah 31:31–34), that baptism is the believer's own public confession of faith in Christ, and that giving the sign to those who cannot yet confess faith confuses the sign and risks false assurance. John Smyth and the first English Baptists in Amsterdam (1609) took this stand, and generations of Baptists have suffered for it rather than surrender it. We disagree with their conclusion, but we do not doubt their sincerity, their love for Scripture, or their place in the family of God — and we state their view here as they would state it themselves.

The Anabaptists. The debate turned sorrowful in the 1520s. In Zurich, Switzerland, a circle of young men around Conrad Grebel had studied under the reformer Ulrich Zwingli and concluded that infant baptism had no warrant in Scripture. On January 21, 1525, in the home of Felix Manz, Grebel baptized George Blaurock, and Blaurock then baptized the others — the first "re-baptisms," from which their enemies named them Anabaptists, "re-baptizers." They called themselves simply Brethren. Their stand cost them everything. Zurich's council made re-baptism a capital crime, and on January 5, 1527, Felix Manz was drowned in the Limmat River — executed by water for baptizing with water, a cruelty his persecutors called his "third baptism." Catholics and Protestants alike persecuted them; thousands died. The Swiss Brethren's Schleitheim Confession (1527) set out their faith with dignity. Out of this suffering movement, through leaders like Menno Simons, came the Mennonites and the Amish. Every Christian today who enjoys freedom of conscience before the state owes something to these people's witness, whatever we conclude about their doctrine. Their blood is a permanent rebuke to every church that has answered an argument with a sword.

The question of mode. How much water, and how applied? The Greek word baptizō means, at its root, to dip or immerse — it is the ordinary word for plunging something into water, and the baptism scenes in the Gospels and Acts, with people going down into rivers, fit immersion naturally. Fairness requires adding that the word's range in Scripture is a little wider than the riverbank: it is used for ceremonial washings of hands, cups, and vessels (Mark 7:4; Luke 11:38), and the Old Testament promises of cleansing speak of sprinkling (Ezekiel 36:25) — a word Peter and Hebrews take up for the blood of Christ and the conscience (1 Peter 1:2; Hebrews 10:22). The Didache, the church's earliest manual, allowed pouring where water was scarce. The Reformed churches have therefore confessed — in the words of the Westminster Confession — that "dipping of the person into the water is not necessary; but baptism is rightly administered by pouring, or sprinkling water upon the person." Baptists and many others insist on immersion alone as the only obedient mode, and they honor the picture of burial and resurrection by it. On this too, we state our conviction plainly — the amount of water is not the essence of the ordinance; the Triune name, the promise, and the Christ it seals are — while honoring the conscience of brothers who see it otherwise. What no one may do is despise the ordinance itself, or treat Christ's command as a small thing over which to break fellowship lightly. The debates are real, and they matter. But the one baptism of Ephesians 4 is bigger than all of them.

9. Baptism Today: Why It Still Matters

So where does this long history leave the ordinary believer today? It leaves us where the apostles left the first believers: with a command from a risen Lord, a promise sealed in water, and no warrant to treat either lightly.

Baptism still matters, first, because it is obedience. Jesus commanded it, and He commanded it for disciples — "make disciples of all nations, baptizing them." A person may be truly saved and yet, through teaching or neglect or fear, remain unbaptized; we do not measure another man's salvation by his baptism, for the thief on the cross entered paradise without it, held by a promise from the Lord's own lips. But that dying thief is an exception of mercy, not a pattern for the living. For the believer with water available and a church at hand, baptism is not an optional extra to the Christian life. It is the first step of public obedience in it, and putting it off year after year is not humility. It is hesitation at the very door Christ opened.

It matters, second, because it is a public confession. Baptism is the believer standing before God, the church, and the world, and saying with his whole body what his heart believes: Jesus Christ is Lord; I am His; my old life is buried; my new life is His. In ages of persecution that confession cost people their property, their families, and their lives — ask Felix Manz. In comfortable ages the cost is lower, but the confession is the same, and it is meant to cost us our privacy about Jesus. A faith that will not be seen will not long be strong.

It matters, third, because it is the door into the visible church. Baptism marks a person out as belonging to Christ's people — it is the badge of the covenant community, the sign that this man, this woman, this child of believers is under the care, teaching, and discipline of the church of Jesus Christ. The three thousand at Pentecost were not baptized into isolation; they were "added" (Acts 2:41), and they devoted themselves to the apostles' teaching and the fellowship. Baptism was never meant to be a private transaction between a soul and God. It joins a person to a people.

And it matters, fourth, because it preaches to the baptized for the rest of their lives. Martin Luther, when the devil accused him and his conscience troubled him, is said to have answered, "I am baptized." Not "I was strong," not "I have done well" — I am baptized. God's promise, sealed in water, in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, stands when feelings fail. That is the daily use of baptism, and it is available to every believer who will make it: when you are tempted, when you are accused, when you are afraid — you are baptized into Christ. You have put on Christ. Walk, then, in newness of life.

So this history ends, as it should, with an invitation. If you are reading these words and you trust in Jesus Christ, but you have never been baptized — friend, the water is not the obstacle. Christ has commanded it, the church stands ready to rejoice over you, and the promise is for you. Do not wait until you feel worthy; baptism is not a trophy for the worthy. It is a washing for the unclean, a door for the entering, a seal for those who believe the promise. Why do you wait? Rise and be baptized, calling on His name.

10. A Closing Word and Prayer

From the washings of Leviticus to a river in Judea, from a jailer's house at midnight to a font in Dura-Europos, from the martyrs of Zurich to the little church down your own road — the water has carried one message the whole way: God washes sinners. Not the strong, not the worthy, not the ones who cleaned themselves up first. Sinners. The water never had the power; the power was always in the Christ to whom the water points, in the blood that really cleanses, in the Spirit who really regenerates, in the Father who speaks over His baptized children the same words He spoke at the Jordan: beloved, and well pleased — because they are in His Son.

I know something about needing to be washed clean. I spent more than thirty years in addiction, and nothing I tried could make me clean — not rehabs, not programs, not my own promises. But God met me where I was, and He washed me in a way no water on earth could do, by the blood of Jesus Christ. Baptism is dear to me because it is the picture of the very thing that saved me: a man goes down dirty, in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, and comes up declaring that Another has made him clean. If that is your story, rejoice in your baptism. If it is not yet your story, it can be today — the fountain Zechariah promised stands open still.

Let us pray.

Father, we thank You for the water and the Word. Thank You for a Savior who went down into the Jordan before us and came up with heaven open above Him. Thank You for every believer across two thousand years who confessed Christ in baptism — the famous and the forgotten, the martyrs and the children, the jailers at midnight and the merchants at noon. Seal Your promise to the hearts of everyone who reads these words. For the baptized, teach us to live out of our baptism daily — dead to sin, alive to You, walking in newness of life. For the one who believes and has not yet obeyed, give courage and a willing heart, and bring them to the water. And keep Your church faithful to the one Lord, the one faith, and the one baptism, until the day we stand clean and complete before Your throne. In the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit. Amen.

Christ Proclaimed. Jesus is Lord.
