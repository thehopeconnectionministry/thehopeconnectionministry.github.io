The Religions of the World — And How Christianity Differs

An Honest Look at What Others Believe, and Why the Gospel Stands Alone

  • Teachings
  • World Religions
  • Apologetics

The Hope Connection — Lewis Fuller

Before We Begin: Honesty and Love

There are more than four billion people on this earth who follow a religion other than the Christian faith. They are our neighbors, our co-workers, our family members, and our friends. They are men and women made in the image of God, and most of them hold their beliefs sincerely, the way they were taught by people they love and trust.

So this history begins with two commitments.

The first is honesty. Every religion in these pages will be described the way its own followers would describe it — no caricatures, no mockery, no straw men knocked down for sport. A man does not honor the truth by misrepresenting his neighbor. If we are going to say where we differ, we will differ with the real thing, not with a cartoon of it.

The second is conviction. Honesty about others does not require silence about ourselves. The Christian faith makes claims — about God, about Jesus, about how a sinner is made right — that cannot all be true at the same time as the claims of other religions. Where they conflict, something has to give. In these pages, the differences will be stated plainly, from the settled convictions of the Reformed faith, without heat and without apology.

For each religion, we will ask the same four questions: Who is God? Who is Jesus? How is a person made right? And what book or practice guides the way? By the end, you will see why the gospel of Jesus Christ does not merely differ from the religions of the world in degree. It differs in kind.

Judaism: The Root We Were Grafted Into

Origin and founder. Judaism is the oldest faith in this history, and it is the soil Christianity grew out of. It begins with Abraham, called by God out of Ur of the Chaldees about two thousand years before Christ, and given a promise: a land, a people, and a blessing for all the families of the earth. It takes its shape under Moses, who led Israel out of slavery in Egypt and received the Law — the Torah — on Mount Sinai. The Jewish people are not the followers of a philosophy. They are the descendants of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, bound to God by covenant.

What it teaches about God. Judaism confesses one God — the LORD, who revealed Himself to Moses, who created heaven and earth, who is holy, just, and merciful. On this ground, Christians stand shoulder to shoulder with the Jewish confession: the Shema of Deuteronomy 6:4, "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is one," is a sentence every Christian can say with his whole heart. Where Judaism and Christianity part is not over the existence or the oneness of God. It is over Jesus.

What it teaches about Jesus. Traditional Judaism regards Jesus of Nazareth as a Jewish teacher of the first century — some Jewish scholars speak of Him respectfully as a rabbi of His time — but it does not confess Him as the Messiah, and it does not confess Him as the Son of God. The Messiah, in Jewish expectation, is still to come: a descendant of David who will restore Israel, rebuild the Temple, and bring in an age of peace. Because Jesus died under a curse on a Roman cross and the age of peace did not follow, the Jewish verdict has been that whatever else He was, He was not the promised One.

How a person is made right. In Judaism, a person stands in right relationship with God by living within the covenant: repentance (teshuvah), prayer, obedience to the commandments of the Torah, acts of charity and mercy. Since the destruction of the Temple in the year 70, there have been no sacrifices; repentance and prayer have taken the place the altar once held.

Its book and practice. The Tanakh — the Law, the Prophets, and the Writings — is the same Scripture Christians call the Old Testament. Alongside it stands the Talmud, the great body of rabbinic teaching and commentary. Worship centers on the synagogue and the home: the Sabbath kept from Friday sundown, the feasts of Passover, Yom Kippur, and the rest, circumcision, and the dietary laws.

Where we differ, stated with love. The Christian claim is that Jesus is the Messiah Israel has waited for — that the promises made to Abraham and the sufferings described by Isaiah found their answer in Him:

"But he was pierced for our transgressions; he was crushed for our iniquities; upon him was the chastisement that brought us peace, and with his wounds we are healed." — Isaiah 53:5 (ESV)

We say this with love, and with a debt we can never repay. The apostle Paul pictured Israel as an olive tree and Gentile believers as wild branches grafted in, and he warned the grafted branches never to boast over the root:

"Do not be arrogant toward the branches. If you are, remember it is not you who support the root, but the root that supports you." — Romans 11:18 (ESV)

Our Scriptures, our Savior, and our hope of a Messiah all came to us through the Jewish people. The difference between us is real, and it is the most important difference in this whole history — but it is a family difference, and Christians should carry it with gratitude and tears, never with contempt.

Islam: The Prophet and the Scales

Origin and founder. Islam begins in the seventh century in Arabia with Muhammad (about 570–632), a merchant of Mecca who, beginning in the year 610, reported receiving revelations from the angel Gabriel. Those revelations, recited and later written down, became the Qur'an. Muhammad united the tribes of Arabia under his message, and within a century of his death, Islam had spread from Spain to Persia. Today it is the second largest religion in the world, with roughly two billion followers, called Muslims.

What it teaches about God. Islam confesses one God — Allah, the Arabic word for God — who is one in an absolute, undivided sense. The central confession, the Shahada, declares: "There is no god but God, and Muhammad is the Messenger of God." Islam stresses God's greatness, His mercy, and His absolute sovereignty. It firmly rejects the Trinity and rejects the idea that God could have a son: God does not beget, and Jesus cannot be God incarnate. This is not a small difference. The God of Islam is one Person; the God of the Bible is one God in three Persons — Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. Christians and Muslims use some of the same words, but at the center, we are not describing the same God in the same way.

What it teaches about Jesus. Islam honors Jesus — Isa — more highly than many Christians realize. Muslims believe He was born of the virgin Mary, that He was a great prophet, that He performed miracles, and that He will return at the end of the age. But Islam denies the two things the New Testament proclaims loudest: that Jesus is the Son of God, and that He died on the cross. The Qur'an teaches that He was not crucified — that it only appeared so — and therefore there is no atoning death and no resurrection in Islam's account.

How a person is made right. A Muslim is made right by submission — "Islam" itself means submission — expressed in the Five Pillars: confessing the Shahada, praying five times daily, giving alms, fasting during Ramadan, and making the pilgrimage to Mecca once in a lifetime if able. On the Day of Judgment, deeds are weighed in the scales. Good deeds that outweigh the bad give hope of Paradise, but there is no certainty in this life: in the end, a Muslim's fate rests on Allah's inscrutable will. Assurance of salvation, as Christians understand it, is not offered.

Its book and practice. The Qur'an is regarded as the literal, final, and perfect word of God, superseding the earlier Scriptures, which Muslims believe were corrupted over time. Alongside it stands the Hadith, the record of Muhammad's words and example. Worship centers on the mosque, Friday prayer, and the discipline of daily prayer.

Where we differ. The difference can be put in one sentence: Islam gives a man a set of scales; the gospel gives him a Savior. The Christian faith proclaims that Jesus truly died and truly rose, and that a sinner is justified not by deeds weighed but by grace received:

"And there is salvation in no one else, for there is no other name under heaven given among men by which we must be saved." — Acts 4:12 (ESV)

Roman Catholicism: Much Shared, Much at Stake

Origin. The Roman Catholic Church traces itself to the apostles and to Peter, whom it regards as the first pope. For the first thousand years of church history, East and West were one church; after the split with the East in 1054, Rome remained the center of Western Christianity until the Reformation of the sixteenth century divided the West itself. Today the Roman Catholic Church is the largest single body of professing Christians in the world, with well over a billion members.

The common ground, named honestly. There is real common ground, and it should be named without grudging. Roman Catholics confess the Trinity, the deity of Christ, the virgin birth, the crucifixion, the bodily resurrection, the return of Christ, and the great ecumenical creeds — the Apostles' Creed and the Nicene Creed — word for word with us. A Catholic neighbor who trusts in the finished work of Christ is not our enemy, and many Catholics love the Lord Jesus sincerely. The Reformation was not a quarrel with everything Rome taught. It was a quarrel over specific doctrines — and on those doctrines, everything depends.

What it teaches about God and Jesus. Catholic teaching confesses one God, Triune, and Jesus Christ as true God and true man. Here the creeds unite us. The differences arise over how God deals with sinners and where the church's authority lies.

How a person is made right. Here is the heart of the divide. Catholic teaching holds that justification is a process: grace is infused into the soul through the sacraments, beginning with baptism, and the believer cooperates with that grace through faith and works, growing in righteousness across a lifetime. Venial sins can be dealt with by penance; the remaining debt after death is purged in purgatory. Against this, the Reformers recovered the Bible's teaching that justification is God's once-for-all declaration over the sinner who believes — received by faith alone, apart from works:

"For by grace you have been saved through faith. And this is not your own doing; it is the gift of God, not a result of works, so that no one may boast." — Ephesians 2:8–9 (ESV)

Its book and practice. The Catholic Bible contains the same Old and New Testaments, plus the deuterocanonical books (called the Apocrypha in Protestant Bibles), and Scripture is read alongside Sacred Tradition, with the Magisterium — the teaching office of the church — as final interpreter. The Mass stands at the center of Catholic worship, taught as the re-presentation of Christ's sacrifice. Mary and the saints are honored and asked for intercession; purgatory awaits most believers after death.

Where we differ, stated plainly and without heat. Four differences, honestly held:

  • Authority. We hold that Scripture alone is the final authority for faith and life; Rome holds Scripture and Tradition together, interpreted by the church.
  • Justification. We hold that a sinner is justified by faith alone, Christ's righteousness counted to him; Rome holds that justification includes the believer's own Spirit-worked righteousness and can be increased and lost.
  • The Mass and the mediation of Christ. We hold that Christ's one offering finished the work forever — "For by a single offering he has perfected for all time those who are being sanctified" (Hebrews 10:14, ESV) — and that "there is one God, and there is one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus" (1 Timothy 2:5, ESV). Prayers to Mary and the saints, however sincerely offered, route around the one Mediator God has given.
  • Purgatory. We find no purgatory in Scripture, and we hold that the blood of Christ cleanses the believer fully, so that death ushers him directly into the presence of the Lord.

These are not small things, and pretending otherwise would help no one. But they can be stated the way a man states his convictions to a neighbor he respects — plainly, and over a fence, not a battlefield.

Eastern Orthodoxy: The Church of the Ancient East

Origin. The Eastern Orthodox Church shares the first thousand years of Christian history with Rome — the same apostles, the same councils, the same creeds. The parting came in the year 1054, in what is called the Great Schism, when Rome and Constantinople broke communion over the authority of the pope and over a change Rome had made to the Nicene Creed (adding that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Son as well as the Father). The Orthodox Church today is centered in the historic patriarchates of the East — Constantinople, Alexandria, Antioch, Jerusalem, and Moscow — and numbers over two hundred million adherents.

What it teaches about God and Jesus. Orthodoxy confesses the Trinity and the full deity and humanity of Christ exactly as the ancient creeds state them. Its theology is famously guarded and worshipful: God is ultimately beyond human comprehension, known truly but never exhaustively. Its worship is liturgical, ancient, and beautiful — the Divine Liturgy, chant, incense, and walls of icons, the painted images of Christ and the saints that fill Orthodox churches and are venerated as "windows to heaven."

How a person is made right. The Orthodox answer is theosis — often translated "deification" — drawn from the apostle Peter's phrase that believers become "partakers of the divine nature" (2 Peter 1:4). Salvation is a lifelong process of being healed and transformed by God's grace, worked out through the sacraments (called "mysteries"), prayer, fasting, and the disciplines of the church, supremely the rule of prayer known as the Jesus Prayer: "Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me, a sinner." Orthodoxy does not usually speak in the legal terms of the Western church — guilt and pardon — but in the language of sickness and healing: man is sick with death, and the church is the hospital.

Its book and practice. Orthodoxy reads the same Bible, within Holy Tradition — the whole life of the church through the centuries, its councils, liturgies, and fathers — which it regards as the one stream in which Scripture lives. The priesthood, the monastic life of Mount Athos, and the calendar of fasts and feasts shape Orthodox practice.

Where we differ. We share the creeds, and we can learn from Orthodoxy's reverence and its memory of the ancient church. But we differ where the Reformation always differs: Scripture must stand as the final judge over tradition, not one voice within it; and a sinner is justified — declared righteous — by faith alone in Christ alone, not gradually made righteous by a process he must complete. The Orthodox language of healing is beautiful, and Scripture uses it too. But the Bible also speaks the courtroom language of Romans, and a condemned man needs a verdict before he needs a hospital: "Therefore, since we have been justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ" (Romans 5:1, ESV).

Hinduism: The Wheel of Rebirth

Origin. Hinduism is the oldest of the world's great religions still practiced, with roots reaching back more than three thousand years to the Indus Valley and the ancient peoples of India. It has no single founder and no single creed; it is better understood as a great river fed by many streams — philosophies, gods, rituals, and castes — gathered under one name. Today it has well over a billion followers, most of them in India and Nepal.

What it teaches about God. Hindu thought holds that behind the many gods — Brahma the creator, Vishnu the preserver, Shiva the destroyer, and a multitude of others — stands one ultimate reality, Brahman: an impersonal, all-encompassing spirit of which everything is a part. The human soul (atman) is, at the deepest level, one with Brahman, though most people live unaware of it. The gods are worshiped in temples and homes with offerings, festivals, and devotion (bhakti), and different families and regions honor different gods as supreme.

What it teaches about Jesus. Many Hindus regard Jesus with genuine respect — as a great teacher, a guru, even a divine incarnation (avatar) of God. But that respect comes at a price Christianity cannot accept: Jesus is honored by being absorbed. He becomes one manifestation of the divine among many, a way up the mountain alongside other ways — not the unique Son of God, and not the only Savior.

How a person is made right. In Hinduism, the human problem is not guilt before a holy God but ignorance and karma. Karma is the law of cause and effect: every deed bears fruit, in this life or the next. The soul is reborn again and again — samsara, the wheel of reincarnation — into a station determined by its karma, human or animal, high caste or low. The goal is moksha: release from the wheel, the soul's realization of its oneness with Brahman. The paths toward it include the way of knowledge, the way of devotion, and the way of disciplined action and meditation (yoga in its original religious sense).

Its book and practice. Hinduism's scriptures are vast: the Vedas, the most ancient; the Upanishads, its philosophical crown; and the beloved Bhagavad Gita, the dialogue between the warrior Arjuna and the god Krishna. Practice includes temple worship (puja), pilgrimage to the Ganges, and the great festivals such as Diwali.

Where we differ. The Bible's answer to the wheel is a straight line and a single life:

"And just as it is appointed for man to die once, and after that comes judgment, so Christ, having been offered once to bear the sins of many, will appear a second time, not to deal with sin but to save those who are eagerly waiting for him." — Hebrews 9:27–28 (ESV)

A man does not live a thousand lives to climb out of his karma. He lives one life, dies one death, and faces one judgment — and the whole weight of his sin can be carried, once and for all, by another. Nor is God an impersonal sea into which the soul dissolves. He is a Father who knows His children by name, and the goal is not escape from personhood but everlasting life with Him, body and soul, in a new creation.

Buddhism: The Path Out of Suffering

Origin and founder. Buddhism begins in the sixth or fifth century before Christ with Siddhartha Gautama, a prince of northern India who renounced his palace, his wealth, and his family to seek the answer to suffering. After years of searching, he attained what he called enlightenment while meditating beneath the Bodhi tree, and he spent the remaining forty-five years of his life teaching the path he had found. He is known as the Buddha — "the Awakened One." Today Buddhism has several hundred million followers, concentrated in Asia and growing in the West.

What it teaches about God. Buddhism, in its original form, has no creator God. The Buddha did not deny that gods might exist; he taught that they, too, are caught in the wheel of rebirth and death, and that they cannot save anyone. There is no personal God to pray to for rescue, no Creator to stand before. The universe runs on karma and cause and effect, beginningless and impersonal.

What it teaches about Jesus. Buddhists generally regard Jesus as a wise and compassionate teacher, and some as an enlightened being. But there is no place in Buddhism for a Savior who dies for another's sins, because there is no God against whom sin is committed and no self, in the end, to be saved — one of Buddhism's most distinctive teachings is anatta, "no-self": what we call the soul is a bundle of changing parts, with no permanent essence beneath.

How a person is made right. The Buddha's diagnosis is set out in the Four Noble Truths: life is marked by suffering (dukkha); suffering arises from craving and attachment; suffering can end when craving ends; and the way to that end is the Noble Eightfold Path — right understanding, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right concentration. Follow the path, through meditation and moral discipline, across as many lifetimes as it takes, and a person reaches nirvana: the blowing out of desire, release from rebirth, awakening to the truth of things. No one can walk this path for you. The Buddha's reported last words were an instruction to work out your own deliverance with diligence.

Its book and practice. Buddhist scriptures are extensive — the Pali Canon preserves the earliest teachings — and practice centers on meditation, chanting, devotion at temples and shrines, and the monastic community (the sangha), whose monks and nuns embody the path.

Where we differ. Buddhism sees the problem of suffering with clear eyes — clearer, honestly, than much of the modern West — but its cure is the extinguishing of desire, achieved by the sufferer's own effort over countless lives. The gospel names a deeper problem beneath suffering: sin, and a holy God who must be reckoned with. And it offers a cure no man achieves: "But God shows his love for us in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us" (Romans 5:8, ESV). Christianity does not ask a drowning man to meditate his way out of the water. It throws him a Rescuer — and it does not quench desire but redirects it, promising a world where the deepest longings of the heart are not extinguished but satisfied forever in God.

Mormonism: Another Testament, Another Jesus

Origin and founder. The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints was founded by Joseph Smith in 1830 in upstate New York. Smith reported that as a boy of fourteen, in 1820, God the Father and Jesus Christ appeared to him and told him all existing churches were in error; that an angel named Moroni later directed him to golden plates buried in a hill; and that he translated those plates into the Book of Mormon, published in 1830 as "another testament of Jesus Christ." From small beginnings, the movement moved west under persecution and, after Smith was killed by a mob in 1844, followed Brigham Young to Utah. Today it claims more than seventeen million members worldwide.

What it teaches about God. Here the difference from historic Christianity is fundamental, and it should be stated exactly as Latter-day Saints themselves teach it: God the Father is an exalted man with a body of flesh and bones, who was not always God but progressed to godhood; Jesus is His literal spirit-child and the spirit-brother of Lucifer; and faithful Mormons may themselves progress to become gods of their own worlds. The Trinity, as the creeds confess it — one God in three Persons, eternal and uncreated — is rejected. This is not the God of Abraham, of the creeds, or of the Reformation. By the standard of historic Christianity, Mormonism is not another denomination. It is another religion wearing Christian words.

What it teaches about Jesus. Jesus is honored as Savior and as the Son of God in a literal, physical sense — the offspring of God the Father and Mary. His atonement in Gethsemane and on the cross makes resurrection universal and opens the door to exaltation, but it does not by itself save a man; it makes salvation possible for those who then qualify. The Jesus of Mormonism is a created, subordinate being — not God of very God, eternally one with the Father.

How a person is made right. A Latter-day Saint is made right through faith, repentance, baptism by immersion at the hands of Latter-day Saint priesthood authority, the gift of the Holy Ghost, and enduring obedience to the laws and ordinances of the Latter-day Saint gospel for life — temple ordinances, including baptism for the dead and eternal marriage, among them. The Book of Mormon itself states the balance its followers live by: "It is by grace that we are saved, after all we can do" (2 Nephi 25:23). Those last five words are the whole difference. The Bible's order is the reverse: grace does everything, and our works follow as fruit, never as the price.

Its book and practice. Four books stand as scripture: the Bible (accepted "as far as it is translated correctly"), the Book of Mormon, the Doctrine and Covenants, and the Pearl of Great Price. Practice includes Sunday worship in wards (congregations), two-year missions undertaken by young members, temple worship closed to outsiders, and a strong emphasis on family.

Where we differ. Paul foresaw exactly this situation and wrote the church's answer in advance:

"But even if we or an angel from heaven should preach to you a gospel contrary to the one we preached to you, let him be accursed." — Galatians 1:8 (ESV)

A new revelation, delivered by an angel, that changes who God is and who Jesus is, is not the recovery of the gospel. It is the replacement of it. We say this while gladly acknowledging the sincerity, the family devotion, and the moral seriousness of our Latter-day Saint neighbors — and while insisting that the Jesus who is the spirit-brother of Satan and the Jesus who is the eternal Son of God cannot both be the real Jesus.

Jehovah's Witnesses: A Created Christ and a Careful Count

Origin and founder. The Jehovah's Witnesses began in the 1870s in Pennsylvania with Charles Taze Russell, a young Bible student who rejected the doctrines of the Trinity, the immortality of the soul, and eternal punishment, and who taught that Christ had returned invisibly in 1874 (later revised to 1914). Russell founded the Watch Tower Society, which became the movement's governing organization, headquartered today in Warwick, New York. Jehovah's Witnesses number over eight million active members worldwide, known everywhere for their door-to-door preaching.

What it teaches about God. Jehovah's Witnesses confess one God, Jehovah, who is a single Person — the Father alone. The Holy Spirit is not a Person but God's active force. God is sovereign and His name is to be honored; on the importance of the divine name, they have a point the wider church has sometimes neglected. But the Trinity is rejected outright as a pagan corruption, and that rejection drives everything else in their system.

What it teaches about Jesus. Jesus, in Witness teaching, is God's first creation — the archangel Michael in his pre-human existence — created before all other things and used by God as the agent of the rest of creation. He is "a god," in the wording of their own translation, the New World Translation (John 1:1), but he is not Almighty God. He died not on a cross but on a torture stake, and he was raised not bodily but as a spirit. Against this stands the apostle Thomas, falling before the risen Jesus with the confession the Witness system cannot allow:

"Thomas answered him, 'My Lord and my God!'" — John 20:28 (ESV)

And John 1:1, in every translation that follows the Greek honestly, reads: "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God" (ESV).

How a person is made right. Salvation, for a Witness, is gained through faith in Jesus' ransom sacrifice joined to obedience, association with God's organization, and active participation in the preaching work. Humanity is divided into two classes: an "anointed" class of exactly 144,000 who will reign in heaven — the number taken literally from Revelation 7 and 14 — and the "other sheep," the great crowd who hope to live forever on a paradise earth. There is no eternal punishment: the wicked are annihilated, ceasing to exist. There is also no assurance in the Christian sense; one's standing depends on continued faithfulness to the end, under the direction of the organization.

Its book and practice. The New World Translation of the Holy Scriptures is the Witnesses' own Bible, produced by their organization, and its renderings depart from all standard translations at precisely the verses that teach Christ's deity. Practice centers on meetings at the Kingdom Hall, the door-to-door ministry, refusal of blood transfusions, and abstention from birthdays, holidays, and political involvement.

Where we differ. The Witness at the door is often sincere, disciplined, and willing to suffer for his beliefs — qualities the comfortable church could learn from. But he is preaching a Jesus who is a creature. And a created savior cannot save, because a creature owes God his own obedience and has nothing left over to pay another man's debt. Only God Himself, come in the flesh, could bear the sins of the world. "For in him the whole fullness of deity dwells bodily" (Colossians 2:9, ESV). That is the Jesus of the Bible, and there is no other.

The Difference: Every Other Way Says "Do" — the Gospel Says "Done"

Lay the religions of the world side by side, and one pattern runs through nearly all of them like a thread through cloth.

Islam says: submit, pray, fast, give, journey — and hope the scales tip in your favor. Hinduism says: work off your karma across a thousand lifetimes until you escape the wheel. Buddhism says: walk the path, master your desires, save yourself by diligence — no one can do it for you. Mormonism says: grace will save you after all you can do. The Jehovah's Witnesses say: stay faithful, stay active, stay in the organization, and perhaps you will be counted worthy. Even the systems closest to us divide here: Rome adds the believer's own works and sufferings to the ledger; the East makes salvation a process a man must complete.

Every one of them, in the end, hands the sinner a ladder and wishes him luck on the climb.

The gospel of Jesus Christ hands him no ladder at all. It announces that the climbing has been done — by Another, in his place, completely:

"For our sake he made him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God." — 2 Corinthians 5:21 (ESV)

From the cross, Jesus did not cry "It is started," or "It is possible," or "It is your turn now." He cried:

"It is finished." — John 19:30 (ESV)

That is the difference between Christianity and every other religion on earth. Every founder of every other way lies in a tomb, or in ashes, or in a grave his followers can visit. Buddha died and was cremated. Muhammad lies in Medina. Joseph Smith lies in Illinois, Charles Taze Russell in Pennsylvania. Jesus of Nazareth lies nowhere. His tomb outside Jerusalem stands empty, and two thousand years of enemies have never produced the body. A finished Savior, a free gift, an empty tomb — no other founder has any of the three, and Christianity has all of them because they are all the same fact: God did for sinners what sinners could never do for themselves.

And so Jesus could make the claim no other religious teacher on earth ever dared to make about himself — not that he would show men the way, but that he is the way:

"Jesus said to him, 'I am the way, and the truth, and the life. No one comes to the Father except through me.'" — John 14:6 (ESV)

That sentence is either the truest thing ever spoken or the most arrogant. There is no middle ground on which to stand politely. The Christian faith stands on it without flinching.

Meeting People Where They Are

None of this is written so that any man can feel superior to his neighbor. The Christian who understands grace knows he did not climb to God either — he was found, the way a lost sheep is found, carried home on shoulders not his own. We do not look at the Muslim at his prayers, the Hindu at her temple, the Witness at our door, and see enemies. We see people we might have been, and in many cases people whose sincerity puts our own comfort to shame — people carrying ladders because nobody has ever told them the climbing is done.

That is why The Hope Connection exists: to meet others where they are in the name of Jesus. Wherever a man is standing — at the mosque door or the Kingdom Hall, in a jail cell or a hospital bed, at the bottom of a bottle or the end of his own rope — the message is the same, and it is not "do better." It is "done." Christ has done it. Come and see.

A closing prayer. Lord God, we thank You that You did not leave us to climb. Thank You for the religions of men, which show us how deeply the whole world knows it needs to be made right — and thank You that only in Your Son is that need answered, fully, finally, and freely. Give us honest tongues and tender hearts: never ashamed of the truth, never cruel with it. Send us to our neighbors the way You sent Your Son to us — not to condemn the world, but that the world through Him might be saved. In Jesus' name, Amen.

Christ Proclaimed. Jesus is Lord.
