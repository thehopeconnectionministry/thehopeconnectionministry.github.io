And when they had sung a hymn, they went out to the Mount of Olives.

Matthew 26:30 (ESV)

He sang.

After the supper, before the garden, before the arrest, Matthew tells us Jesus and His disciples sang a hymn and went out to the Mount of Olives (Matthew 26:30). Mark gives the same account: “And when they had sung a hymn, they went out to the Mount of Olives” (Mark 14:26).

That hymn was the Hallel — Psalms 113–118, the praise songs of the Passover. In the traditional order of the meal, the Hallel was sung in two halves: Psalms 113–114 before the meal, and Psalms 115–118 after it, once the cup had been blessed. So the last songs on the way out the door were the back half — the half that ends with Psalm 118.

Think about what was on His lips. He sang, “The stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone” (Psalm 118:22) — about Himself, hours before they rejected Him. He sang, “This is the day that the LORD has made; let us rejoice and be glad in it” (Psalm 118:24) — about that day. And woven through that same psalm stands the cry the crowds had shouted on Palm Sunday: “Hosanna” is Psalm 118:25 — “Save us, we pray, O LORD!” The shout of the triumphal entry and the song of the upper room come from the same page.

And those Psalms open and close with a word worth breaking down: HALLELUJAH. It is two Hebrew words in one. Halləlû — a command, plural: all of you, praise! From the root hālal: to shine, to boast, to celebrate out loud. And Yāh — the Lord’s own short name, Yahweh in His closest form, the name Exodus sings: “The LORD is my strength and my song” (Exodus 15:2). Hallelujah doesn’t describe praise. It summons it: “You all — praise Yah!”

That word sounds 24 times in the Psalms, and nowhere else in the Old Testament. Then it waits in silence — until Revelation 19, where heaven shouts it four times over the marriage supper of the Lamb. The song Jesus sang on the road to the cross is the song heaven sings at the wedding. Same word. Same praise. He was singing His way toward the very joy Revelation shows us finished.

How could He sing with the cross hours away? Hebrews tells us plainly: “for the joy that was set before him [he] endured the cross, despising the shame” (Hebrews 12:2). His singing was not because the cross was easy. It was because He willingly went to fulfill Scripture and save His people. His death was not an accident; it was God’s appointed plan — “delivered up according to the definite plan and foreknowledge of God” (Acts 2:23) — and His resurrection proves the sacrifice was accepted (1 Corinthians 15:3–4).

He sang on the way to the cross — not because the night was easy, but because He trusted His Father all the way through it.

Brother, sister — if He could sing hallelujah on that road, praise is never out of place on yours. Not praise that pretends the night isn’t dark. Praise that walks into the garden still trusting the Father. The bad news is real — the arrest was real, the cross was real. But the song was real too, and the song had the last word: an empty tomb, and heaven still singing.

Reflect — Questions & Answers

1. What hymn did Jesus sing on the night before the cross? The Hallel — Psalms 113–118, the praise songs of the Passover, sung in two halves around the meal. Matthew records it in one line: “And when they had sung a hymn, they went out to the Mount of Olives” (Matthew 26:30; see also Mark 14:26).

2. What does “hallelujah” actually mean? Two Hebrew words in one: halləlû — “all of you, praise!” — and Yāh, the Lord’s short name. It doesn’t describe praise; it summons it: “You all — praise Yah!” The word sounds 24 times in the Psalms, then four times in Revelation 19.

3. How could Jesus sing with the cross only hours away? Because of what was set before Him: “for the joy that was set before him [he] endured the cross, despising the shame” (Hebrews 12:2). He sang Psalm 118’s “The stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone” about Himself — and trusted His Father all the way through the night.
