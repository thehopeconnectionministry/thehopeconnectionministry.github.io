# Jesus in Psalms — A Commentary on the Psalter

**The Hope Connection**  
**Lewis Fuller**

*Meeting others where they are in the Name of Jesus*

*"And beginning with Moses and all the Prophets, he interpreted to them in all the Scriptures the things concerning himself."* — Luke 24:27 (ESV)

---

## Introduction — Jesus and the Psalms

Of all the books of the Bible, this is the one that lives on the nightstand. The Psalms are the book people reach for at 2 a.m., when the diagnosis has come back, when the marriage is hanging by a thread, when the cravings are loud and the house is quiet, when there are no words left and somebody has to lend us theirs. For three thousand years, God's people have prayed this book in hospital rooms and prison cells, in foxholes and delivery rooms, at gravesides and kitchen tables. And here is the wonder at the center of it: before the Psalms were ever our prayer book, they were His.

**Jesus prayed this book.** That is not a figure of speech. Jesus of Nazareth grew up singing the Psalms in the synagogue at Nazareth. He sang the Hallel — Psalms 113–118 — at the table on the night He was betrayed, hours before He drank the cup those songs were carrying Him toward. He prayed Psalm 22 from the cross: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" He committed His spirit to the Father with the words of Psalm 31: "Into your hand I commit my spirit." He answered His tempters, comforted His disciples, and confounded His enemies out of this book. When He wanted to prove that David's son was also David's Lord, He reached for Psalm 110. When He wanted to tell the disciples what had happened to Judas, He reached for Psalms 69 and 109. The Psalter was the prayer book of the Son of God. When you pray these psalms, you are not praying alone, and you are not praying first. You are joining a prayer that Jesus Himself has already prayed — and praying it, now, in Him.

**Jesus said this book speaks of Him.** On the evening of the resurrection, on the road to Emmaus and then in the upper room, the risen Christ opened the Scriptures to His disciples. Luke tells us exactly what He said:

> "And he said to them, 'These are my words that I spoke to you while I was still with you, that everything written about me in the Law of Moses and the Prophets and the Psalms must be fulfilled.' Then he opened their minds to understand the Scriptures." — Luke 24:44–45 (ESV)

Notice the threefold division — the Law, the Prophets, and *the Psalms* — the whole Old Testament as the Jews of that day counted it, with the Psalms standing at the head of the third and largest section. And notice what Jesus says is written there: things *about me*. Not some of the Psalms. The Psalms. The whole book, in its joys and its rages and its tears, is a book about Christ — about His sufferings and His glories, His prayers and His kingdom, His cross and His crown. That is why this commentary is called *Jesus in Psalms*. We are not dragging Christ into a book that is not about Him. We are following Him into the book He told us was His.

**The church's prayer book and the hurting person's book.** The Psalms are the only book of the Bible that is made up almost entirely of words spoken *to* God rather than *from* God — and yet they are God's own words, given back to us so that we would know how to speak to Him. Every human condition is here. There is praise here that shakes the rafters and lament here that barely gets above a whisper. There is confession with the sin named out loud, and thanksgiving with the deliverance still fresh. There are psalms for the sickbed and psalms for the battlefield, psalms for the guilty and psalms for the betrayed, psalms for the man who cannot sleep and psalms for the woman who cannot stop weeping. John Calvin called the Psalter "an anatomy of all the parts of the soul" — there is no feeling a human being can have, he said, that is not mirrored here as in a glass. He was right. Whatever is in your heart tonight, there is a psalm that has already said it to God before you. You do not have to clean it up first. You just have to pray it.

**The shape of the book: five books.** The Psalter is not a pile of songs thrown together. It is a book, carefully arranged in five books — an echo, many have noticed, of the five books of Moses, as if to say: here is Israel's answer to God's Word, prayer answering Scripture, song answering law. Each of the five books closes with a doxology, a burst of praise that marks the end of one movement and the beginning of the next:

- **Book One — Psalms 1–41**, closing with Psalm 41:13: "Blessed be the LORD, the God of Israel, from everlasting to everlasting! Amen and Amen." These are mostly David's psalms — personal, close, full of trust and trouble, the prayers of a man after God's own heart learning to lean on God in caves and in palaces.
- **Book Two — Psalms 42–72**, closing with Psalm 72:18–19: "Blessed be the LORD, the God of Israel, who alone does wondrous things. Blessed be his glorious name forever; may the whole earth be filled with his glory! Amen and Amen!" Here the horizon widens — the sons of Korah, Asaph, and David again — and the book ends with the great royal psalm of the coming King, and a quiet note: "The prayers of David, the son of Jesse, are ended."
- **Book Three — Psalms 73–89**, closing with Psalm 89:52: "Blessed be the LORD forever! Amen and Amen." This is the darkest of the five books. The temple is in ruins, the covenant seems broken, and Book Three ends in lament, with Ethan's question hanging in the air: where is God's steadfast love of old? The Psalter does not skip the dark chapters of God's people's life. Neither will we.
- **Book Four — Psalms 90–106**, closing with Psalm 106:48: "Blessed be the LORD, the God of Israel, from everlasting to everlasting! And let all the people say, 'Amen!' Praise the LORD!" The answer to Book Three's darkness begins with the oldest psalm of all — Moses' Psalm 90 — and thunders through the great "The LORD reigns" psalms. When there is no king on Israel's throne, these psalms announce that there is still a King on heaven's.
- **Book Five — Psalms 107–150**, closing not with a verse but with a crescendo: the final five psalms, 146–150, each beginning and ending with "Praise the LORD!" — the great Hallelujah at the end of the book. Here are the Songs of Ascents that pilgrims sang on the road to Jerusalem, the great acrostics, the story of redemption retold — and everything gathers toward praise. The Psalter begins with one blessed man meditating on God's Word day and night, and it ends with everything that has breath praising the LORD. That is the journey. That is the road this commentary walks.

**Read in the Reformation way.** This commentary reads the Psalms the way the Reformers taught the church to read every book of the Bible, and it is worth naming their five watchwords up front, because they run through every psalm that follows. *Sola scriptura* — Scripture alone is our rule; the Psalms interpret the Psalms, and every claim in these pages stands or falls by the text. *Sola gratia* — grace alone saves; nobody in this book earns God's mercy — David the adulterer, Asaph the doubter, Heman in the darkness all receive it as gift or not at all. *Sola fide* — faith alone receives that grace; the Psalter's own heartbeat word is *trust* — "in you I trust" — from the first book to the last. *Solus Christus* — Christ alone is the Savior; the King of Psalm 2, the sufferer of Psalm 22, the priest-king of Psalm 110 are one and the same Person, and every psalm finds its Amen in Him (2 Corinthians 1:20). And *soli Deo gloria* — glory to God alone; that is why the book ends the way it does, with everything that has breath giving Him praise.

The Reformers loved this book with a particular love. Martin Luther called the Psalter "a little Bible," a summary of the whole Scripture in song, and he turned to it constantly — Psalm 46 gave him "A Mighty Fortress Is Our God," and in his darkest hours he would say to his friends, "Come, let us sing Psalm 130." Calvin, as we have seen, called it an anatomy of the soul, and under his hand Geneva filled its worship with the singing of psalms — ordinary people, with God's own words on their lips. Spurgeon spent twenty years preaching and writing on this book and called his work *The Treasury of David*; he said the Psalms were the very breath of the church's prayer life. When you read the Psalms with the Reformers, you learn two things at once: to keep the law and the gospel distinct — the psalms of confession will search you and strip away every hiding place (the bad news), and the psalms of trust will set before you a Savior who has prayed your prayers, borne your sins, and risen (the good news) — and to expect that the God who gave us this book intends to be prayed to with it, boldly, honestly, and in Jesus' name.

**How this commentary is arranged.** A hundred and fifty psalms cannot be handled the way sixteen chapters of Mark were handled — a full treatment of every verse of every psalm would fill a shelf and bury the reader. So the arrangement here is honest and deliberate. *Every psalm is here* — all 150, none skipped, in order, organized by the five books. Every psalm gets its own entry: its theme, how Jesus is found in it, and its key verse quoted in full from the English Standard Version. And the great psalms — the messianic psalms and the beloved psalms that God's people have lived on for centuries — get the full treatment: a walk through the psalm's movements, three to five key verses in full, the bad news and the good news, a note from the Reformers, and a reflection question. Psalm 22 and Psalm 23, Psalm 51 and Psalm 130, Psalm 110 and Psalm 139 — those we slow down for. The rest we walk through steadily, psalm by psalm, the way you would walk a road you intend to finish.

Read it slowly. Read it with your Bible open. And remember, as you read, that you are holding the prayer book of the Lord Jesus Christ — and that He has given it to you, so that on the nights when you have no words, you would have His.

## How to Use This Commentary

This commentary was built to be used, not just read. Here is how to get the most out of it.

**The order of every session.** Work through each psalm the same way, in this order:

1. **Read the psalm in your own Bible first.** Open your Bible to the psalm and read it all the way through — pray it, if you can — before you read a word of commentary. The commentary serves the Scripture; it never replaces it. Read it out loud if you can. The Psalms were written to be heard and sung and prayed aloud, and you will catch things with your ears that your eyes slide past.
2. **Then read the commentary entry for that psalm.** Keep your Bible open beside it and look up the verses as they come. Start with "Jesus in this psalm" — before anything else, fix your eyes on how Christ is revealed there, how He prayed it, or how it leads to Him. Charles Spurgeon said a passage is not finished until Christ has been seen in it, and that is the aim of every entry here.
3. **Then sit with the question.** The full-treatment psalms end with a reflection question and a "bad news and good news" paragraph. Do not rush past them. Read the bad news honestly — let the Word do its searching work — and then read the good news slowly, out loud if you can. Answer the reflection question in prayer, out loud to God, not just in your head. For the shorter entries, let the key verse itself be your prayer: read it back to God in your own words.
4. **Write in a notebook.** Keep a simple notebook with this study. Write down the key verse of the psalms you are walking through, your answer to the reflection questions, and anything the Lord shows you along the way. A truth is not really yours until you have written it, prayed it, and lived it. You do not need anything fancy — a dollar notebook and a pen will do the whole Psalter.

**The rhythm: a few psalms a week.** This fits the study rhythm used across The Hope Connection: three sessions a week, one book at a time, until the book is done. With the Psalms, a natural session is one full-treatment psalm, or a small run of shorter entries — say three to five psalms at a sitting. At that pace you will walk the whole Psalter in the better part of a year, and that is no flaw: this is a book the church has prayed for three thousand years, and it repays slowness. If you want to go slower still — one psalm a day, with your morning coffee, all the way through — that is a fine and ancient way to pray this book, and many of God's people have done exactly that, over and over, their whole lives long. Either pace is fine. What matters is the steadiness, not the speed.

**No catch-up piles.** This rule is not negotiable, because it protects the whole habit: if you miss a day, you do not owe anybody two sessions. You simply pick up at the next psalm at the next session. Life happens — sickness, family, work, hard weeks. A study rhythm that piles up guilt every time life interrupts will not survive a month. This one bends and keeps going. Missed a week? Start again at the next psalm. The Lord is not keeping a tally, and neither are we. The Psalms themselves will teach you this: they are full of people who failed, wandered, and came back — and found the door still open.

**Using it alone.** Everything above is written for one reader, one Bible, one notebook, and the Lord. Pray before you open the book — a short, real prayer: "Lord, open my eyes, and let this Word search me." Pray again when you finish, turning the psalm's good news into thanksgiving and its bad news into confession. And on the days when you cannot find words of your own, do what God's people have always done: pray the psalm itself, slowly, as your own prayer, in Jesus' name. It counts. It has always counted.

**Using it with a spouse or a friend.** Read the psalm out loud together at the start of the session — take turns reading. Then read the commentary entry, or take turns reading it aloud too. Talk through the reflection question together, honestly. Pray for each other out of what the psalm showed you. There is a reason the Psalms are written so often in the plural — "let us," "we will" — this book was made for voices joined together, and two people praying it together will see things one reader alone will miss.

**Using it in a small group.** Take one full-treatment psalm per meeting, or a small run of entries. Have someone read the psalm aloud from the Bible to open. Walk through the commentary together, stopping wherever the text raises a question. Save real time at the end for the reflection question — that is where study becomes life. Close in prayer, and let the memory verse for that part of the Psalter (see the Psalms Study Guide below) be the group's verse for the week: read it together at the start of the next meeting and see who can say it from memory. Keep the group small enough that everybody talks, and keep the door open — the Psalms are good news for people who have never opened a Bible, and every entry here is written plain enough for a brand-new believer to follow.

**The memory verses.** The study guide below gives memory verses for the key psalms of each of the five books — the spine of the Psalter. If you take one verse a week alongside the psalm rhythm, by the time you finish the book you will carry its whole story in your heart: the blessed man and the King, the forsaken cry and the Shepherd's care, the thirst of the deer and the depths of Psalm 130, the Lord who neither slumbers nor sleeps, and the final Hallelujah. That is treasure no one can take from you — and on the hardest nights, it is the kind of treasure that prays itself, when you have nothing left.

### Study Questions — One per Book

Work each question in your notebook as you finish that book of the Psalter. Many of them follow the Bible's own pattern of putting off the old and putting on the new (Ephesians 4:22–24; Colossians 3:8–10).

1. **Book One — Learn to trust in the cave.** Book One is David praying through trouble — betrayal, sickness, enemies, delay. Put off the habit of praying only when things are good; put on David's habit of running to God first. Which psalm from Book One will you make your own prayer this week — and when will you pray it, out loud, on purpose?
2. **Book Two — Come clean, and look for the King.** Book Two holds both the thirst of Psalm 42 and the confession of Psalm 51, and it ends with the King of Psalm 72. Put off hiding; put on confession. What is one sin you will name to God plainly this week, the way David did — and then look up, and ask the King to reign in that very place?
3. **Book Three — Hold on in the dark.** Book Three is the Psalter's valley: envy, ruins, and a covenant that looks broken. Put off measuring God's love by your circumstances; put on Asaph's turnaround — "until I went into the sanctuary." What is one loss you will bring honestly to God this week, without dressing it up, and one truth from His Word you will set beside it?
4. **Book Four — Let the King have His throne.** Book Four answers the darkness with a single announcement: the LORD reigns. Put off one anxiety about the future of your family, your church, or your country that you have been carrying as if the throne were empty; put on worship. Which of the "LORD reigns" psalms will you read aloud this week, slowly, as an act of surrender?
5. **Book Five — Walk the road home, singing.** Book Five is pilgrims going up to Jerusalem and a whole creation ending in praise. Put off silence; put on telling. Psalm 107 says, "Let the redeemed of the LORD say so." Who is one person you will tell this week what the Lord has done for you — and which psalm from Book Five will you carry in your pocket as you go?

# Book One — Psalms 1–41: David's Prayers

*Mostly the psalms of David — a shepherd, a fugitive, a king, and a sinner — learning to trust God in caves and palaces alike. Book One closes with Psalm 41:13: "Blessed be the LORD, the God of Israel, from everlasting to everlasting! Amen and Amen."*

---

## Psalm 1 — The Blessed Man: Two Ways to Live

### Jesus in this psalm

Jesus is the Blessed Man of Psalm 1 — the only man who ever fully lived what this psalm describes. He never walked in the counsel of the wicked, never stood in the way of sinners, never sat in the seat of scoffers; His delight was in the law of the LORD, and on it He meditated day and night. Every other man and woman who has ever lived has failed this psalm. He did not. And because He lived it, He can give its blessedness away: those who are in Christ are planted by His streams, and the fruit of His righteousness is counted as theirs. The psalm stands at the gate of the whole Psalter as both a portrait of Jesus and an invitation to walk His way.

### Commentary

**The blessed man described (1:1–2).** The Psalter opens not with a prayer but with a beatitude — "Blessed is the man." Blessed means happy in the deepest sense: approved by God, flourishing under His favor, right side up in a world gone wrong. And notice how the blessing is first described by what the man does *not* do. He does not *walk* in the counsel of the wicked — he does not take his directions from people who leave God out. He does not *stand* in the way of sinners — he does not settle into their path, lingering where sin is comfortable. He does not *sit* in the seat of scoffers — he does not take his place among those who mock holy things. Walk, stand, sit: the verbs slow down as the compromise deepens. Sin rarely takes a man all at once; it slows him down until he is seated. The blessed man is blessed, first, because he has learned what to refuse.

But the psalm will not leave us with refusals. The man's positive life is in verse 2:

> "But his delight is in the law of the LORD, and on his law he meditates day and night." — Psalm 1:2 (ESV)

Delight — not duty, not drudgery. The word of God is this man's pleasure, the thing he turns over in his mind (*meditates* — the picture is of murmuring it aloud, chewing it over) in the morning and in the night watches. This is the doorway into the whole Psalter: before the Psalms teach us to pray, Psalm 1 teaches us to feed. Prayer that is not fed by the Word becomes an echo of our own feelings. A man who meditates on the Word day and night will have something to say to God at 2 a.m. — and something to hear.

**The tree (1:3).** The picture now is one of the most beloved in all Scripture:

> "He is like a tree planted by streams of water that yields its fruit in its season, and its leaf does not wither. In all that he does, he prospers." — Psalm 1:3 (ESV)

Planted — not wild, not accidental. Someone put this tree where the water is. And the tree does what trees do: it drinks, it roots, it bears fruit *in its season* — not all at once, not on demand, but faithfully, in God's timing — and its leaf does not wither, because the supply beneath it does not fail. This is not a promise that the righteous man will be rich or trouble-free; the very next psalms will show the righteous man in caves and on sickbeds. It is a promise that a life rooted in God's Word has a hidden supply that circumstances cannot cut off. The drought may come. The roots are in the river.

**The wicked are not so (1:4–5).** The turn is abrupt: "The wicked are not so." Everything the righteous man is, the wicked man is not. He is "like chaff that the wind drives away" — the dry husk of the grain, weightless, rootless, useless, gone with the first gust off the threshing floor. Chaff has no root and no fruit; it only has a direction — away. Therefore the wicked will not stand in the judgment, nor sinners in the congregation of the righteous. There is a judgment coming, and there is a congregation — an assembly of the righteous — and the tragedy of the wicked is not merely punishment but exclusion: they will have no place among the people they refused to join.

**The LORD knows the way (1:6).** The psalm ends with the ground of it all:

> "For the LORD knows the way of the righteous, but the way of the wicked will perish." — Psalm 1:6 (ESV)

"Knows" here is more than awareness; it is the knowledge of love and ownership, the way a shepherd knows his sheep. Two ways, two ends. One way is known by God — watched, kept, and brought home. The other way perishes — it does not arrive anywhere; it simply ends. Psalm 1 sets before every reader of the Psalter the oldest choice in the world, the same choice Moses set before Israel and Jesus set before His hearers when He spoke of the narrow gate and the wide road. And the whole book that follows is God's gracious tutoring of our feet into the way that He knows.

### Key verses

> "Blessed is the man who walks not in the counsel of the wicked, nor stands in the way of sinners, nor sits in the seat of scoffers." — Psalm 1:1 (ESV)

> "But his delight is in the law of the LORD, and on his law he meditates day and night." — Psalm 1:2 (ESV)

> "He is like a tree planted by streams of water that yields its fruit in its season, and its leaf does not wither. In all that he does, he prospers." — Psalm 1:3 (ESV)

> "For the LORD knows the way of the righteous, but the way of the wicked will perish." — Psalm 1:6 (ESV)

### The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Psalm 1 is that, measured by this psalm, none of us is the blessed man. We have all walked in the counsel of the wicked, stood in the way of sinners, and sat down comfortably among the scoffers; our delight has run to a hundred things before it ran to God's Word, and left to ourselves we are chaff — weightless, rootless, and bound to be driven away. But the good news is that One Man *did* live this psalm, completely, and His name is Jesus Christ. He is the tree planted by the streams, and everyone who is joined to Him by faith is grafted into His life: His meditation becomes our feeding, His righteousness our standing, His way our way. The LORD knows the way of the righteous — because the righteous are those who are in Christ, and the Father knows His own Son's way, and ours in Him.

**A note from the Reformers.** Luther loved Psalm 1 as the right doorway into the Psalter, and he read its blessed man the way the church must: not as a ladder for sinners to climb, but as a portrait of Christ and of those made righteous in Him. The law in this psalm shows us what we are not; the gospel shows us the One who is — and gives us His blessedness as a gift. That is *sola fide* at the very gate of the prayer book.

**Reflection question:** Where have your feet been slowing down — walking, standing, sitting — in a way that is pulling you from the Word? And what would it look like this week, concretely, to move your roots back to the streams: one time of day, one passage, one practice?

## Psalm 2 — The LORD's Anointed: Kiss the Son

### Jesus in this psalm

Jesus is the Son and the Anointed King of Psalm 2 — this is one of the most directly messianic psalms in the Bible, and the New Testament quotes it again and again about Christ. The apostles prayed it in Acts 4 about the conspiracy of Herod and Pilate against Jesus. Paul preached it in Acts 13 about the resurrection: "You are my Son, today I have begotten you." The Father speaks it again from heaven in Revelation, where the risen Christ rules the nations with a rod of iron. The raging of the nations, the decree of the Father, the inheritance of the earth, and the final call — "Kiss the Son" — all of it is about Jesus Christ, the King God has set on Zion, whom every rebel is summoned to embrace before it is too late.

### Commentary

**The nations rage (2:1–3).** The psalm opens with a question of astonishment:

> "Why do the nations rage and the peoples plot in vain?" — Psalm 2:1 (ESV)

The kings of the earth set themselves, and the rulers take counsel together, against the LORD and against His Anointed — *Anointed* is the word *Messiah* in Hebrew, *Christ* in Greek. Their counsel is summed up in one sentence of rebellion: "Let us burst their bonds apart and cast away their cords from us." That is the creed of fallen man in every age: God's rule is felt as bondage, and freedom is defined as life without Him. The plot is called, in advance, *vain* — empty, futile. The nations are raging against heaven, and heaven is not nervous.

**The Father laughs, and decrees (2:4–6).** The scene shifts from earth to heaven, and the tone is one of the most startling in Scripture: "He who sits in the heavens laughs; the Lord holds them in derision." This is not the laughter of cruelty; it is the calm of absolute sovereignty. The rebellion of earth does not threaten the throne; it only reveals the folly of the rebels. Then God speaks in wrath and terrifies them in His fury — and what He says is not an argument but an announcement: "As for me, I have set my King on Zion, my holy hill." The kings of the earth *set themselves*; God *has set* His King. The contest is not between two rival installations that might go either way. One is already done.

**The Son speaks (2:7–9).** Now the King Himself speaks, and recites the decree of His appointment:

> "I will tell of the decree: The LORD said to me, 'You are my Son; today I have begotten you.'" — Psalm 2:7 (ESV)

This is the verse the apostles applied to the resurrection of Jesus — the day God publicly declared and enthroned His Son with power. And with sonship comes inheritance: "Ask of me, and I will make the nations your heritage, and the ends of the earth your possession." The Son's kingdom is not a corner of Palestine; it is the ends of the earth. "You shall break them with a rod of iron and dash them in pieces like a potter's vessel." The rod is real. The King who was crucified will judge; mercy offered and refused becomes judgment executed. The New Testament ends with this same picture — the Rider on the white horse who rules the nations with a rod of iron (Revelation 19:15). Psalm 2 is the whole career of Christ in one psalm: opposed, enthroned, inheriting, and judging.

**Kiss the Son (2:10–12).** And then — this is the grace that runs through the whole Bible — the psalm turns from the rebels and *pleads* with them. "Now therefore, O kings, be wise; be warned, O rulers of the earth. Serve the LORD with fear, and rejoice with trembling." And the final command:

> "Kiss the Son, lest he be angry, and you perish in the way, when his wrath is kindled but a little. Blessed are all who take refuge in him." — Psalm 2:12 (ESV)

To kiss the son in the ancient world was the act of homage — the subject bending to kiss the king's hand or feet, surrendering and honoring at once. That is what God commands rebels to do: not to storm the throne, not to negotiate terms, but to come and kiss the Son — to surrender to Jesus Christ and to love Him in the surrender. And the last line turns the whole psalm into an invitation: "Blessed are *all* who take refuge in him." Psalm 1 began with the blessed man; Psalm 2 ends with the blessed rebels — all of them, any of them — who flee to the very King they opposed and find, instead of the rod, a refuge.

### Key verses

> "Why do the nations rage and the peoples plot in vain?" — Psalm 2:1 (ESV)

> "As for me, I have set my King on Zion, my holy hill." — Psalm 2:6 (ESV)

> "I will tell of the decree: The LORD said to me, 'You are my Son; today I have begotten you.'" — Psalm 2:7 (ESV)

> "Ask of me, and I will make the nations your heritage, and the ends of the earth your possession." — Psalm 2:8 (ESV)

> "Kiss the Son, lest he be angry, and you perish in the way, when his wrath is kindled but a little. Blessed are all who take refuge in him." — Psalm 2:12 (ESV)

### The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Psalm 2 is that every one of us was born on the wrong side of this psalm. The rage of the nations is not just out there in parliaments and armies; it is in here, in every heart that has said to God, "Let us burst their bonds apart" — that has felt His commands as cords and His lordship as a cage. And the Son we have rebelled against is not a weak claimant: He is the installed King, and His wrath is kindled but a little against those who refuse Him to the end. But the good news is that this same psalm, with the rod in the King's hand, ends with an open door: "Blessed are all who take refuge in him." The King against whom we raged is the refuge for ragers. Jesus Christ bore the wrath Himself at the cross so that rebels who come and kiss the Son — who surrender and trust — find not judgment but blessing. The door is open to *all*. That includes you.

**A note from the Reformers.** Calvin, commenting on this psalm, insisted that God does not merely permit the kingdom of Christ but has decreed it, so that the church's confidence rests not on the mood of nations but on the Father's settled purpose: "I have set my King." And the Reformers pressed Psalm 2:12 on their hearers as the sum of the gospel's demand and promise together — Christ is to be received by faith's homage, and those who take refuge in Him are pronounced blessed by God Himself. No refuge in our works, our wisdom, or our rage — refuge in the Son alone. *Solus Christus.*

**Reflection question:** In what area of your life are you still, quietly, saying "let us burst their bonds apart" — resisting the lordship of Christ? What would it mean, this week, to kiss the Son there: to surrender that ground and take refuge in Him instead of standing against Him?

### Psalm 3 — A Shield About Me

**Jesus in this psalm:** David wrote this fleeing from his own son Absalom — betrayed by family, weeping up the Mount of Olives barefoot (2 Samuel 15). Jesus walked that same road in reverse: He crossed to the Mount of Olives to be betrayed by a friend and arrested, the greater David absorbing the rebellion of those He loved. And where David lay down and slept in safety in the middle of a revolt, Jesus slept in a boat in the middle of a storm — the same untroubled trust in the Father. Jesus in this psalm is the true King who was betrayed, fled, and trusted — and who rose, as David prayed, with God as the lifter of His head.

**The key verse:**

> "But you, O LORD, are a shield about me, my glory, and the lifter of my head." — Psalm 3:3 (ESV)

When enemies multiply and voices say "there is no salvation for him in God," the answer is not to count the enemy but to name the Shield. David's head was bowed in shame; God lifted it. He will lift yours.

### Psalm 4 — Answered in the Night

**Jesus in this psalm:** David prays this at night, slandered by men who love lies, and he lies down in peace while his enemies are still talking. Jesus prayed this way — He was slandered as a glutton, a drunkard, a friend of sinners, a man in league with demons, and He answered His accusers mostly with silence and His Father with trust, committing Himself to Him who judges justly (1 Peter 2:23). In Christ, this psalm becomes the prayer of every believer who has been lied about: God hears, God knows the godly, and God — not the verdict of men — has the last word before morning.

**The key verse:**

> "In peace I will both lie down and sleep; for you alone, O LORD, make me dwell in safety." — Psalm 4:8 (ESV)

Safety is not the absence of enemies; it is the presence of God. That is why a man can sleep on the worst night of his life — and why you can too.

### Psalm 5 — Morning Prayer

**Jesus in this psalm:** David comes to God in the morning and then *watches* — he prays and looks for the answer like a watchman for the dawn. Jesus is the supreme morning pray-er: rising very early, while it was still dark, He went out to pray (Mark 1:35), and He taught us that the Father hears those who ask, seek, and knock. But Jesus is also the answer to this psalm's other half: David asks to be led in God's righteousness and kept from the company of the wicked; in Christ we are given both a righteousness not our own and a Leader who walks the straight path ahead of us. Pray this psalm in the morning, and you are praying with Jesus and to the Father who heard Him.

**The key verse:**

> "O LORD, in the morning you hear my voice; in the morning I prepare a sacrifice for you and watch." — Psalm 5:3 (ESV)

Give God the first voice of the day, before the phone, before the news, before the noise. Then watch. A God who is addressed in the morning will be noticed all day.

---

### Psalm 6 — Tears in the Night

**Jesus in this psalm:** This is the first of the seven penitential psalms — David sick in body and sick at heart, bones troubled, soul greatly troubled, flooding his bed with tears. Jesus prayed in this psalm's register in Gethsemane, where His soul was "very sorrowful, even to death," and where, in the days of His flesh, He offered up prayers and supplications "with loud cries and tears" (Hebrews 5:7). He was heard — not by being spared the cross, but by being raised through it. That is how God hears this psalm in Christ: not always by removing the trouble, but by carrying the pray-er through it to the far side, where "the LORD has heard my plea" can be said with a whole heart.

**The key verse:**

> "The LORD has heard my plea; the LORD accepts my prayer." — Psalm 6:9 (ESV)

David says this while the tears are still wet. That is faith: not pretending the night is over, but announcing, in the middle of it, that heaven has heard.

### Psalm 7 — The Righteous Judge

**Jesus in this psalm:** David, falsely accused by Cush the Benjaminite, runs not to revenge but to the Judge: "O LORD my God, in you do I take refuge." He even invites God's examination — if I have done this evil, let my enemy pursue me. Jesus lived this psalm perfectly: falsely accused before councils and governors, He opened not His mouth in self-defense, and entrusted Himself — and His vindication — entirely to the Father. And Jesus is also the Judge this psalm looks to: the One before whom every false accusation will be reversed and every hidden wrong set right. In Christ, the slandered can do what David did — hand the case up.

**The key verse:**

> "I will give to the LORD the thanks due to his righteousness, and I will sing praise to the name of the LORD, the Most High." — Psalm 7:17 (ESV)

The psalm ends not with the verdict but with thanksgiving — praise offered in advance, because the Judge is righteous and the case is in His hands. That is the sound of a man who has stopped trying to be his own defense attorney.

## Psalm 8 — What Is Man? The Son of Man Crowned

### Jesus in this psalm

Jesus is the Son of Man of Psalm 8 — and He told us so Himself. When the children cried "Hosanna to the Son of David" in the temple and the chief priests objected, Jesus answered them from this psalm: "Have you never read, 'Out of the mouth of infants and nursing babies you have prepared praise'?" (Matthew 21:16). The apostles take the psalm's great question — "What is man that you are mindful of him, and the son of man that you care for him?" — and answer it with Jesus: "But we see him who for a little while was made lower than the angels, namely Jesus, crowned with glory and honor because of the suffering of death" (Hebrews 2:9). Psalm 8 is the story of mankind's lost crown and of the one Man who won it back: humbled beneath the angels, then crowned with glory and honor, with all things put under His feet.

### Commentary

**The LORD's majesty, and babies' praise (8:1–2).** The psalm begins and ends with the same cry: "O LORD, our Lord, how majestic is your name in all the earth!" David looks up at the heavens God has set His glory above, and then looks down at the strangest army God ever fielded: "Out of the mouth of babies and infants, you have established strength because of your foes, to still the enemy and the avenger." God's strength is not displayed the way the world expects. He stills His enemies not with louder enemies but with the praise of nursing babies — the weakest voices in the world carrying the strongest truth. Jesus reaches for exactly this verse when the powerful are offended by children's praise. Heaven is not impressed by our volume. The praise of the small and the helpless, offered to a majestic God, is a fortress.

**The great question (8:3–4).** Then comes the night sky and the question every honest person has asked under it:

> "When I look at your heavens, the work of your fingers, the moon and the stars, which you have set in place, what is man that you are mindful of him, and the son of man that you care for him?" — Psalm 8:3–4 (ESV)

The heavens are "the work of your *fingers*" — as if the galaxies were needlework to God, something He made with delicate ease. Against that backdrop, man is dust and a breath. And yet — here is the astonishment the whole Bible is built on — God is *mindful* of him. He remembers him. He *cares for* him, visits him, attends to him. The question is not really "why are we so great?" It is "why is God so gracious?" Psalm 8 does not answer by inflating man. It answers by exalting God's stooping love.

**Crowned, and given dominion (8:5–8).** "Yet you have made him a little lower than the heavenly beings and crowned him with glory and honor." This is Genesis 1 sung back to God: man made in God's image, crowned — actually crowned, like a king — and given dominion over the works of God's hands: sheep and oxen, beasts of the field, birds of the heavens, fish of the sea. "You have put all things under his feet." That was the design. But anyone who reads Psalm 8 honestly and then reads the news knows the design is in ruins: man does not rule creation in glory; he exploits it, fears it, and is ruled by his own appetites. The crown has slipped. The dominion is broken. Psalm 8, read alone, is a psalm about what we were made to be and are not.

**The answer: Jesus (Hebrews 2).** That is why the New Testament's reading of this psalm is so precious. The writer to the Hebrews quotes Psalm 8 and then admits, "At present, we do not yet see everything in subjection to him" — to man. "But we see him… namely Jesus." Jesus took our nature and our place. For a little while He was made lower than the angels — truly man, hungry, weary, mortal. He tasted death for everyone. And now He is "crowned with glory and honor because of the suffering of death." The crown Adam dropped, Christ picked up — not by grasping, but by suffering. And because the crown is on *His* head, it will one day be shared: those who are in Christ will reign with Him, and creation itself will be set free into the freedom of the glory of the children of God. Psalm 8 is not finally about our dignity. It is about His — and about the dignity He restores to everyone who is His.

### Key verses

> "O LORD, our Lord, how majestic is your name in all the earth! You have set your glory above the heavens." — Psalm 8:1 (ESV)

> "When I look at your heavens, the work of your fingers, the moon and the stars, which you have set in place, what is man that you are mindful of him, and the son of man that you care for him?" — Psalm 8:3–4 (ESV)

> "Yet you have made him a little lower than the heavenly beings and crowned him with glory and honor." — Psalm 8:5 (ESV)

> "You have given him dominion over the works of your hands; you have put all things under his feet." — Psalm 8:6 (ESV)

### The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Psalm 8 is that the crown does not fit us anymore. We were made to be mindful-of-God image-bearers ruling a good creation in glory and honor, and instead we are small, sinful, and mortal — "what is man?" is a question our pride cannot survive. Left to ourselves, the dominion is lost and the glory is gone, and the stars preach our smallness every clear night. But the good news is that God has not left the question hanging. The Son of Man came down — lower than the angels, down to a manger, down to a cross, down to a tomb — and He has been raised and crowned with glory and honor, with all things being put under His feet. In Him, the mindful God has visited us not in wrath but in mercy, and everyone who belongs to Jesus will share His crown. The God who made the galaxies with His fingers knows your name, and He has not forgotten it.

**A note from the Reformers.** Calvin marveled at this psalm's picture of God's condescension: that the God whose glory is above the heavens should be mindful of man at all is, he said, a miracle greater than the creation itself. And the Reformers read the psalm's "son of man" Christologically, with Hebrews: the dominion of Psalm 8 is presently and truly Christ's, and only derivatively — by grace, in Him — ours. Our worth is not a possession we defend; it is a gift we receive from the crowned Christ. *Soli Deo gloria* — the psalm ends where it began, with His majestic name, not ours.

**Reflection question:** Under a night sky, the question is "what is man that you are mindful of him?" Where have you been trying to prove your own significance — and what would change if you rested instead in this: the God of the galaxies is mindful of you, and His Son has been crowned for you?

### Psalm 9 — The LORD Is a Stronghold for the Oppressed

**Jesus in this psalm:** Psalms 9 and 10 form one acrostic poem in the Hebrew — a song of thanksgiving for God's judgment on the wicked and His care for the afflicted. David praises the God who sits enthroned forever, who does not forget the cry of the afflicted, who is "the avenger of blood." Jesus stands in this psalm as the Judge who will set every crooked thing straight and as the King who tells His suffering church, in Revelation, that the blood of the martyrs is remembered and will be answered. In Christ, no cry of the oppressed has ever been filed away and forgotten; it is on the Judge's desk, and the Judge is coming.

**The key verse:**

> "The LORD is a stronghold for the oppressed, a stronghold in times of trouble." — Psalm 9:9 (ESV)

A stronghold is not a feeling; it is a place you run to. When trouble comes — and this psalm assumes it will — the oppressed have somewhere to go, and Someone to go to.

### Psalm 10 — Why Do You Stand Far Away?

**Jesus in this psalm:** This is the prayer of the victim: the wicked man hunts the poor, boasts, renounces God, and seems to get away with it — while God appears to stand far away and hide Himself. Every sufferer who has watched evil prosper has prayed this psalm, and so, in His humanity, did the Lord who was Himself hunted, seized, and killed by wicked men who thought God would not call them to account. But Jesus is also the answer to the psalm's cry: He is the King forever who hears the desire of the afflicted and does justice for the fatherless and the oppressed — now in part, through His church and His providence, and soon in full, at His appearing.

**The key verse:**

> "O LORD, you hear the desire of the afflicted; you will strengthen their heart; you will incline your ear." — Psalm 10:17 (ESV)

God's ear is inclined — leaned in, like someone listening hard at a door. The silence of heaven is not the absence of God. He hears the desire before it becomes words.

### Psalm 11 — In the LORD I Take Refuge

**Jesus in this psalm:** Advisers are telling David to flee like a bird to the mountains — the foundations are destroyed; what can the righteous do? David's answer is the first line: "In the LORD I take refuge." Jesus prayed and lived this psalm under greater pressure: urged to flee Herod, to come down from the cross, to save Himself, He refused every escape route that would take Him out of the Father's will, because He trusted the Father who "loves righteous deeds" and whose face the upright shall behold. In Christ, the question "what can the righteous do?" has its answer: not flee, not despair — take refuge, and behold His face.

**The key verse:**

> "For the LORD is righteous; he loves righteous deeds; the upright shall behold his face." — Psalm 11:7 (ESV)

The foundations of the world may shake; the face of God does not change. The upright shall behold it — that is a promise Jesus bought with His own forsakenness, when the face was hidden from Him so it would never be hidden from us.

### Psalm 12 — The Words of the LORD Are Pure

**Jesus in this psalm:** The godly are gone, the faithful have vanished, and lying lips are everywhere — "with flattering lips and a double heart they speak." David cries, "Save, O LORD," and God's answer is a promise: "I will now arise." Jesus is that arising. He is also the great contrast of this psalm: the Word made flesh, whose every word was pure — no lie, no flattery, no double heart was found in Him. And He has left us the words this psalm celebrates: the promises of God, refined like silver seven times over, against the cheap talk of men. When every human voice has disappointed you, there is still a voice that has never once lied.

**The key verse:**

> "The words of the LORD are pure words, like silver refined in a furnace on the ground, purified seven times." — Psalm 12:6 (ESV)

Silver refined seven times has nothing left in it that is not silver. God's promises are like that: no dross, no exaggeration, no fine print. You can build a life — and an eternity — on them.

### Psalm 13 — How Long, O LORD?

**Jesus in this psalm:** Four times in two verses David cries "How long?" — how long will You forget me, hide Your face, let sorrow fill my heart and my enemy exalt over me? This is the prayer of everyone who has waited in a hospital corridor, a courtroom, a relapse, a silent heaven. Jesus entered this "how long" Himself — He asked the Father why He had forsaken Him, and in Gethsemane He prayed while heaven seemed shut. But notice where David lands, and where every believer in Christ can land with him: not on an explanation, but on a Person. "But I have trusted in your steadfast love." The psalm ends with singing — before the circumstances change. That is not denial. That is faith with its eyes open.

**The key verse:**

> "But I have trusted in your steadfast love; my heart shall rejoice in your salvation." — Psalm 13:5 (ESV)

"But I have trusted" — past tense, settled ground. When you cannot trace God's hand, you can still trust His heart, because at the cross He showed it to you once for all.

### Psalm 14 — The Fool Says There Is No God

**Jesus in this psalm:** This psalm is God's X-ray of the human race: the fool says in his heart, "There is no God"; all have turned aside; there is none who does good, not even one. Paul takes these very verses as the centerpiece of his indictment of mankind in Romans 3 — Jew and Gentile alike, all under sin. So Jesus in this psalm is, first, the absent One we are all indicted for excluding — and then, gloriously, He is the salvation the psalm ends by longing for: "Oh, that salvation for Israel would come out of Zion!" It did. Salvation came out of Zion, in a manger and on a cross, for fools who said there was no God — or who lived as if there wasn't, which is the same folly with better manners.

**The key verse:**

> "Oh, that salvation for Israel would come out of Zion! When the LORD restores the fortunes of his people, let Jacob rejoice, let Israel be glad." — Psalm 14:7 (ESV)

The psalm that begins with the fool ends with rejoicing — because God's answer to a world of fools is not to abandon it but to send salvation into the middle of it. He sent it. His name is Jesus.

### Psalm 15 — Who Shall Dwell on Your Holy Hill?

**Jesus in this psalm:** "O LORD, who shall sojourn in your tent? Who shall dwell on your holy hill?" The answer describes a blameless walker, a truth-speaker, a man who does not slander, does not lend at interest, cannot be bribed, and "shall never be moved." Read honestly, this psalm is a locked door: which of us has done *all* of this, always? Only One. Jesus is the true citizen of Zion, the only man who ever fully met this psalm's terms — and He met them not to keep the hill to Himself but to bring guests up it with Him. In Christ, sinners who could never qualify are welcomed into the tent, clothed in His blamelessness, and then — by His Spirit — slowly made into the kind of people this psalm describes.

**The key verse:**

> "He who walks blamelessly and does what is right and speaks truth in his heart." — Psalm 15:2 (ESV)

This verse is first a portrait of Jesus, then a promise of what grace makes of us. We enter the hill by His blamelessness; we learn to walk by His Spirit. Both are gifts. Neither is earned.

## Psalm 16 — You Will Not Abandon My Soul to Sheol

### Jesus in this psalm

Jesus is the resurrection promise of Psalm 16 — and the apostles said so, in sermons, in the first days of the church. On the day of Pentecost, Peter stood up and quoted this psalm about David: David foresaw and spoke about the resurrection of the Christ, "that he was not abandoned to Hades, nor did his flesh see corruption" (Acts 2:31). Paul preached the same text in Pisidian Antioch (Acts 13:35). David's body decayed in its tomb; the words could not rest on David. They were always about his greater Son, whose body saw no corruption because God raised Him on the third day. And because they are true of Jesus, they are true of everyone in Jesus: "You make known to me the path of life" is now the believer's own inheritance.

### Commentary

**Preserve me, O God (16:1–4).** The psalm opens with a prayer for keeping: "Preserve me, O God, for in you I take refuge." Refuge again — the Psalter's heartbeat. David confesses, "I say to the LORD, 'You are my Lord; I have no good apart from you.'" That sentence could be carved over the whole life of faith. Whatever good we have — every friendship, every meal, every mercy — is downstream of Him; apart from Him, the spring itself is dry. David delights in "the saints in the land," God's people, and renounces the gods of the nations: those who run after another god multiply their sorrows, and David will not so much as take the names of those gods on his lips. There is a clean break here: one Lord, one refuge, no divided loyalties.

**The lines have fallen in pleasant places (16:5–6).** Then comes one of the most contented passages in the Bible:

> "The LORD is my chosen portion and my cup; you hold my lot. The lines have fallen for me in pleasant places; indeed, I have a beautiful inheritance." — Psalm 16:5–6 (ESV)

The language is from the dividing of the promised land — the surveyor's lines marking out each tribe's portion. David looks at the lot God has measured out for him — his life, his circumstances, his calling — and calls it *beautiful*. This is not the contentment of a man who has everything; David wrote psalms like this from caves. It is the contentment of a man whose portion is not a plot of land but the LORD Himself. When God is your portion, the lines cannot fall badly, whatever field they cross. "You hold my lot" — the future is not in the surveyor's hands or in chance's hands. It is in God's.

**The LORD at my right hand (16:7–8).** "I bless the LORD who gives me counsel; in the night also my heart instructs me." God counsels in the night — in the watches when fears talk loudest, the heart that has fed on God's Word in the day finds instruction in the dark. And then the verse Peter quoted at Pentecost:

> "I have set the LORD always before me; because he is at my right hand, I shall not be shaken." — Psalm 16:8 (ESV)

Peter applies this first to Jesus: the Son set the Father always before Him, through Gethsemane and through the cross, and therefore was not shaken — not in His trust, not in His obedience, not in His hope of resurrection. And what was true of the Head becomes true of the body: the believer who sets the LORD always before him has the same promise. Shaken circumstances, yes. A shaken standing, no.

**The path of life (16:9–11).** "Therefore my heart is glad, and my whole being rejoices; my flesh also dwells secure." And now the psalm rises to its summit:

> "For you will not abandon my soul to Sheol, or let your holy one see corruption." — Psalm 16:10 (ESV)

Sheol is the grave, the place of the dead. David confesses that God's care does not stop at the edge of it. For David himself, this was hope reaching beyond what he could see; for David's Son, it was prophecy fulfilled to the letter — the Holy One laid in a tomb on Friday and raised on Sunday, His flesh seeing no corruption, His soul not abandoned. And the final verse opens the door to every believer:

> "You make known to me the path of life; in your presence there is fullness of joy; at your right hand are pleasures forevermore." — Psalm 16:11 (ESV)

The path of life — the road that goes *through* death and out the other side. Fullness of joy — not a trickle, not a taste, but fullness — in His presence. Pleasures forevermore — not pleasures that end, at the right hand of God. Jesus walked that path first, and He walked it as a forerunner: where the Head has gone, the body will follow. Psalm 16 is why Christians can stand at gravesides and sing.

### Key verses

> "Preserve me, O God, for in you I take refuge." — Psalm 16:1 (ESV)

> "The LORD is my chosen portion and my cup; you hold my lot." — Psalm 16:5 (ESV)

> "I have set the LORD always before me; because he is at my right hand, I shall not be shaken." — Psalm 16:8 (ESV)

> "For you will not abandon my soul to Sheol, or let your holy one see corruption." — Psalm 16:10 (ESV)

> "You make known to me the path of life; in your presence there is fullness of joy; at your right hand are pleasures forevermore." — Psalm 16:11 (ESV)

### The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Psalm 16 is that, apart from God, the grave gets the last word on every one of us. Our portion in this world — health, family, work, even life itself — is held with a loose grip and a short lease, and Sheol does not negotiate. David's confidence cannot be explained by David's circumstances; measured by the flesh, every one of us is headed for corruption, and no amount of pleasant lines changes that. But the good news is that God has not abandoned His Holy One to the grave — and in raising Jesus, He has published His intentions for everyone who is in Him. Your soul will not be abandoned. Your flesh, too, dwells secure, because the One who holds your lot has already walked the path of life through death and out the other side, and He will bring you along it — to fullness of joy, in God's presence, forevermore.

**A note from the Reformers.** Spurgeon called Psalm 16 "the golden psalm" — its Hebrew title, *Michtam*, may mean exactly that, a golden jewel — and he read it, with Peter and Paul, as the resurrection psalm of Christ, in whose rising our own is pledged. Calvin noted the order of David's comfort: the psalm does not begin with immortality but with refuge — "in you I take refuge" — because the hope of resurrection is not a philosophy for the curious but a comfort for those who have fled to God. Faith first, then forever. That is the Reformation order too: *sola fide*, and the fullness of joy follows.

**Reflection question:** David says, "The LORD is my chosen portion." What are you treating as your portion instead — the thing whose loss would feel like losing everything? What would it mean, today, to choose the LORD as your portion again, and to say over your actual circumstances, "The lines have fallen for me in pleasant places"?

### Psalm 17 — Keep Me as the Apple of Your Eye

**Jesus in this psalm:** David prays as an innocent man under attack — "hear a just cause" — and asks to be kept "as the apple of your eye," hidden "in the shadow of your wings." His confidence at the end is resurrection-shaped: "As for me, I shall behold your face in righteousness; when I awake, I shall be satisfied with your likeness." Jesus is the truly innocent pray-er of this psalm, the One whose cause was perfectly just and who was attacked by men "whose portion is in this life." And Jesus is the answer to its closing hope: He awoke from death beholding the Father's face, and everyone in Him will awake the same way — satisfied, at last, with His likeness (1 John 3:2).

**The key verse:**

> "As for me, I shall behold your face in righteousness; when I awake, I shall be satisfied with your likeness." — Psalm 17:15 (ESV)

Men whose portion is in this life get their fill here and now. God's people get something better and later: waking up in His likeness. Some hopes are worth sleeping on.

### Psalm 18 — The LORD Is My Rock: The King's Song of Deliverance

**Jesus in this psalm:** This is David's great victory song (also found in 2 Samuel 22), sung "on the day when the LORD delivered him from the hand of all his enemies." Its language is too large for David alone — the earth reeling, God bowing the heavens, the cords of Sheol, and a deliverance "to his king" and "to his anointed… and to his offspring forever." Paul quotes its missionary line — "Therefore I will praise you among the Gentiles" (Psalm 18:49) — in Romans 15, about Christ. Jesus is the Anointed of this psalm: the One who cried from the grip of death and was heard, who was delivered because the Father delighted in Him, and whose deliverance is now extended to His offspring — all who are His by faith.

**The key verse:**

> "The LORD is my rock and my fortress and my deliverer, my God, my rock, in whom I take refuge, my shield, and the horn of my salvation, my stronghold." — Psalm 18:2 (ESV)

Nine titles in one verse — David stacking up every word he knows for "safe." When one name for God isn't enough for your trouble, do what David did: use them all. He answers to every one.

## Psalm 19 — The Heavens Declare: The Glory of God and the Gold of His Word

### Jesus in this psalm

Jesus is the Word at the center of Psalm 19 — and He is the sun's greater counterpart at its opening. The heavens declare God's glory in a speech all nations hear; Jesus is the fuller Word, the brightness of that glory in a human face, through whom the Father has spoken His last and best word (Hebrews 1:1–3). Paul quotes this psalm's opening in Romans 10 — "their voice has gone out to all the earth" — about the preaching of the gospel of Christ, as if to say: the silent sermon of the skies was always meant to lead to the spoken sermon of the Son. And the psalm's closing prayer — "let the words of my mouth and the meditation of my heart be acceptable in your sight, O LORD, my rock and my redeemer" — is a prayer Jesus prayed perfectly and now perfects in us: He is the Redeemer named in the last line, and it is His acceptability that makes our words acceptable.

### Commentary

**The heavens declare (19:1–4).** The psalm opens with the most famous nature poetry in the world:

> "The heavens declare the glory of God, and the sky above proclaims his handiwork. Day to day pours out speech, and night to night reveals knowledge." — Psalm 19:1–2 (ESV)

The heavens are preachers. Day after day, night after night, without pause, they pour out a sermon: there is a Maker, and He is glorious. This sermon has a strange property — "there is no speech, nor are there words, whose voice is not heard." It is speech without words, heard in every language because it needs no translation. No one on earth is outside the sound of it. The peasant and the professor, the believer and the atheist, all live under the same preaching sky. This is what theologians call *general revelation*: God has not left Himself without a witness anywhere. No one will be able to say they never heard a sermon.

**The sun's course (19:4–6).** David's eye fixes on the greatest preacher in the sky: the sun, for whom God has set a tent in the heavens. "In them he has set a tent for the sun, which comes out like a bridegroom leaving his chamber, and, like a strong man, runs its course with joy." The sun rises like a bridegroom — radiant, glad, dressed for the day — and runs the sky like an athlete, from one end of heaven to the other, "and there is nothing hidden from its heat." Nothing escapes the sun's circuit or its warmth. The picture quietly prepares us for the greater Sun: Malachi will promise that "the sun of righteousness shall rise with healing in its wings," and Jesus — the Light of the world, the bright morning star — runs a course that leaves nothing and no one outside the reach of His light and heat, whether to warm or to expose.

**The law of the LORD is perfect (19:7–11).** Then, without warning, the psalm turns from God's works to God's words — from the book of nature to the book of Scripture — and the praise intensifies:

> "The law of the LORD is perfect, reviving the soul; the testimony of the LORD is sure, making wise the simple; the precepts of the LORD are right, rejoicing the heart; the commandment of the LORD is pure, enlightening the eyes." — Psalm 19:7–8 (ESV)

Six names for God's Word, six perfections, six effects. The Word revives the soul — it brings the deadened heart back to life. It makes the simple wise — the uneducated man with an open Bible can become wiser than the scholar without one. It rejoices the heart and enlightens the eyes. It is "more to be desired… than gold, even much fine gold; sweeter also than honey and drippings of the honeycomb." And it is practical: "Moreover, by them is your servant warned; in keeping them there is great reward." God's Word is not merely true; it is *good* — good for the soul the way food is good for the body, good the way light is good for the eyes. The heavens tell us God is glorious; the Word tells us who He is, what He has done, and how to live. We need both books, but only one of them can revive a soul.

**Who can discern his errors? (19:12–14).** And then the psalm does something remarkable: having praised the perfect Word, David turns it on himself — and it searches him. "Who can discern his errors? Declare me innocent from hidden faults. Keep back your servant also from presumptuous sins; let them not have dominion over me!" The Word that enlightens the eyes shows a man the sins he could not see: hidden faults, and worse, presumptuous sins — deliberate, high-handed rebellion. David prays to be kept from their *dominion* — sin as a ruling power is what he fears most. And the psalm closes with the prayer that has closed a million sermons and quiet times since:

> "Let the words of my mouth and the meditation of my heart be acceptable in your sight, O LORD, my rock and my redeemer." — Psalm 19:14 (ESV)

From the glory of the galaxies to the meditation of one man's heart — that is the sweep of Psalm 19. And the last word is *Redeemer*. The God who made the heavens and gave the law is the same God who buys back sinners. David knew him by that name. We know the price he paid.

### Key verses

> "The heavens declare the glory of God, and the sky above proclaims his handiwork." — Psalm 19:1 (ESV)

> "The law of the LORD is perfect, reviving the soul; the testimony of the LORD is sure, making wise the simple." — Psalm 19:7 (ESV)

> "More to be desired are they than gold, even much fine gold; sweeter also than honey and drippings of the honeycomb." — Psalm 19:10 (ESV)

> "Let the words of my mouth and the meditation of my heart be acceptable in your sight, O LORD, my rock and my redeemer." — Psalm 19:14 (ESV)

### The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Psalm 19 is twofold, and it leaves no one out. First, the heavens have been preaching at us our whole lives, and we have suppressed what we heard — no one is an innocent bystander before a silent God. Second, the Word that is perfect shows us that we are not: it uncovers hidden faults we could not discern and presumptuous sins we discerned all too well, and under its light our words and meditations are not acceptable in God's sight. But the good news is that the last word of the psalm is *Redeemer* — and the Redeemer has come. Jesus Christ, the Word made flesh, lived with words and meditations perfectly acceptable to the Father, and He offers His acceptability to sinners as a gift. In Him, the God of the galaxies is not merely our Creator and Lawgiver; He is our Rock and our Redeemer, and the same Word that searches us also revives our souls.

**A note from the Reformers.** The Reformers leaned hard on Psalm 19's two books: the heavens give a knowledge of God that leaves man without excuse, but it is the written Word — perfect, sure, right, pure — that revives the soul and makes wise the simple. Calvin called Scripture the spectacles through which we learn to read the book of nature rightly; without the Word, we look at the heavens and see only weather. And Luther prized verse 14 as the right end of all theology: study that does not finish in prayer — "let the words of my mouth… be acceptable" — has not finished at all.

**Reflection question:** The psalm moves from the sky, to the Scripture, to the heart. Which of the three needs your attention most right now — noticing God's glory again, returning to His Word as sweeter than honey, or letting that Word search out one hidden fault you will confess by name today?

### Psalm 20 — Some Trust in Chariots

**Jesus in this psalm:** This is the congregation's prayer for the king on the eve of battle — "May the LORD answer you in the day of trouble!" — prayed over David, and prayed, in its fullest reach, over David's Son. The church prayed this way for Jesus without knowing it: the King went out to His battle alone, to the cross, and the Father did answer Him in the day of trouble — by raising Him. And the psalm's famous confession is the motto of every believer facing a fight: some trust in chariots and some in horses — in their own strength, money, connections, and plans — "but we trust in the name of the LORD our God." Jesus is both the King prayed for and the name trusted in.

**The key verse:**

> "Some trust in chariots and some in horses, but we trust in the name of the LORD our God." — Psalm 20:7 (ESV)

Chariots rust, horses tire, and every human strength has a limit stamped on it. The name of the LORD does not. On the day of trouble, what you trust in will be found out — choose now what will hold then.

### Psalm 21 — The King Rejoices in Your Strength

**Jesus in this psalm:** Psalm 21 is the morning after Psalm 20 — the battle is over, the king is home, and the crown is on his head. The church has always heard more than David in it: a crown of fine gold, a life asked for and given — "length of days forever and ever" — and a king whose glory is great through God's salvation. David died; this King asked for life and received forever and ever. Jesus is the King of Psalm 21: crowned not with gold only but with glory, alive forevermore, and the fountain of blessing to His people — "you make him most blessed forever; you make him glad with the joy of your presence."

**The key verse:**

> "For you meet him with rich blessings; you set a crown of fine gold upon his head." — Psalm 21:3 (ESV)

God *meets* His King with blessings — He comes out to greet Him. The crown of thorns came first, but it was not the last crown. The Father Himself has done the crowning, and no one can uncrown whom God has crowned.

## Psalm 22 — My God, My God: The Psalm of the Cross

### Jesus in this psalm

Jesus is not merely *in* Psalm 22 — Psalm 22 is, in a way no other chapter of the Old Testament quite matches, *about* Him, spoken from inside His own suffering. He quoted its first line from the cross as His own prayer: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Matthew 27:46). The psalm describes, a thousand years before crucifixion existed in Israel, the pierced hands and feet, the divided garments and the casting of lots, the mockers wagging their heads and quoting the very taunt the priests would use at the cross: "He trusts in the LORD; let him deliver him." The writer to the Hebrews puts this psalm's later verse on Jesus' lips: "I will tell of your name to my brothers." When you read Psalm 22, you are reading the cross from the inside — the suffering of Christ, and then, astonishingly, His triumph, in His own words before He ever spoke them aloud.

### Commentary

**The cry of forsakenness (22:1–2).** The psalm opens with the most desolate sentence ever prayed:

> "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me? Why are you so far from saving me, from the words of my groaning?" — Psalm 22:1 (ESV)

David prayed this out of his own forsaken-feeling seasons, and every sufferer since has found their voice in it. But on Good Friday the words found their true Speaker. Hanging in the darkness, bearing the sin of the world, Jesus took this cry as His own — and notice: even in the forsakenness, He says "*My* God." Twice. Faith at its lowest ebb still clings with personal pronouns. The cry is a question, but it is a question addressed *to God*, not away from Him. That is the difference between despair and lament: despair stops praying; lament prays the worst thing it feels, to the God it still calls *mine*.

**Yet you are holy (22:3–5).** And then — verse 3 turns on a hinge: "Yet you are holy, enthroned on the praises of Israel." The sufferer argues with God from God's own history: "In you our fathers trusted; they trusted, and you delivered them. To you they cried and were rescued; in you they trusted and were not put to shame." This is how faith fights in the dark: it recites. It drags the record of God's past faithfulness into the courtroom of present pain and lays it before Him. Our fathers trusted and were not put to shame — the argument is that God has a reputation to uphold, a history of hearing, and the sufferer is standing in that line.

**Scorned by mankind (22:6–8).** "But I am a worm and not a man, scorned by mankind and despised by the people." A worm — the crushed thing underfoot. The mockery is described with an accuracy that reads like a report from Golgotha: "All who see me mock me; they make mouths at me; they wag their heads; 'He trusts in the LORD; let him deliver him; let him rescue him, for he delights in him!'" Matthew records the chief priests saying nearly those exact words at the cross (Matthew 27:43). The taunt is always the same, at every cross and in every suffering: if God loved you, you would not be here. The psalm answers that lie not with an argument but with the rest of the psalm — keep reading; the delight of God and the forsakenness of the sufferer are, for a season, both true at once.

**Surrounded, pierced, stripped (22:12–18).** The middle of the psalm is the cross in slow motion. Strong bulls of Bashan surround him; lions tear and roar; "I am poured out like water, and all my bones are out of joint; my heart is like wax; it is melted within my breast; my strength is dried up like a potsherd, and my tongue sticks to my jaws; you lay me in the dust of death." And then the verses that stop every reader:

> "For dogs encompass me; a company of evildoers encircles me; they have pierced my hands and my feet — I can count all my bones — they stare and gloat over me; they divide my garments among them, and for my clothing they cast lots." — Psalm 22:16–18 (ESV)

Pierced hands and feet. Garments divided. Lots cast for clothing — John tells us the soldiers did this at the cross "so that the Scripture might be fulfilled" (John 19:24). David had enemies, but David's hands were never pierced and his garments never gambled away at his execution. The psalm overshoots David entirely and lands, with terrible precision, on his Son. This is why Jesus could quote its first line and mean the whole psalm: He knew how it ended, and He was praying His way through His own story.

**The turn (22:19–24).** "But you, O LORD, do not be far off! O you my help, come quickly to my aid!" The prayer keeps pleading — deliver my soul from the sword, my precious life from the power of the dog — and then, in the middle of verse 21, the whole psalm turns on a word: "You have rescued me from the horns of the wild oxen!" Past tense. Heard. Answered. The sufferer who began forsaken now stands delivered, and his first act is worship with his brothers: "I will tell of your name to my brothers; in the midst of the congregation I will praise you." Hebrews 2:12 puts that sentence in the mouth of the risen Jesus, singing praise in the midst of His gathered church. The Forsaken One now leads the worship of the people He died forsaken to save. And verse 24 gives the theology of the whole psalm in one line:

> "For he has not despised or abhorred the affliction of the afflicted, and he has not hidden his face from him, but has heard, when he cried to him." — Psalm 22:24 (ESV)

He has *not* hidden His face. The forsakenness was real — and it was not final, and it was not rejection. God heard. He always hears. The cross looked like the Father turning away forever; Easter morning revealed that He had been listening the whole time.

**The ends of the earth (22:25–31).** The psalm ends in a vision as wide as the world: "All the ends of the earth shall remember and turn to the LORD, and all the families of the nations shall worship before you." The afflicted shall eat and be satisfied; the prosperous and the dying alike bow; posterity shall serve Him; "they shall come and proclaim his righteousness to a people yet unborn, that he has done it." *He has done it* — the last phrase of the psalm. Students of the cross have long heard in it an echo of Jesus' final word: "It is finished." The psalm that begins with the cry of forsakenness ends with the proclamation of completion, to a people yet unborn. That includes the people reading this page. We are the posterity Psalm 22 was talking about.

### Key verses

> "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me? Why are you so far from saving me, from the words of my groaning?" — Psalm 22:1 (ESV)

> "Yet you are holy, enthroned on the praises of Israel. In you our fathers trusted; they trusted, and you delivered them." — Psalm 22:3–4 (ESV)

> "They divide my garments among them, and for my clothing they cast lots." — Psalm 22:18 (ESV)

> "For he has not despised or abhorred the affliction of the afflicted, and he has not hidden his face from him, but has heard, when he cried to him." — Psalm 22:24 (ESV)

> "They shall come and proclaim his righteousness to a people yet unborn, that he has done it." — Psalm 22:31 (ESV)

### The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Psalm 22 is that sin costs exactly this. The forsakenness Jesus cried out under was not theater; it was the real experience of bearing the curse of God against human sin — our sin — in His own body and soul. Left to ourselves, that forsakenness is where we were headed: separated from God, mocked in our need, laid in the dust of death with no one to hear us cry. But the good news is that Jesus has been there in our place — truly forsaken, so that we never will be. The Father did not finally hide His face from Him, and He will never hide His face from anyone who is in Him. Your worst night is not God's absence; the sufferer of Psalm 22 is now the risen Lord who leads the congregation's praise, and He has not despised or abhorred your affliction. He has heard, when you cried to Him. He has done it — and what He has done covers you.

**A note from the Reformers.** Spurgeon called Psalm 22 a picture of the cross "photographed" by the Holy Spirit a thousand years before it happened, and he warned preachers to take off their shoes here — this is holy ground, the Holy of Holies of the Psalter. Luther, in his darkest season, meditated on this psalm's turn: the same mouth that cried "why have you forsaken me" ends by proclaiming God's righteousness to a people yet unborn — proof, he said, that the cry of dereliction was never unbelief, but faith praying from inside the judgment it would conquer. The Reformers read the psalm whole: cross and congregation, forsakenness and "he has done it," law and gospel in a single prayer.

**Reflection question:** When have you prayed verse 1 — "why have you forsaken me?" — and stopped reading there? What would it mean for you to pray the whole psalm this week: to recite God's past faithfulness like verse 4, to plead like verse 19, and to say verse 24 over your own affliction — "he has not hidden his face from me, but has heard, when I cried to him"?

## Psalm 23 — The LORD Is My Shepherd

### Jesus in this psalm

Jesus is the Shepherd of Psalm 23 — He said so: "I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep" (John 10:11). Every line of this psalm is a line about Him. He is the Shepherd who provides ("I shall not want"), who leads beside still waters and restores souls, who guides in paths of righteousness for His name's sake. He is the Companion in the valley of the shadow of death — and He is the only Shepherd who has been through that valley Himself, all the way to its end, and come back. He is the Host who prepares a table in the presence of enemies and anoints the head with oil. And the goodness and mercy that follow the psalmist all the days of his life are not abstractions: they have a name and a face. The sheep of Psalm 23 are safe not because the valley is empty, but because the Shepherd is Jesus.

### Commentary

**I shall not want (23:1).** The most beloved sentence in the Psalter is only six words in English: "The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want." Everything in the psalm flows from the first four: *The LORD is my shepherd.* Not "the LORD is a shepherd," or "the LORD is the shepherd of the important people" — *my* shepherd. David, who had been a shepherd himself and knew exactly what sheep are — helpless, wandering, unable to find pasture or water or safety on their own — takes the low place gladly: I am a sheep; He is the shepherd. And the conclusion follows like sunrise: "I shall not want." Not "I have everything I ever desired," but "I lack nothing I truly need" — because the Shepherd sees to it. Want, in this psalm, is not abolished by wealth. It is abolished by a Person.

**He makes me lie down (23:2–3).** "He makes me lie down in green pastures. He leads me beside still waters. He restores my soul." Sheep will not lie down if they are hungry, frightened, or tormented; the Shepherd removes every reason for unrest and then *makes* them rest — sometimes the first mercy a driven, anxious soul receives is being made to stop. The waters are *still* waters — sheep fear rushing water and will not drink from it; the Shepherd knows His sheep's fears and leads them to water they can drink from without terror. And "He restores my soul" — He brings back the wandering inner life, revives the exhausted one, returns the straying one. The Hebrew picture is of a sheep carried back. "He leads me in paths of righteousness for his name's sake" — the Shepherd's guidance is not random kindness; His own reputation is staked on bringing His sheep through on the right paths. He leads well because of who He is.

**The valley (23:4).** Then the psalm walks into the dark:

> "Even though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil, for you are with me; your rod and your staff, they comfort me." — Psalm 23:4 (ESV)

Notice the honesty: the path of righteousness goes *through* the valley of the shadow of death. The Shepherd does not route His sheep around every valley; He accompanies them through this one. Notice, too, the grammar of faith: in the bright verses David talks *about* the Shepherd — "he makes me, he leads me" — but in the valley he talks *to* Him: "for *you* are with me." Trouble turns theology into prayer. And the comfort is not that the valley is harmless — it is a valley of death's shadow, and the shadow is real — the comfort is presence: rod for the wolves, staff for the wandering, and the Shepherd Himself between the sheep and every evil. A shadow cannot bite. And this Shepherd has already died in that valley and risen, so He does not merely point the way through; He has blazed it.

**The table (23:5).** The picture changes from Shepherd to Host, and the mercy gets bolder: "You prepare a table before me in the presence of my enemies; you anoint my head with oil; my cup overflows." Not after the enemies are gone — *in the presence of* them. God does not wait for your circumstances to clear before He feeds you. The table is spread within sight of everything that threatens you, and the mood at the table is not survival but honor: the anointed head, the overflowing cup. This is God's answer to the scarcity the valley preached. The Shepherd who walked you through the dark now seats you at a feast in front of your foes, and the foes get to watch you eat.

**Goodness and mercy shall follow me (23:6).** The psalm ends looking backward and forward at once:

> "Surely goodness and mercy shall follow me all the days of my life, and I shall dwell in the house of the LORD forever." — Psalm 23:6 (ESV)

"Follow" is too tame a word — the Hebrew verb is the one used for pursuing, chasing down. Goodness and mercy are not trailing behind the believer like a reluctant escort; they are hunting him, every day of his life, and they will catch him every time. And the end of the road is not the valley and not the table but the house: "I shall dwell in the house of the LORD forever." The sheep ends as a resident — not a visitor in God's house, not a guest on a pass, but a dweller, forever. Six verses, and they carry a whole life: want abolished, rest given, soul restored, valley traversed, table spread, mercy in pursuit, and home at the end. No wonder this is the psalm the church reads at bedsides and gravesides. It fits both, because the Shepherd stands in both.

### Key verses

> "The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want." — Psalm 23:1 (ESV)

> "He restores my soul. He leads me in paths of righteousness for his name's sake." — Psalm 23:3 (ESV)

> "Even though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil, for you are with me; your rod and your staff, they comfort me." — Psalm 23:4 (ESV)

> "You prepare a table before me in the presence of my enemies; you anoint my head with oil; my cup overflows." — Psalm 23:5 (ESV)

> "Surely goodness and mercy shall follow me all the days of my life, and I shall dwell in the house of the LORD forever." — Psalm 23:6 (ESV)

### The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Psalm 23 is written between its lines: we are sheep. Sheep stray, sheep panic, sheep cannot find pasture, cannot defend themselves, cannot survive the valley alone — and left to ourselves, that is our whole story: wandering from the Shepherd, wanting everything except Him, and headed through the valley of the shadow of death with no one beside us. But the good news is that the LORD is a shepherd who comes looking. Jesus Christ laid down His life for the sheep — the Shepherd died for the flock, in the valley, under the shadow — and rose to lead them still. If you are His, you shall not want, not finally; He is with you in the valley, not merely watching it; His goodness and mercy are in pursuit of you today, on purpose; and the road ends in His house, forever. The psalm is six verses long because comfort does not need to be long. It needs to be true.

**A note from the Reformers.** Spurgeon said Psalm 23 has been "the death-song of many a saint" and "the life-song of many more," and he pointed preachers to its logic: the psalm's confidence is not in the sheep's grip on the Shepherd but in the Shepherd's grip on the sheep — "I shall not want" because *He* is what He is. Calvin likewise stressed the word *my*: faith, he taught, is not merely believing that God is a shepherd to others, but being able to say, with David, that He is shepherd to *me*. That personal pronoun, laid hold of by faith, is *sola fide* set to music.

**Reflection question:** Which line of this psalm is the one your life is arguing with right now — "I shall not want," "I will fear no evil," or "goodness and mercy shall follow me"? Take that line to the Shepherd in prayer this week, honestly: tell Him where it feels untrue, and ask Him to make it true in your sight as it already is in His keeping.

### Psalm 24 — The King of Glory

**Jesus in this psalm:** This is the ascension psalm. It begins with ownership — "The earth is the LORD's and the fullness thereof" — asks who may ascend the hill of the LORD (the clean-handed and pure-hearted), and ends with the gates of the city flung open for a King: "Lift up your heads, O gates! And be lifted up, O ancient doors, that the King of glory may come in." The church has heard in those closing verses the risen Christ ascending into heaven, the ancient doors of glory opening to a Man — the crucified and risen Jesus, "the LORD, strong and mighty, the LORD, mighty in battle," coming home victorious. Who is this King of glory? The psalm answers twice, so no one misses it: the LORD of hosts, He is the King of glory — and His name is Jesus.

**The key verse:**

> "Lift up your heads, O gates! And be lifted up, O ancient doors, that the King of glory may come in." — Psalm 24:7 (ESV)

Gates that had never opened to a son of Adam opened to Him. And because the King of glory has gone in, the way stands open for everyone who comes in His name and with His clean hands reckoned to them.

### Psalm 25 — Teach Me Your Paths

**Jesus in this psalm:** David's great teach-me psalm — an acrostic prayer of a man who wants, more than rescue, to be *led*: "Make me to know your ways, O LORD; teach me your paths." It holds shame and hope together — "let me not be put to shame" alongside "for you I wait all the day long" — and it grounds everything in God's character: the LORD is good and upright, He instructs sinners in the way, He leads the humble and teaches them. Jesus is the Teacher this psalm asks for: the One who said "learn from me, for I am gentle and lowly in heart," who is Himself the Way the psalm wants to be taught. And Jesus is the ground of its confidence — God pardons guilt, however great, "for your name's sake" — and that name is now Jesus.

**The key verse:**

> "Lead me in your truth and teach me, for you are the God of my salvation; for you I wait all the day long." — Psalm 25:5 (ESV)

Waiting is not wasted time in this psalm; it is the classroom. The man who waits on God all the day long is being taught all the day long — in the waiting itself.

---

### Psalm 26 — Vindicate Me, O LORD

**Jesus in this psalm:** David asks God to test him, try him, and vindicate him, because he has walked in integrity and refused the company of hypocrites, evildoers, and the wicked. Prayed by itself, this psalm is a high-wire act — none of us survives a full audit. But it finds its home in Christ two ways. First, Jesus prayed it truly: He alone walked in perfect integrity, hated the assembly of evildoers while eating with sinners to save them, and was vindicated by God in the resurrection when men condemned Him. Second, believers pray it in Him — not claiming perfection, but appealing, from inside a sincere (if stumbling) walk, for God to distinguish them from the wickedness they have renounced: "I love the habitation of your house."

**The key verse:**

> "O LORD, I love the habitation of your house and the place where your glory dwells." — Psalm 26:8 (ESV)

Here is the test David offers for himself: not his record, but his loves. A man may be known by what he loves. To love the place where God's glory dwells is to be, whatever else is true, facing the right direction.

## Psalm 27 — The LORD Is My Light and My Salvation

### Jesus in this psalm

Jesus is the light of Psalm 27 — He claimed the title outright: "I am the light of the world. Whoever follows me will not walk in darkness, but will have the light of life" (John 8:12). He is also the beauty of the LORD that David longs to gaze upon in the temple; in Christ, the glory David glimpsed in the sanctuary has come in the flesh, and we behold it with unveiled faces (2 Corinthians 3:18). And Jesus prayed this psalm's darkest verse from inside it: "If my father and mother forsake me" — the Son was forsaken by friends, disciples, and (in the cry of Psalm 22) seemingly by the Father Himself, yet He committed Himself to the God who takes the forsaken in. The confidence of Psalm 27 — "I believe that I shall look upon the goodness of the LORD in the land of the living" — is resurrection confidence, and Jesus is its ground: because He lives, the land of the living is not a metaphor. It is an address, and His people are headed there.

### Commentary

**Whom shall I fear? (27:1–3).** The psalm opens with one of the great battle-cries of faith:

> "The LORD is my light and my salvation; whom shall I fear? The LORD is the stronghold of my life; of whom shall I be afraid?" — Psalm 27:1 (ESV)

Light, salvation, stronghold — three names, and two questions that answer themselves. When evildoers assail me, when an army encamps against me, when war rises — "my heart shall not fear; though war arise against me, yet I will be confident." This is not the confidence of a man who has never seen an army. David wrote lines like this with armies in view. The confidence is not in the size of his resources but in the identity of his God. Fear asks, "What if they come?" Faith asks a better question: "Who is my light?" Get the first verse settled, and the rest of the psalm — and much of life — falls into place.

**One thing (27:4–6).** And now the psalm reveals the secret of that fearlessness. It is not a technique. It is a desire, narrowed to a point:

> "One thing have I asked of the LORD, that will I seek after: that I may dwell in the house of the LORD all the days of my life, to gaze upon the beauty of the LORD and to inquire in his temple." — Psalm 27:4 (ESV)

One thing. A man surrounded by enemies, and his one request is not first for deliverance but for *presence* — to dwell where God dwells, to gaze on His beauty, to ask Him questions in His temple. This is the ordering of loves that makes a person unafraid: when God Himself is the one thing, enemies can only threaten the other things. "For he will hide me in his shelter in the day of trouble; he will conceal me under the cover of his tent; he will lift me high upon a rock." And the result of being hidden is a head lifted up above the enemies — and singing: "I will offer in his tent sacrifices with shouts of joy; I will sing and make melody to the LORD." The man who seeks one thing ends up shouting. Worship is fear's funeral.

**Seek my face (27:7–12).** The second half of the psalm is rougher — the shine wears into pleading. "Hear, O LORD, when I cry aloud; be gracious to me and answer me!" And then one of the tenderest exchanges in the Bible: "You have said, 'Seek my face.' My heart says to you, 'Your face, LORD, do I seek.'" God commands what the heart, awakened by grace, wants to do anyway. But the trouble is real: false witnesses have risen, breathing out violence, and David prays, "Give me not up to the will of my adversaries." In the middle stands the verse every forsaken person has underlined: "For if my father and mother forsake me, the LORD will take me in." The deepest human bonds can break — David knew family betrayal from the inside — and behind them stands a God who takes in the forsaken. Takes in: the picture is of a child gathered into arms, adopted into a house not his own by birth. That is what God does. That is, in Christ, what God has done for every believer.

**Wait for the LORD (27:13–14).** The psalm lands on a confession and a command:

> "I believe that I shall look upon the goodness of the LORD in the land of the living! Wait for the LORD; be strong, and let your heart take courage; wait for the LORD!" — Psalm 27:13–14 (ESV)

"I believe that I shall look" — future tense, present faith. Not "I might," not "I hope the odds are good": *I believe that I shall*. And the land of the living is this world and the next — God's goodness is not reserved for heaven only, though it is guaranteed there. Then the command, spoken by David to his own heart and to ours: wait. Twice. Waiting is the psalm's final word because waiting is so often faith's final exam — be strong, let your heart take courage, and wait for the LORD. The God who was light in verse 1 is worth waiting for in verse 14. He has never once been late to keep an appointment He made.

### Key verses

> "The LORD is my light and my salvation; whom shall I fear? The LORD is the stronghold of my life; of whom shall I be afraid?" — Psalm 27:1 (ESV)

> "One thing have I asked of the LORD, that will I seek after: that I may dwell in the house of the LORD all the days of my life, to gaze upon the beauty of the LORD and to inquire in his temple." — Psalm 27:4 (ESV)

> "For if my father and mother forsake me, the LORD will take me in." — Psalm 27:10 (ESV)

> "I believe that I shall look upon the goodness of the LORD in the land of the living!" — Psalm 27:13 (ESV)

> "Wait for the LORD; be strong, and let your heart take courage; wait for the LORD!" — Psalm 27:14 (ESV)

### The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Psalm 27 is that the threats are real and the forsaking is real. Armies do encamp; adversaries do breathe out violence; fathers and mothers do forsake — and so do friends, and sometimes our own hearts forsake us. Left to ourselves, we have no light for that darkness, no stronghold that men cannot breach, and no guarantee that we will see goodness again in the land of the living. But the good news is that the LORD has made Himself all three: light, salvation, stronghold. Jesus Christ entered our forsakenness — abandoned by His own, delivered to false witnesses, crucified outside the gate — so that God could say to every forsaken sinner who comes to Him: I will take you in. And He has. In Christ you are taken in, hidden in the day of trouble, and promised a sight of God's goodness that death itself cannot cancel. So wait for the LORD. The waiting is not empty. He is coming, and He is worth it.

**A note from the Reformers.** Calvin prized Psalm 27 as a pattern of faith's warfare: David, he observed, does not deny the armies but out-looks them — he sets the light of God's countenance against the darkness of his circumstances and judges by the light. And on verse 4, the Reformers together pressed the ordering of desire: the "one thing" is the soul's great simplification — when God is sought first and most, fears lose their rank. Spurgeon called this psalm a charm against fear for the people of God, and noted that its final command is not "work" or "run" but "wait" — the hardest labor of faith is often stillness before a God who has never failed to arrive.

**Reflection question:** What is your "one thing" — honestly, by the evidence of your time and your worries? And what would change this week if David's one thing became yours: to dwell near God, gaze on His beauty, and let every fear be re-ranked under that single desire?

### Psalm 28 — The LORD Is My Strength and My Shield

**Jesus in this psalm:** David cries to God as his Rock, begging not to be dragged off with the wicked — men who speak peace with their neighbors while evil is in their hearts — and then the psalm turns, mid-prayer, into thanksgiving: "Blessed be the LORD! For he has heard the voice of my pleas for mercy." Jesus prayed this pattern at the tomb of Lazarus — "Father, I thank you that you have heard me" — and He is the greater answer to its closing prayer: "Oh, save your people and bless your heritage! Be their shepherd and carry them forever." The Shepherd who carries His people forever is Jesus, and the strength and shield of this psalm are found, finally, in Him.

**The key verse:**

> "The LORD is my strength and my shield; in him my heart trusts, and I am helped; my heart exults, and with my song I give thanks to him." — Psalm 28:7 (ESV)

Trust, help, exulting, song — that is the order of the Christian life in one verse. The song is not the cause of the help; it is the result. And sometimes, by faith, we sing the result before we see it.

### Psalm 29 — The Voice of the LORD

**Jesus in this psalm:** Seven times the voice of the LORD thunders over the waters, breaks the cedars, shakes the wilderness, and strips the forests bare — while the heavenly court cries "Glory!" Jesus is that voice made flesh: the Word of God in person. At His baptism the voice of the LORD came over the waters and named Him Son; at His word, storms on Galilee fell silent — the same voice that shakes the wilderness, calming the sea with a sentence. And the psalm's end is His gift: the LORD who sits enthroned over the flood "will give strength to his people; the LORD will bless his people with peace" — the peace Jesus left His disciples the night before the cross.

**The key verse:**

> "The LORD will give strength to his people; the LORD will bless his people with peace!" — Psalm 29:11 (ESV)

The God whose voice can break cedars chooses, toward His people, to give strength and bless with peace. The thunder is real — but for those who are His, the thunder is not the last sound. Peace is.

### Psalm 30 — Joy Comes with the Morning

**Jesus in this psalm:** David sings this at the dedication of the house, looking back on a season when God let him fall — through his own pride ("I shall never be moved") — and then lifted him from the pit. Its most famous line is the motto of every believer who has survived a night: "Weeping may tarry for the night, but joy comes with the morning." Jesus is the morning this psalm is talking about. His resurrection is the great instance of it: weeping tarried for the night of Friday and the silence of Saturday, and joy came — literally — with the morning. And because He rose, every lesser night of His people is a down payment on the same pattern: "You have turned for me my mourning into dancing."

**The key verse:**

> "For his anger is but for a moment, and his favor is for a lifetime. Weeping may tarry for the night, but joy comes with the morning." — Psalm 30:5 (ESV)

Weeping is a lodger; it tarries for a night and no longer. Joy is the homeowner, arriving with the morning. If it is night for you now, hear the psalm plainly: the night is not the whole story, and it is not the end of it.

### Psalm 31 — Into Your Hand I Commit My Spirit

**Jesus in this psalm:** Jesus died with this psalm on His lips: "Into your hand I commit my spirit" (Psalm 31:5) was His last word from the cross in Luke's Gospel. The whole psalm is a sufferer's prayer — a net hidden for him, slander, terror on every side, his life spent with sorrow and his years with sighing — and it is the prayer Jesus chose for His dying breath, adding one word of His own: "*Father*, into your hands I commit my spirit." That tells us how to pray this psalm: the sufferings in it were His first, and because He committed His spirit to the Father and was not put to shame, every believer can commit theirs the same way — in life, and at the last, in death itself.

**The key verse:**

> "Into your hand I commit my spirit; you have redeemed me, O LORD, faithful God." — Psalm 31:5 (ESV)

The safest place in the universe is the hand of God. Jesus put His spirit there from the cross, and the Father kept it — and brought Him back with it on the third day. What He kept for the Son, He will keep for the sons and daughters.

## Psalm 32 — Blessed Is the One Whose Transgression Is Forgiven

### Jesus in this psalm

Jesus is the forgiveness at the center of Psalm 32 — the reason any transgression can be forgiven, any sin covered, any iniquity not counted. Paul quotes this psalm's opening in Romans 4 as the very definition of the gospel: "Blessed are those whose lawless deeds are forgiven, and whose sins are covered; blessed is the man against whom the Lord will not count his sin." David knew the misery of unconfessed sin from the inside — this psalm stands beside Psalm 51 in the shadow of his great fall — and he knew the relief of a confession answered. But David could not have explained *how* a holy God covers sin without counting it. The cross explains it: the sin was counted — counted against Jesus, who bore it in His body on the tree — and therefore it is not counted against those who confess and trust in Him. Psalm 32 is justification by faith, sung.

### Commentary

**The blessedness of forgiveness (32:1–2).** The psalm begins where Psalm 1 began — "Blessed" — but with a deeper happiness:

> "Blessed is the one whose transgression is forgiven, whose sin is covered. Blessed is the man against whom the LORD counts no iniquity, and in whose spirit there is no deceit." — Psalm 32:1–2 (ESV)

Three words for sin — transgression (rebellion), sin (missing the mark), iniquity (crookedness) — and three words for God's dealing with it: forgiven (lifted away), covered (hidden from sight), not counted (never entered in the ledger). This is the happiest sentence a guilty person can hear: there exists a blessedness that does not depend on having no past, but on having a God who deals with the past this way. And notice the company it keeps: "in whose spirit there is no deceit" — the forgiven man is the honest man. Forgiveness and honesty belong together; you cannot have the first while clinging to the second's opposite.

**The misery of silence (32:3–4).** David tells us what it cost him to keep quiet:

> "For when I kept silent, my bones wasted away through my groaning all day long. For day and night your hand was heavy upon me; my strength was dried up as by the heat of summer." — Psalm 32:3–4 (ESV)

Unconfessed sin is not a private arrangement between a man and his conscience; it is a sickness in the bones. God's hand was heavy — that heaviness was itself a mercy, the pressure of a Father who would not let His child be comfortable in hiding. Many a believer has lived these verses: the groaning, the drying up, the exhaustion of maintaining a secret before God. David's testimony is that silence, not confession, is what destroys a man. The cover-up is always worse than the crime's confession, because the cover-up has to be carried every day, all day long.

**I said, "I will confess" (32:5).** Then comes the hinge of the psalm, one of the most important verses in the Bible about repentance:

> "I acknowledged my sin to you, and I did not cover my iniquity; I said, 'I will confess my transgressions to the LORD,' and you forgave the iniquity of my sin." — Psalm 32:5 (ESV)

Watch the speed. "I said, 'I will confess'… and you forgave." The forgiveness is not at the end of a long penance; it meets the confession at the door. David did the one thing the silent years refused to do: he acknowledged, he uncovered, he named it — "my sin," "my iniquity," "my transgressions," no excuses, no renaming, no blaming. And God did what God delights to do. This is the pattern the whole Bible repeats — "If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins" (1 John 1:9) — and it is the pattern every person in recovery learns or relearns: the way out of the pit is not hiding better. It is telling the truth to God.

**Therefore let everyone pray (32:6–7).** David turns his private mercy into public instruction: "Therefore let everyone who is godly offer prayer to you at a time when you may be found." His case becomes an invitation — what God did for this confessed sinner, He will do for any confessed sinner who comes while He may be found. And the result of forgiveness is not merely relief but refuge: "You are a hiding place for me; you preserve me from trouble; you surround me with shouts of deliverance." The man who tried to hide his sin *from* God discovers that God Himself is the only safe hiding place *for* him. Shouts of deliverance — the forgiven life is a surrounded life, ringed with singing.

**Be not like a horse or a mule (32:8–11).** God speaks in the closing section: "I will instruct you and teach you in the way you should go; I will counsel you with my eye upon you." Then the warning every stubborn heart needs: "Be not like a horse or a mule, without understanding, which must be curbed with bit and bridle, or it will not stay near you." God would rather lead us by His eye — by a look, by nearness, by a responsive heart — than by the bit of painful circumstances. "Many are the sorrows of the wicked, but steadfast love surrounds the one who trusts in the LORD." And the psalm that began with the blessedness of one forgiven man ends with a congregation rejoicing: "Be glad in the LORD, and rejoice, O righteous, and shout for joy, all you upright in heart!" Confession, honestly made, ends in a shout. It always does.

### Key verses

> "Blessed is the one whose transgression is forgiven, whose sin is covered." — Psalm 32:1 (ESV)

> "For when I kept silent, my bones wasted away through my groaning all day long." — Psalm 32:3 (ESV)

> "I acknowledged my sin to you, and I did not cover my iniquity; I said, 'I will confess my transgressions to the LORD,' and you forgave the iniquity of my sin." — Psalm 32:5 (ESV)

> "You are a hiding place for me; you preserve me from trouble; you surround me with shouts of deliverance." — Psalm 32:7 (ESV)

> "Be not like a horse or a mule, without understanding, which must be curbed with bit and bridle, or it will not stay near you." — Psalm 32:9 (ESV)

### The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Psalm 32 is that unconfessed sin does not stay quiet — it wastes the bones, dries up the strength, and turns God's fatherly hand into a weight we carry day and night. We cannot cover our iniquity and keep our health of soul; the hiding itself is the misery, and God in mercy will not let us get comfortable in it. And even our confession cannot pay for what we confess: sin must be *covered*, and we have no covering of our own. But the good news is that God has provided the covering. Jesus Christ bore the iniquity David confessed — and yours — uncovered, in the open, on the cross, so that God could forgive it, cover it, and refuse to count it against everyone who comes clean. The invitation of this psalm stands tonight: acknowledge it, do not cover it, say "I will confess" — and find that forgiveness is faster than your fear, and that God Himself becomes your hiding place.

**A note from the Reformers.** Paul made Psalm 32:1–2 a foundation stone of justification by faith in Romans 4, and the Reformers built on it accordingly: here is a blessedness pronounced not on the man who has no sin, nor on the man who pays for his sin, but on the man whose sin God *does not count* — imputation, in a psalm. Luther, who had tortured himself for years trying to confess his way to peace, found in verses like these the discovery of his life: God's forgiveness is not the wage of a sufficiently thorough confession but the gift that meets the honest one. Confess, therefore, not to earn the pardon, but because the pardon is real and the Father is waiting.

**Reflection question:** What have you been keeping silent about before God — the thing whose covering-up is costing you your strength? Will you say David's sentence today, out loud, with the thing named: "I will confess my transgressions to the LORD" — and then believe the next clause is about you: "and you forgave the iniquity of my sin"?

### Psalm 33 — The Word of the LORD Is Upright

**Jesus in this psalm:** This joyful psalm — no title, no named author, all congregation — celebrates the God whose word creates and whose counsel stands forever. "By the word of the LORD the heavens were made" — and that Word, through whom all things were made, is Jesus (John 1:1–3). The psalm warns the nations that their counsels come to nothing while the counsel of the LORD stands forever, and it ends with the church's eyes on the LORD, "our help and our shield," waiting in hope for His steadfast love. Jesus prayed in this psalm's confidence — and as the Word who spoke the heavens into being and then spoke forgiveness into sinners, He is its substance from first line to last.

**The key verse:**

> "By the word of the LORD the heavens were made, and by the breath of his mouth all their host." — Psalm 33:6 (ESV)

The God who made galaxies by speaking is the God who remakes sinners by speaking. "Let there be light" and "Your sins are forgiven" come from the same mouth, with the same authority.

### Psalm 34 — Taste and See That the LORD Is Good

**Jesus in this psalm:** David wrote this after feigning madness before Abimelech and being driven away — a humiliated fugitive praising God for deliverance. Its promises are the church's pantry: "Oh, taste and see that the LORD is good"; "The LORD is near to the brokenhearted and saves the crushed in spirit"; "Many are the afflictions of the righteous, but the LORD delivers him out of them all." And one line is pure prophecy: "He keeps all his bones; not one of them is broken" — quoted by John at the cross, where the soldiers broke the legs of the two thieves but, finding Jesus already dead, broke none of His (John 19:36). Jesus is the righteous sufferer of this psalm, delivered through death itself, and the goodness He invites us to taste is Himself.

**The key verse:**

> "Oh, taste and see that the LORD is good! Blessed is the man who takes refuge in him!" — Psalm 34:8 (ESV)

God does not ask for blind assent; He invites an experiment of the soul. Taste — come near, try Him, take Him at His word in one real trouble — and see. A God who invites testing like this is not afraid of the result.

### Psalm 35 — Contend, O LORD, with Those Who Contend with Me

**Jesus in this psalm:** This is one of the imprecatory psalms — David, betrayed and falsely accused by men he had mourned for and prayed for, calls on God to fight his battle and plead his cause. Jesus quoted this psalm's grief about Himself: "They hated me without a cause" (Psalm 35:19; John 15:25) — the hatred that met Him was causeless, repaid for love with plots. And Jesus prayed this psalm's longing for vindication not by taking revenge but by committing His cause to the Father who judges justly. In Christ, believers may pray Psalm 35 honestly — naming the wrong, asking God to act — while leaving the vengeance where David ultimately left it: in God's hands, not ours.

**The key verse:**

> "Let not those rejoice over me who are wrongfully my foes, and let not those wink the eye who hate me without cause." — Psalm 35:19 (ESV)

"Without cause" — that is the phrase Jesus took up. When you are hated without cause, you are in the company of David, and of David's Son. The cause is God's to plead, and He does not lose cases He takes up.

### Psalm 36 — Your Steadfast Love, O LORD, Extends to the Heavens

**Jesus in this psalm:** David sets two things side by side: the wicked man, whose heart has no room for the fear of God and who flatters himself that his sin will never be found out — and the steadfast love of God, which extends to the heavens, under whose wings the children of mankind take refuge and feast on the abundance of His house. Jesus is the embodiment of that steadfast love and the fountain this psalm names: "For with you is the fountain of life; in your light do we see light." He called Himself the light of the world and promised rivers of living water to those who come to Him. The self-deceived sinner of verse 2 and the feasting refugee of verse 8 are the two humanities; Jesus stands at the door of the second, holding it open.

**The key verse:**

> "How precious is your steadfast love, O God! The children of mankind take refuge in the shadow of your wings." — Psalm 36:7 (ESV)

A hen's wings are not much against a storm — unless the hen is God. Under His wings there is refuge, a feast, and a river. The psalm's word is *precious*: this love is not common, and it is not cheap.

### Psalm 37 — Fret Not Yourself Because of Evildoers

**Jesus in this psalm:** David, an old man now, wrote this for everyone tempted to envy the wicked who prosper: "Fret not yourself because of evildoers; be not envious of wrongdoers! For they will soon fade like the grass." Its most famous promise Jesus took up into the Beatitudes: "the meek shall inherit the land" becomes "Blessed are the meek, for they shall inherit the earth" (Matthew 5:5). Jesus is both the teacher of this psalm and its pattern — the truly meek One, who did not fret at the prosperity of Pilate or the triumph of His enemies, who committed His way to the LORD and waited patiently, and who now inherits the earth and shares it with the meek. In Him, "delight yourself in the LORD" stops being a strategy and becomes a description of home.

**The key verse:**

> "Delight yourself in the LORD, and he will give you the desires of your heart." — Psalm 37:4 (ESV)

This is not a vending machine; it is a transformation. The man who delights in the LORD finds his desires re-ordered until God's gifts and his heart's wants are the same list. The promise is kept by changing the desirer.

### Psalm 38 — There Is No Soundness in My Flesh

**Jesus in this psalm:** A penitential psalm of a man under God's rebuke — arrows sunk deep, no soundness in his flesh, loathsome sores, a heart that throbs, friends and loved ones standing aloof, enemies laying snares. David's sickness and sin are tangled together, and he knows it: "For my iniquities have gone over my head; like a heavy burden, they are too heavy for me." Jesus prayed the depths of this psalm representatively — forsaken by friends who stood aloof from His plague, bearing a burden of iniquity not His own that went over His head like a flood — so that guilty sufferers could pray it and be answered: "O Lord, all my longing is before you; my sighing is not hidden from you." The sighing of God's people is not hidden. It never has been.

**The key verse:**

> "But for you, O LORD, do I wait; it is you, O Lord my God, who will answer." — Psalm 38:15 (ESV)

When a man has no defense, no health, and no friends left close, waiting becomes his whole theology — and it is enough, because of who he is waiting for. The answer may tarry. The Answerer will not fail.

### Psalm 39 — What Is the Measure of My Days?

**Jesus in this psalm:** David, muzzled before the wicked and burning within, finally asks the question underneath all our hurry: "O LORD, make me know my end and what is the measure of my days; let me know how fleeting I am!" Man is a mere breath; he heaps up wealth and does not know who will gather it. Jesus taught this psalm's wisdom in parables — the rich fool who heaped up and heard, "This night your soul is required of you" — and He lived its hope: "And now, O Lord, for what do I wait? My hope is in you." In Christ, the brevity of life is not a terror but a tutor: it teaches us where to put the hope that a breath-length life cannot hold.

**The key verse:**

> "And now, O Lord, for what do I wait? My hope is in you." — Psalm 39:7 (ESV)

Seven verses of wrestling collapse into one settled sentence. When everything fleeting has been honestly faced, hope does not disappear — it concentrates. It lands, entirely, on a Person.

## Psalm 40 — I Waited Patiently for the LORD: Sacrifice and Offering You Have Not Desired

### Jesus in this psalm

Jesus speaks in Psalm 40 in the first person — the writer to the Hebrews says so outright. Quoting this psalm's center — "Sacrifice and offering you have not desired, but a body have you prepared for me… Behold, I have come to do your will, O God" — Hebrews 10 tells us: "he said [this]… as he speaks of [Christ]" coming into the world. The psalm's opening is the testimony of a man pulled from a miry pit and set on a rock, singing a new song; its center is the Son volunteering for the incarnation and the cross; its close is a sufferer crying, "Do not delay, O my God." Testimony, incarnation, and passion in one psalm — and the Speaker who unites them is Jesus, who waited patiently in the pit of death and was raised to sing the new song in the great congregation.

### Commentary

**He drew me up from the pit (40:1–3).** The psalm opens with testimony — the kind a man gives when he has survived something:

> "I waited patiently for the LORD; he inclined to me and heard my cry. He drew me up from the pit of destruction, out of the miry bog, and set my feet upon a rock, making my steps secure." — Psalm 40:1–2 (ESV)

Waiting *patiently* — the Hebrew is an intensified waiting, waiting and waiting. And the picture of rescue is exact: a pit, a bog — mud that holds a man by the feet and takes him down an inch at a time — and a hand that draws him *up*, sets his feet on rock, and makes his steps secure. This is the testimony underneath every conversion and every recovery: I could not climb out; I was drawn out. And the result is a song: "He put a new song in my mouth, a song of praise to our God. Many will see and fear, and put their trust in the LORD." A rescued man becomes a sermon. God puts the song in; the seeing and trusting of others follows.

**Blessed is the man who makes the LORD his trust (40:4–5).** "Blessed is the man who makes the LORD his trust, who does not turn to the proud, to those who go astray after a lie!" David marvels at the multiplication of God's works: "You have multiplied, O LORD my God, your wondrous deeds and your thoughts toward us; none can compare with you! I will proclaim and tell of them, yet they are more than can be told." Thoughts toward us — God's mind is full of His people, and His deeds outrun our counting. This is the settled confidence out of which the psalm's great center now speaks.

**A body you have prepared for me (40:6–8).** Now the psalm rises from testimony to prophecy, and the Speaker changes. David wrote the words, but the words are greater than David:

> "In sacrifice and offering you have not delighted, but you have given me an open ear. Burnt offering and sin offering you have not required. Then I said, 'Behold, I have come; in the scroll of the book it is written of me: I delight to do your will, O my God; your law is within my heart.'" — Psalm 40:6–8 (ESV)

The Old Testament's whole sacrificial system is here declared, by God Himself, to be insufficient in itself: bulls and goats were never the point; they were shadows pointing at a Person. What God wanted was a willing Man — one with an open, obedient ear, one who would *delight* to do God's will with the law written inside His heart — and in the fullness of time that Man came. "Behold, I have come" is the Son's own word at the incarnation, and Hebrews drives it home: by that will — by the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all — "we have been sanctified." The delight matters: Jesus did not merely endure the Father's will. He delighted in it, all the way to the cross, for the joy set before Him.

**I have told the glad news (40:9–10).** The Speaker has a congregation and a message: "I have told the glad news of deliverance in the great congregation; behold, I have not restrained my lips… I have not hidden your deliverance within my heart; I have spoken of your faithfulness and your salvation." This is Jesus the Preacher — and it is also the pattern of every delivered person: deliverance hidden is deliverance wasted. The man of verse 2, drawn from the bog, becomes by verse 9 a herald in the congregation. That is the order of the Christian life: pit, rock, song, witness.

**Be pleased to deliver me (40:11–17).** And then — because this psalm is honest about how life actually goes — the delivered man is in trouble again: "For evils have encompassed me beyond number; my iniquities have overtaken me, and I cannot see." The new song did not exempt him from new sorrows. The psalm closes not with triumph but with a cry the church has prayed ever since: "As for me, I am poor and needy, but the Lord takes thought for me. You are my help and my deliverer; do not delay, O my God!" Poor and needy — and *taken thought for* by the Lord of Psalm 40:5, whose thoughts toward us cannot be numbered. That is not a contradiction. That is the whole Christian life in two lines: our poverty, and His thoughts.

### Key verses

> "He drew me up from the pit of destruction, out of the miry bog, and set my feet upon a rock, making my steps secure." — Psalm 40:2 (ESV)

> "He put a new song in my mouth, a song of praise to our God. Many will see and fear, and put their trust in the LORD." — Psalm 40:3 (ESV)

> "Then I said, 'Behold, I have come; in the scroll of the book it is written of me: I delight to do your will, O my God; your law is within my heart.'" — Psalm 40:7–8 (ESV)

> "As for me, I am poor and needy, but the Lord takes thought for me. You are my help and my deliverer; do not delay, O my God!" — Psalm 40:17 (ESV)

### The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Psalm 40 is that the pit is real and our feet are in the miry bog — sins beyond number, iniquities that overtake us until we cannot see — and no sacrifice we can offer will climb us out: God has never delighted in our offerings as payment, because they cannot pay. Worse, left in the bog, we do not merely suffer; we sink. But the good news is that God sent the One who said, "Behold, I have come." Jesus Christ took the body God prepared for Him, delighted to do the Father's will all the way to the cross, and offered Himself once for all — the sacrifice God actually delights in, because it actually saves. And the hand that drew David up from the pit, and raised Jesus out of a deeper one, is the same hand stretched toward you: He inclines to hear, He draws up, He sets feet on rock, and He puts a new song where the groaning was. Poor and needy you may be. Taken thought for — that you also are, today, by name.

**A note from the Reformers.** Hebrews 10 made this psalm a cornerstone of the Reformation's teaching on the finished work of Christ: if the Son has come, delighted in the Father's will, and offered His body *once for all*, then no further sacrifice for sin remains to be offered — not by priests, not by penitents, not by us. Calvin marveled at the word *delight*: Christ's obedience was not the reluctant compliance of a servant but the joyful will of a Son, and it is that willing obedience, credited to believers, that the Reformers called our righteousness. The open ear of verse 6 became, in their reading, the mark of the true servant — and the rebuke of every religion of mere ritual.

**Reflection question:** Where is your miry bog — the place you have waited and waited, and perhaps stopped expecting to be drawn up from? And will you pray this psalm's two sentences over it this week: the testimony in advance — "He drew me up… and set my feet upon a rock" — and the honesty that follows it — "I am poor and needy, but the Lord takes thought for me"?

### Psalm 41 — Blessed Is the One Who Considers the Poor

**Jesus in this psalm:** David, sick and slandered, watches even his closest friend turn against him: "Even my close friend in whom I trusted, who ate my bread, has lifted his heel against me." Jesus quoted this very verse about Judas at the Last Supper (John 13:18) — the friend who ate His bread, whose heel lifted against Him for thirty pieces of silver. But the psalm's opening is Jesus' own teaching too: "Blessed is the one who considers the poor" is the beatitude of the Good Samaritan and of Matthew 25 — and the LORD who "delivers him in the day of trouble" is the God who raised both David from his sickbed and Jesus from a worse one. Book One ends with David asking to be raised up; the gospel is the story of the Raising.

**The key verse:**

> "Even my close friend in whom I trusted, who ate my bread, has lifted his heel against me." — Psalm 41:9 (ESV)

Betrayal by a stranger is war; betrayal by a friend who ate your bread is a special kind of wound — and it is a wound Jesus knows from the inside. If you have been betrayed by someone who sat at your table, you are not telling Him anything He has not felt.

Book Two closes with Psalm 72:18–19: "Blessed be the LORD, the God of Israel, who alone does wondrous things. Blessed be his glorious name forever; may the whole earth be filled with his glory! Amen and Amen!"*

## Psalm 42 — As the Deer Pants for Water: Hope in God

### Jesus in this psalm

Jesus is the thirsty One of Psalm 42 — and the water He thirsted for, and now gives. On the cross He cried, "I thirst" (John 19:28), entering the exile this psalm describes: cut off from the house of God, taunted with "Where is your God?", drinking tears day and night. But Jesus is also the answer to the thirst: the One who stood at the feast and cried, "If anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink" (John 7:37), and who promised the woman at the well water that becomes a spring welling up to eternal life. The sons of Korah wrote this psalm out of exile from the temple; Jesus prayed its depths in His own exile from the Father's felt presence, and He rose to become the living water for every thirsty exile who hopes in God.

### Commentary

**The thirst (42:1–2).** Book Two opens with the most famous thirst in literature:

> "As a deer pants for flowing streams, so pants my soul for you, O God. My soul thirsts for God, for the living God. When shall I come and appear before God?" — Psalm 42:1–2 (ESV)

This is not casual interest in religion; it is a dying animal's need for water. The psalmist — a son of Korah, a temple singer — is far from Jerusalem, up near the sources of the Jordan, and he is cut off from the worship he loves. His thirst is for God Himself: "for the *living* God." Not for relief, not for answers, not even for deliverance first of all — for God. That ordering is the psalm's first lesson: the deepest need underneath every other need is God Himself, and no substitute stream will touch this thirst.

**Tears, taunts, and memories (42:3–4).** "My tears have been my food day and night, while they say to me all the day long, 'Where is your God?'" Tears for food — grief as a diet. And the enemy's question is the one every sufferer hears, from others and from inside: *Where is your God?* If He loved you, where is He? The psalmist's answer in verse 4 is memory: "These things I remember, as I pour out my soul: how I would go with the throng and lead them in procession to the house of God with glad shouts and songs of praise." He remembers worship — the crowd, the singing, the gladness — and the memory both wounds and steadies him. He has been somewhere real with God before. The absence he feels is the absence of a presence he has known, and that is a very different thing from never having known it at all.

**The refrain: talk to your soul (42:5).** Then the psalm does the thing it is most famous for — the sufferer stops talking to God for a moment and talks to himself:

> "Why are you cast down, O my soul, and why are you in turmoil within me? Hope in God; for I shall again praise him, my salvation and my God." — Psalm 42:5 (ESV)

This is the discipline at the heart of the whole Psalter: preaching to your own soul. The psalmist cross-examines his despair — *why* are you cast down? — and then commands his hope: Hope in God. Not "feel hopeful," but *hope*, as an act of the will, on stated grounds: "for I shall again praise him." Future tense. He does not yet praise as he did in the processions; he prophesies that he will. The refrain returns at the end of this psalm and again at the end of Psalm 43 — three times in all — because once is rarely enough. A cast-down soul usually needs to be told more than once, and the psalm gives us permission to keep telling it.

**Deep calls to deep (42:6–7).** "My soul is cast down within me; therefore I remember you from the land of Jordan and of Hermon, from Mount Mizar." Memory again — he fights distance with remembrance. And then the image that has named a thousand sermons:

> "Deep calls to deep at the roar of your waterfalls; all your breakers and your waves have gone over me." — Psalm 42:7 (ESV)

The waterfalls of Hermon, the flood-waters of chaos — wave after wave, *your* breakers, *your* waves. He is honest enough to say the waters are God's: sovereign waters, not random ones. Jonah, sinking in the sea, quoted this verse from inside the fish (Jonah 2:3). And Jesus went under these waters fully — the baptism of suffering He spoke of (Luke 12:50), the waves and billows of God's judgment passing over Him at the cross. Deep called to deep there as nowhere else: the depth of our sin calling down the depth of His suffering, and beneath both, the deeper depth of God's love.

**By day and by night (42:8–11).** "By day the LORD commands his steadfast love, and at night his song is with me, a prayer to the God of my life." Even in exile, the day has commanded love and the night has a song — the psalmist says this while still asking, in verse 9, "Why have you forgotten me? Why do I go mourning because of the oppression of the enemy?" Both are in the same psalm, verses apart. That is not contradiction; that is faith and feeling in one honest heart. The psalm ends where verse 5 ended — the refrain, a third telling on the way (Psalm 43:5 completes it): "Why are you cast down, O my soul… Hope in God; for I shall again praise him, my salvation and my God." The circumstances have not changed by the last verse. The man has. That is often the first deliverance God gives: not a new situation, but a soul that has learned to command its own hope.

### Key verses

> "As a deer pants for flowing streams, so pants my soul for you, O God." — Psalm 42:1 (ESV)

> "My tears have been my food day and night, while they say to me all the day long, 'Where is your God?'" — Psalm 42:3 (ESV)

> "Why are you cast down, O my soul, and why are you in turmoil within me? Hope in God; for I shall again praise him, my salvation and my God." — Psalm 42:5 (ESV)

> "Deep calls to deep at the roar of your waterfalls; all your breakers and your waves have gone over me." — Psalm 42:7 (ESV)

> "By day the LORD commands his steadfast love, and at night his song is with me, a prayer to the God of my life." — Psalm 42:8 (ESV)

### The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Psalm 42 is that God's people can find themselves in exile — far from the house of God, far from the felt presence of God, drinking tears and hearing "Where is your God?" from every direction, including their own hearts. The psalm does not pretend otherwise, and neither should we: distance from God, when it is felt, is one of the heaviest afflictions a believer can carry, and sin and sorrow both know the road to that far country. But the good news is that the thirst itself is a sign of life — dead men do not thirst for God — and that Jesus has been to the far country ahead of us. He thirsted, He was taunted, the waves went over Him — and He rose, and now He gives the living water He thirsted for, freely, to every exile who hopes in God. You shall again praise Him. That is not wishful thinking; it is the testimony of the risen Christ, who is your salvation and your God.

**A note from the Reformers.** Spurgeon made much of the psalmist's self-preaching: we must not permit our souls to preach to us unchallenged, he said; the believer must learn to take his own heart in hand, question its despondency, and command its hope — faith is not the absence of a cast-down soul but the refusal to let it have the last word. Calvin saw in the refrain the anatomy of the Christian's inner warfare: sense and faith disputing in one breast, and faith winning not by silencing the senses but by out-arguing them with the character of God. That is why this psalm has been the companion of depressed saints for three thousand years: it does not mock the darkness; it teaches the darkened how to talk back.

**Reflection question:** What has your soul been saying to you lately — and have you been listening to it, or preaching to it? Take the refrain as your own assignment this week: ask your soul its question out loud — "Why are you cast down?" — and then give it its orders: "Hope in God; for I shall again praise him." Which part of that sentence is hardest for you to say, and why?

### Psalm 43 — Send Out Your Light and Your Truth

**Jesus in this psalm:** Psalm 43 is the final movement of the prayer Psalms 42 began — the same refrain closes it — and its center is a prayer for guidance home: "Send out your light and your truth; let them lead me; let them bring me to your holy hill and to your dwelling!" Jesus is the light and the truth sent out from the Father — "I am the light of the world"; "I am the way, and the truth, and the life" — and He leads exiles home exactly the way this psalm asks: not by a map but by Himself. The psalm ends at the altar, with a lyre and a vow of praise: "I will praise you with the lyre, O God, my God." In Christ, the exile's road ends there — at the altar, at home, with God Himself as "my exceeding joy."

**The key verse:**

> "Send out your light and your truth; let them lead me; let them bring me to your holy hill and to your dwelling!" — Psalm 43:3 (ESV)

When you do not know the way home, you do not need more effort; you need a guide who is sent. Light to see by, truth to walk by — God sends both, and in the end He sent a Person.

### Psalm 44 — Awake! Why Are You Sleeping, O Lord?

**Jesus in this psalm:** This is the prayer of a defeated nation that cannot find its sin to explain the defeat: "All this has come upon us, though we have not forgotten you." The people plead God's past deeds — "In God we have boasted continually" — against His present silence, and cry the boldest line in the Psalter: "Awake! Why are you sleeping, O Lord?" Paul takes this psalm's darkest verse — "For your sake we are killed all the day long; we are regarded as sheep to be slaughtered" — and quotes it in Romans 8 about the church, answering it not with an explanation but with a conquest: "In all these things we are more than conquerors through him who loved us." Jesus is that Him: the slaughtered Sheep who became the conquering Shepherd, and whose love guarantees that no defeat of His people is the final chapter.

**The key verse:**

> "Rise up; come to our help! Redeem us for the sake of your steadfast love!" — Psalm 44:26 (ESV)

"For the sake of your steadfast love" — when God's people run out of arguments from their own record, they appeal to His. His steadfast love is the one ground that never gives way, even when the battlefield does.

## Psalm 45 — The Royal Wedding: Your Throne, O God, Is Forever

### Jesus in this psalm

Jesus is the King and Bridegroom of Psalm 45 — the writer to the Hebrews quotes its center directly as God's address to the Son: "Your throne, O God, is forever and ever. The scepter of uprightness is the scepter of your kingdom. You have loved righteousness and hated wickedness" (Hebrews 1:8–9). This wedding song for a king overflows its original occasion entirely: the King is addressed as *God*, anointed with the oil of gladness above His companions, riding out in splendor for the cause of truth and meekness and righteousness, with a bride in gold of Ophir at His right hand. The church has always read it as the wedding song of Christ and His bride — the marriage supper toward which all of history is moving, when the King who loved righteousness takes home the people He redeemed.

### Commentary

**The King's beauty (45:1–2).** The psalmist begins like a man whose heart cannot hold it: "My heart overflows with a pleasing theme; I address my verses to the king; my tongue is like the pen of a ready scribe." Overflow — that is the right start for a wedding song about this King. And the theme is the King's beauty: "You are the most handsome of the sons of men; grace is poured upon your lips; therefore God has blessed you forever." Handsome — the King is not merely powerful but *beautiful*, and His beauty is crowned by His speech: grace poured on His lips. Everyone who heard Jesus said the same: "No one ever spoke like this man." The congregation that sings this psalm is not admiring a distant monarch; it is falling in love with its Bridegroom.

**The King's sword and reign (45:3–7).** "Gird your sword on your thigh, O mighty one, in your splendor and majesty! In your majesty ride out victoriously for the cause of truth and meekness and righteousness." Truth, meekness, righteousness — an unlikely war-cry, and the exact description of Christ's conquest: He rode out not with legions but with truth on His lips, meekness in His manner, and righteousness in His cause, and His arrows — the words of the gospel — fell sharp in the hearts of His enemies, turning rebels into subjects. Then the psalm says what no merely human king could bear to have said of him:

> "Your throne, O God, is forever and ever. The scepter of uprightness is the scepter of your kingdom. You have loved righteousness and hated wickedness. Therefore God, your God, has anointed you with the oil of gladness beyond your companions." — Psalm 45:6–7 (ESV)

God, speaking to the King, calls the King *God* — and in the same breath speaks of "God, your God," who anoints Him. Father and Son, in one verse, a thousand years before Bethlehem. Hebrews 1 seizes on it as proof of the Son's deity and of the moral root of His joy: He is anointed with gladness *because* He loved righteousness and hated wickedness. The gladness of Jesus is not despite His holiness; it is the fruit of it.

**The bride (45:9–15).** And now the bride enters: "At your right hand stands the queen in gold of Ophir." The psalm turns and addresses her directly: "Hear, O daughter, and consider, and incline your ear: forget your people and your father's house, and the king will desire your beauty." Forget your father's house — the bride leaves her old life, her old name, her old country, because the King desires her beauty; and her beauty, the psalm says, is "within" — "All glorious is the princess in her chamber, with robes interwoven with gold." This is the church, and every believer in her: called out of the old life, made glorious not with borrowed finery but with a glory worked *within* — the righteousness of Christ worn as her own wedding dress. She is led to the King "with gladness and rejoicing," in a procession, with her virgin companions following her. The wedding of Psalm 45 is not a private elopement; it is a parade.

**The promise (45:16–17).** The psalm ends with sons and a name: "In place of your fathers shall be your sons; you will make them princes in all the earth. I will cause your name to be remembered in all generations; therefore nations will praise you forever and ever." The King's family fills the earth, and His name outlasts every generation — which is precisely the history of Jesus Christ for two thousand years. A wedding song that ends with the nations praising the Bridegroom forever: that is not a royal Israelite wedding anymore. That is Revelation 19 — the marriage supper of the Lamb — sung in advance, in the Psalms, by a poet whose heart overflowed.

### Key verses

> "You are the most handsome of the sons of men; grace is poured upon your lips; therefore God has blessed you forever." — Psalm 45:2 (ESV)

> "In your majesty ride out victoriously for the cause of truth and meekness and righteousness; let your right hand teach you awesome deeds!" — Psalm 45:4 (ESV)

> "Your throne, O God, is forever and ever. The scepter of uprightness is the scepter of your kingdom." — Psalm 45:6 (ESV)

> "Hear, O daughter, and consider, and incline your ear: forget your people and your father's house, and the king will desire your beauty." — Psalm 45:10–11 (ESV)

### The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Psalm 45 is that none of us comes to this wedding beautiful. We come from our father's house — the old life, the old country of sin — with nothing glorious within, nothing the King could desire, and no wedding garment of our own. Left to ourselves, we are not the bride; we are the rebels His arrows were aimed at. But the good news is that this King does not wait for beautiful people. He rides out for the cause of truth and meekness and righteousness, He conquers His enemies by making them His bride, and He clothes her — us — in gold He provides: His own righteousness, worked within by His Spirit, until the princess is all glorious. He desires the beauty He Himself bestows. Forget your father's house: the old life has no claim that outranks His call, and the wedding procession is forming up now.

**A note from the Reformers.** The Reformers read Psalm 45 with Hebrews 1 open beside it, and they were blunt about it: a psalm that calls the King "O God" and speaks of "your God" in the same breath is a psalm about the two natures and the eternal Sonship of Christ, and any reading that makes Him a mere man has explained away the text rather than explained it. Calvin added the pastoral note the Reformers loved: the bride's glory is "within" — the church's true beauty is not her outward pomp but the hidden righteousness and holiness Christ works in her. A bride adorned by her Bridegroom has nothing to boast of but Him. *Soli Deo gloria* — even at the wedding.

**Reflection question:** What part of "your father's house" — the old life, the old loyalties, the old name — are you still keeping a room in? And what would it mean this week to forget it on purpose, because the King who desires you has called you out of it and into His procession?

### Psalm 46 — God Is Our Refuge and Strength: Be Still

**Jesus in this psalm:** This is Luther's psalm — the one that became "A Mighty Fortress Is Our God" — sung over a city that cannot be shaken because God is in the midst of her. Its opening is the charter of every frightened believer: "God is our refuge and strength, a very present help in trouble." Jesus is that very present help in the flesh — Immanuel, God with us — who stood in a boat in a roaring sea, when the mountains were sliding into the heart of the deep, and said "Peace! Be still!" and the wind ceased. And the psalm's great command, "Be still, and know that I am God," reaches its fullness in Him: the stillness God commands is not the stillness of giving up but of knowing — knowing who is enthroned, who is with us, and who will be exalted in the earth.

**The key verse:**

> "God is our refuge and strength, a very present help in trouble." — Psalm 46:1 (ESV)

"Very present" — not distant, not eventual, not theoretical. In trouble, God is not on His way; He is here, nearer than the trouble itself. That is why the psalm can say "therefore we will not fear" before it knows how the story ends.

### Psalm 47 — God Is the King of All the Earth

**Jesus in this psalm:** A shout of a psalm: "Clap your hands, all peoples! Shout to God with loud songs of joy! For the LORD, the Most High, is to be feared, a great king over all the earth." Its center is an ascension scene: "God has gone up with a shout, the LORD with the sound of a trumpet" — the church has always heard in it the ascension of Jesus, gone up with shouts of angels and the trumpet of heaven, to the throne of all the earth. The psalm ends with the princes of the peoples gathered as the people of the God of Abraham — the nations streaming to the King — which is exactly the history that began when the ascended Christ poured out His Spirit and sent His church to the ends of the earth.

**The key verse:**

> "For God is the King of all the earth; sing praises with a psalm!" — Psalm 47:7 (ESV)

Not the king of Israel only, not the king of the respectable or the religious — King of *all the earth*, including the parts that do not yet know it. The church sings this psalm as news, not as a wish.

### Psalm 48 — Great Is the LORD: The City of Our God

**Jesus in this psalm:** Zion — Jerusalem, the city of the great King — is praised as beautiful in elevation, the joy of all the earth, impregnable because God Himself is in her citadels. The church reads this psalm now with transferred eyes: the city of God is the people of God, gathered around the risen Christ, against which the gates of hell shall not prevail. And the psalm's last verse is the promise that makes the city safe forever — this God is *our* God, forever and ever, and "he will guide us forever." Jesus is that Guide: the Shepherd-King of the city who leads His people home, and the true temple at its center, the place where God and man meet.

**The key verse:**

> "That this is God, our God forever and ever. He will guide us forever." — Psalm 48:14 (ESV)

Forever and ever — and then, as if that needed underlining, "he will guide us *forever*." Death does not end the guidance. The Guide does not resign at the grave; He walks His people through it and home.

### Psalm 49 — Why Should I Fear? Riches Cannot Ransom

**Jesus in this psalm:** A wisdom psalm aimed at the rich and the poor alike: do not fear when a man becomes rich, for wealth cannot ransom a soul — "Truly no man can ransom another, or give to God the price of his life, for the ransom of their life is costly and can never suffice." That "never" is the psalm's great honesty — and it is the exact gap the gospel fills. What no man could pay, Jesus paid: He gave Himself as a ransom for many (Mark 10:45), the price found sufficient because the Life offered was of infinite worth. And the psalm's hope becomes personal faith: "But God will ransom my soul from the power of Sheol, for he will receive me." In Christ, that *will* has become *has*.

**The key verse:**

> "But God will ransom my soul from the power of Sheol, for he will receive me." — Psalm 49:15 (ESV)

Every other grave in this psalm is final; the rich and the wise alike "must die" and leave their wealth to others. Verse 15 is the one crack of light — and the resurrection of Jesus blew it wide open. God receives His own.

### Psalm 50 — God Himself Is Judge: The Sacrifice of Thanksgiving

**Jesus in this psalm:** Asaph's psalm is a courtroom scene: God summons heaven and earth and puts His own people on trial — not for neglecting sacrifices, but for imagining He needed them: "I will not accept a bull from your house… for every beast of the forest is mine." What God asks is the sacrifice of thanksgiving and a life that matches the liturgy: "The one who offers thanksgiving as his sacrifice glorifies me." Jesus is the end of this trial in both directions — He is the Judge who will come in fire and tempest, and He is the once-for-all sacrifice that makes the whole sacrificial system a shadow fulfilled. In Him, God's people offer the only sacrifice left and the one always wanted: thanksgiving, from hearts His mercy has made honest.

**The key verse:**

> "And call upon me in the day of trouble; I will deliver you, and you shall glorify me." — Psalm 50:15 (ESV)

Here is God's own summary of true religion: call, and I will deliver, and you will glorify. Prayer, rescue, praise. The order cannot be improved, and it cannot be reversed — the glorifying is the echo of the delivering.

## Psalm 51 — Create in Me a Clean Heart: The Penitent's Psalm

### Jesus in this psalm

Jesus is the mercy David pleads for in Psalm 51 — the ground on which a holy God can wash an adulterer and murderer and call him clean. David cries, "Purge me with hyssop, and I shall be clean; wash me, and I shall be whiter than snow" — and hyssop is the branch that painted the Passover blood on the doorposts, and the branch lifted to Jesus' lips on the cross. The cleansing David begged for exists because of the blood Jesus shed. And the sacrifice God will not despise — "a broken and contrite heart" — is the only sacrifice this psalm finally offers, because the sacrifice that actually atones had not yet been offered. It has now. Psalm 51 is the prayer of every sinner who has ever come home, and it is answered at the cross before it is finished being prayed: David's "against you, you only, have I sinned" was answered by a Savior who was sinned against, and who bore it, and who washes whiter than snow everyone who prays this psalm in truth.

### Commentary

**The occasion.** The title tells the story: "A Psalm of David, when Nathan the prophet went to him, after he had gone in to Bathsheba." The king had committed adultery, arranged a murder to cover it, and lived a year in silence (Psalm 32 tells us what that year cost his body). Then Nathan came with a story about a lamb, and David condemned himself out of his own mouth: "You are the man." Psalm 51 is what the man said next. It is the Bible's great psalm of repentance, prayed by the church for three thousand years, because it refuses every shortcut: no excuses, no blame-shifting, no bargaining — just a guilty man, a merciful God, and a plea with nothing in its hands.

**According to your steadfast love (51:1–2).** The psalm opens not with the sin but with the character of God:

> "Have mercy on me, O God, according to your steadfast love; according to your abundant mercy blot out my transgressions. Wash me thoroughly from my iniquity, and cleanse me from my sin!" — Psalm 51:1–2 (ESV)

David appeals to nothing in himself. His grounds are all in God: *steadfast love* — covenant love that does not let go; *abundant mercy* — compassion in quantities that swamp the offense. And his requests are total: blot out (the record), wash thoroughly (the stain), cleanse (the pollution). Sin is a debt, a dirt, and a disease all at once, and David asks God to deal with all three. Notice: he does not ask God to overlook, to minimize, or to look the other way. He asks to be *washed*. Forgiveness in this psalm is not God pretending; it is God cleansing.

**Against you, you only (51:3–6).** "For I know my transgressions, and my sin is ever before me." The year of silence is over; now the sin is ever before him — and that, painful as it is, is the beginning of health. Then the sentence that has puzzled and pierced readers ever since:

> "Against you, you only, have I sinned and done what is evil in your sight, so that you may be justified in your words and blameless in your judgment." — Psalm 51:4 (ESV)

David sinned against Bathsheba, against Uriah, against his family and his nation — and he knew it. But he saw to the bottom of his sin and found that every sin is, at its root, against God: His law broken, His name dishonored, His kingship defied. That is why Paul quotes this verse in Romans 3 — God is justified when He judges, because sin is measured by the One sinned against. And David goes deeper still: "Behold, I was brought forth in iniquity, and in sin did my mother conceive me." He is not excusing himself by his birth; he is confessing that the rot goes all the way down, to the root — he is not a good man who did a bad thing but a sinner whose sin flowered. "Behold, you delight in truth in the inward being, and you teach me wisdom in the secret heart." God wants truth in the inward being — not performance, not public relations, but honesty in the secret heart. That is where repentance lives.

**Whiter than snow (51:7–9).** "Purge me with hyssop, and I shall be clean; wash me, and I shall be whiter than snow." Hyssop — the little branch used to sprinkle blood and water in Israel's cleansing rites, the branch of the Passover. David is asking for a cleansing that only God can perform, with blood God must provide. "Let me hear joy and gladness; let the bones that you have broken rejoice." The broken bones of Psalm 32 — God's fatherly breaking — are asked to *rejoice*: the same hand that broke is asked to heal, and David expects the healing to be so complete that joy returns to the very places the judgment touched. "Hide your face from my sins, and blot out all my iniquities." Hide Your face from my sins — not from me. That distinction is the gospel in miniature.

**Create in me a clean heart (51:10–12).** Now the center of the psalm, and one of the most prayed verses in human history:

> "Create in me a clean heart, O God, and renew a right spirit within me. Cast me not away from your presence, and take not your Holy Spirit from me. Restore to me the joy of your salvation, and uphold me with a willing spirit." — Psalm 51:10–12 (ESV)

*Create* — the Hebrew word is *bara*, the word of Genesis 1, used only of God. David knows his heart cannot be renovated by effort; it must be *created*, made new by the only Creator. He does not ask merely for pardon of the past but for a new heart for the future — a right, steadfast, willing spirit. And notice what he asks restored: not salvation itself, but "the *joy* of your salvation." A believer can lose the joy without losing the salvation — David is the proof — and the recovery of joy is one of God's great restorations. This is the verse for every person who has fallen after conversion and wonders if the joy can ever come back. David prayed it after adultery and murder. It came back.

**Then I will teach transgressors (51:13–15).** Forgiveness turns outward: "Then I will teach transgressors your ways, and sinners will return to you." The restored sinner becomes a teacher of sinners — not from a pedestal, but from the pit's edge, with a map drawn by his own rescue. "Deliver me from bloodguiltiness, O God, O God of my salvation, and my tongue will sing aloud of your righteousness. O Lord, open my lips, and my mouth will declare your praise." David names his sin precisely — *bloodguiltiness*; he had Uriah's blood on him and he says so. Repentance does not use soft words for hard sins. And the mouth that was shut for a year of silence asks to be opened — for praise.

**A broken and contrite heart (51:16–19).** The psalm closes with the theology of the whole matter: "For you will not delight in sacrifice, or I would give it; you will not be pleased with a burnt offering. The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit; a broken and contrite heart, O God, you will not despise." David — the king, who could have offered a thousand rams — confesses that no sacrifice he could bring would touch this sin. What God receives is the broken heart itself. And then the psalm's last turn is outward again: "Do good to Zion in your good pleasure; build up the walls of Jerusalem." The private confession ends in public prayer for God's people. The man who fell and rose wants the city to stand. True repentance always ends there: not wallowing in the sin, but washed, singing, and praying for others.

### Key verses

> "Have mercy on me, O God, according to your steadfast love; according to your abundant mercy blot out my transgressions." — Psalm 51:1 (ESV)

> "Against you, you only, have I sinned and done what is evil in your sight." — Psalm 51:4 (ESV)

> "Purge me with hyssop, and I shall be clean; wash me, and I shall be whiter than snow." — Psalm 51:7 (ESV)

> "Create in me a clean heart, O God, and renew a right spirit within me." — Psalm 51:10 (ESV)

> "Restore to me the joy of your salvation, and uphold me with a willing spirit." — Psalm 51:12 (ESV)

> "The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit; a broken and contrite heart, O God, you will not despise." — Psalm 51:17 (ESV)

### The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Psalm 51 is that sin goes all the way down and all the way up: down to the root of our nature — "in sin did my mother conceive me" — and up to the throne of God, against whom every sin is finally committed. It cannot be covered by silence (David's year of it wasted his bones), cannot be paid for by sacrifice (a thousand rams would not touch it), and cannot be fixed by trying harder, because the heart itself is the problem. If forgiveness depended on our record, our efforts, or our offerings, none of us would ever hear joy and gladness again. But the good news is that God delights in the one thing we can bring: a broken and contrite heart. And He has provided the hyssop and the blood Himself. Jesus Christ — sinned against by every sin this psalm confesses — bore those sins in His own body, so that the God who is justified in judging could also be the God who washes adulterers, murderers, addicts, and liars whiter than snow. Create, wash, restore — those are God's verbs, and He is still conjugating them over everyone who prays this psalm in truth. The joy can come back. David is the witness.

**A note from the Reformers.** Luther called Psalm 51 one of the chief psalms and lectured on it with trembling; he saw in it the whole doctrine of justification — a sinner with nothing to plead but God's steadfast love, and a God who justifies the ungodly who come with broken hearts. Calvin insisted on the order of verse 10: David asks first for a *created* heart, because pardon without renewal would leave the fountain poisoned; the Reformers called this the double grace of Christ — justification and sanctification, received together, never earned separately. And they pressed verse 17 on every formalism: God will not be bought with sacrifices, services, or sums. A broken and contrite heart is the one offering He has never once despised.

**Reflection question:** Which line of this psalm is the one you have been avoiding — "against you, you only," "my sin is ever before me," "create in me a clean heart," or "restore to me the joy of your salvation"? Pray the whole psalm aloud this week, slowly, with your own sin named where David named his — and then say verse 7 back to God as a promise He made, not a hope you invented: "wash me, and I shall be whiter than snow."

### Psalm 52 — Why Do You Boast of Evil, O Mighty Man?

**Jesus in this psalm:** David wrote this against Doeg the Edomite, the informer whose report to Saul led to the slaughter of the priests of Nob — a man whose tongue was "a sharp razor" and who loved evil more than good. Against Doeg's boasting in his wealth and wickedness, David sets his own future: "But I am like a green olive tree in the house of God. I trust in the steadfast love of God forever and ever." Jesus stands in this psalm as the righteous One betrayed by an informer — Judas was a Doeg with a kiss — and as the steadfast love in which the betrayed take root. Informers die and are laughed at by the righteous; olive trees in God's house are green forever.

**The key verse:**

> "But I am like a green olive tree in the house of God. I trust in the steadfast love of God forever and ever." — Psalm 52:8 (ESV)

Doeg trusted in the abundance of his riches; David trusted in the steadfast love of God. One of those roots is in soil that washes away. Plant yourself in the other, and you will still be green when the informers are forgotten.

### Psalm 53 — The Fool Says in His Heart, "There Is No God"

**Jesus in this psalm:** Psalm 53 is Psalm 14 set again to music — nearly the same words, with God's name changed throughout to *Elohim* — which tells us the church was meant to sing this indictment more than once. The fool's practical atheism, the corruption of the whole race, God's search for anyone who understands and seeks Him, and the closing longing for salvation out of Zion: all of it stands as Romans 3 reads it, the universal verdict that makes the universal Savior necessary. Jesus is the salvation out of Zion that both versions of the psalm end by longing for — and the fact that God gave us this psalm twice suggests He knows how slowly we learn that the problem is not them. It is us. And the answer is not us either. It is Him.

**The key verse:**

> "Oh, that salvation for Israel would come out of Zion! When God restores the fortunes of his people, let Jacob rejoice, let Israel be glad." — Psalm 53:6 (ESV)

Twice the Psalter sings this longing — and the longing has been answered. Salvation came out of Zion, and Jacob's rejoicing has become the church's. Some prayers God answers so completely that we forget they were ever questions.

---

### Psalm 54 — God Is My Helper

**Jesus in this psalm:** A short, sharp prayer from David's days of hiding, when the Ziphites went to Saul and betrayed his location — "Is not David hiding among us?" David's answer to betrayal by his own countrymen is one sentence of theology: "Behold, God is my helper; the Lord is the upholder of my life." Jesus prayed this psalm's substance through His own betrayals — by Judas, by the fleeing disciples, by a nation that handed Him over — and He is now the Helper this psalm names for us: the Lord who upholds the lives of the betrayed, and who answers prayer not always by removing the pursuers but by upholding the pursued.

**The key verse:**

> "Behold, God is my helper; the Lord is the upholder of my life." — Psalm 54:4 (ESV)

*Upholder* — the picture is of a hand under a man who would otherwise fall. David's life was not upheld by his hiding places, which kept being betrayed, but by a hand that could not be. Yours is upheld the same way.

### Psalm 55 — Cast Your Burden on the LORD

**Jesus in this psalm:** This is the betrayal psalm at its most personal: "For it is not an enemy that taunts me — then I could bear it… But it is you, a man, my equal, my companion, my familiar friend. We used to take sweet counsel together; within God's house we walked in the throng." Ahithophel is the shadow behind it — David's counselor who joined Absalom's revolt — and Judas is its fulfillment: the familiar friend, the table companion, the man of the inner circle whose betrayal was by kiss. Jesus prayed this psalm's anguish in truth — "my heart is in anguish within me; the terrors of death have fallen upon me" — and He is the LORD of its famous promise, quoted by Peter for the anxious church: cast your burden on Him, and He will sustain you (1 Peter 5:7).

**The key verse:**

> "Cast your burden on the LORD, and he will sustain you; he will never permit the righteous to be moved." — Psalm 55:22 (ESV)

*Cast* — throw it, the way you throw something too heavy to carry carefully. The promise is not that the burden disappears, but that the bearer is sustained: God carries the man who throws his load on God. He has never yet dropped one.

### Psalm 56 — When I Am Afraid, I Put My Trust in You

**Jesus in this psalm:** David wrote this "when the Philistines seized him in Gath" — a fugitive in the hometown of Goliath, surrounded by enemies who trampled him and twisted his words all day long. His refrain is the whole art of fear faced honestly: "When I am afraid, I put my trust in you." Not "I am never afraid" — *when* I am afraid, trust is what I *do* with the fear. Jesus prayed this trust through Gethsemane and the cross, and He stands in the psalm's tenderest verse as the God who keeps the record of His people's sorrows: "You have kept count of my tossings; put my tears in your bottle. Are they not in your book?" The God of Jesus Christ bottles tears. None of yours has ever been spilled on the ground unnoticed.

**The key verse:**

> "When I am afraid, I put my trust in you." — Psalm 56:3 (ESV)

Fear is not the opposite of faith in this psalm; it is the occasion for it. The verse does not promise you will never be afraid. It teaches you what to do in the first sixty seconds after fear arrives.

### Psalm 57 — Be Exalted, O God, Above the Heavens

**Jesus in this psalm:** David wrote this in a cave, fleeing Saul — and caves run through the story of God's people like a refrain. From inside the cave, with enemies' teeth like spears and arrows around him, David sings one of the most defiant lines in the Psalter: "My heart is steadfast, O God, my heart is steadfast! I will sing and make melody!" Jesus is the greater cave-dweller who sang in the night before His arrest and who, laid in a cave-tomb, turned it into the place from which God's glory was exalted above the heavens. And the psalm's wing-shadow — "in the shadow of your wings I will take refuge, till the storms of destruction pass by" — is Jesus' own image for His longing over Jerusalem: "How often would I have gathered your children together as a hen gathers her brood under her wings."

**The key verse:**

> "Be merciful to me, O God, be merciful to me, for in you my soul takes refuge; in the shadow of your wings I will take refuge, till the storms of destruction pass by." — Psalm 57:1 (ESV)

"Till the storms pass by" — storms, in this psalm, have an expiration date, and refuge has none. The cave is temporary. The wings are not. Steadfast hearts are made in caves, by people who sing before the storm is over.

### Psalm 58 — Surely There Is a God Who Judges on Earth

**Jesus in this psalm:** David addresses corrupt rulers and judges — "gods" who decree injustice, whose hands deal out violence — and calls down God's judgment on them with some of the Psalter's fiercest language. This psalm is the prayer of the powerless against rigged courts, and it ends with the verdict the whole world needs to hear: "Surely there is a reward for the righteous; surely there is a God who judges on earth." Jesus is that Judge — the One who warned unjust judges in parables, who will separate the nations at His coming, and who meanwhile teaches His people to pray this psalm without taking the judgment into their own hands. The fierceness of the psalm is itself a mercy: it gives the wronged somewhere to take their rage — to the bench of God, where it belongs.

**The key verse:**

> "Surely there is a reward for the righteous; surely there is a God who judges on earth." — Psalm 58:11 (ESV)

Twice "surely." When every court on earth seems bought, this verse is the appeal to the court that isn't. Men will say it, the psalm promises, when they see God act: there *is* a God who judges. He does not hurry. He does not miss.

### Psalm 59 — Deliver Me from My Enemies, O My God

**Jesus in this psalm:** Saul has sent men to watch David's house in order to kill him, and David prays through the night siege: his enemies prowl like dogs at evening, bellowing, sure of themselves — "God will not hear." David's answer is to sing before the deliverance arrives: "But I will sing of your strength; I will sing aloud of your steadfast love in the morning." Jesus knew this night — watched houses, plotted arrests, enemies who prowled and bellowed and were sure heaven was silent — and He is the strength and fortress of everyone who prays this psalm tonight. The pattern is the psalm's gift: sing of His strength in the siege, and of His steadfast love in the morning. Sometimes in that order, by faith.

**The key verse:**

> "But I will sing of your strength; I will sing aloud of your steadfast love in the morning. For you have been to me a fortress and a refuge in the day of my distress." — Psalm 59:16 (ESV)

Morning singing is faith's signature — praise scheduled before the evidence is in. The men outside the house were loud all night; David was louder at dawn, because he knew whose voice would be heard last.

### Psalm 60 — With God We Shall Do Valiantly

**Jesus in this psalm:** A national psalm of defeat: God has rejected us, broken our defenses, made His people see hard things and drink the wine of staggering — and David, in the middle of it, asks the question every beaten believer asks: "Who will bring me to the fortified city?" The psalm refuses two easy roads: it will not pretend the defeat didn't happen, and it will not trust in man to reverse it — "vain is the salvation of man." Jesus stands in this psalm as the banner God sets up for those who fear Him and as the true answer to its closing confession: with God we shall do valiantly — the valor finally Christ's, who fought the fortified city of sin and death alone and won, and who brings His staggered people into victories they could never have taken by themselves.

**The key verse:**

> "With God we shall do valiantly; it is he who will tread down our foes." — Psalm 60:12 (ESV)

Note the division of labor: *we* do valiantly; *he* treads down the foes. God's people fight, and God's power wins. The psalm holds both without embarrassment — effort and dependence, swords and sovereignty, in one verse.

### Psalm 61 — Lead Me to the Rock That Is Higher Than I

**Jesus in this psalm:** David prays "from the end of the earth" — far from home, heart fainting — and his whole prayer is one request: "Lead me to the rock that is higher than I." He does not ask to be made higher; he asks to be *led* to a rock that already is. Jesus is that Rock — the Rock of ages, the stone the builders rejected, the foundation that stands above every flood of distance and faintness. And the psalm's vow — "I will dwell in your tent forever" and sing praises daily — is the exile's road home, walked by every believer who has ever felt far away and discovered that the distance is measured by our feelings, not by God's nearness.

**The key verse:**

> "From the end of the earth I call to you when my heart is faint. Lead me to the rock that is higher than I." — Psalm 61:2 (ESV)

Higher than I — that is the whole qualification of a refuge. A rock at your own height will drown when you do. Bring your faint heart to the Rock that stands above the waterline of every flood, and ask to be led: He has never refused that prayer.

### Psalm 62 — For God Alone My Soul Waits in Silence

**Jesus in this psalm:** David's psalm of *alone* — the word tolls through it like a bell: "For God alone my soul waits in silence"; "He alone is my rock and my salvation"; "Trust in him at all times, O people; pour out your heart before him." Against enemies who plot his ruin and bless with their mouths while cursing inwardly, David sets the arithmetic of faith: men of low estate are a breath, men of high estate are a delusion, and riches, if they increase, are not to be set the heart upon. Jesus is the rock and salvation this psalm waits for — and He lived its silence: silent before His accusers, waiting on the Father alone, while men weighed Him in their balances and found Him, in their delusion, light. God found Him otherwise — and finds us, in Him, weighty with a glory not our own.

**The key verse:**

> "Trust in him at all times, O people; pour out your heart before him; God is a refuge for us." — Psalm 62:8 (ESV)

"At all times" — not only in the good ones. And the method of trust is pouring: emptying the heart out before God like water from a jar, everything in it, unedited. A refuge you cannot pour your heart out in is not much of a refuge. This one invites the pouring.

### Psalm 63 — Your Steadfast Love Is Better Than Life

**Jesus in this psalm:** David wrote this in the wilderness of Judah — thirsty land, thirsty soul: "O God, you are my God; earnestly I seek you; my soul thirsts for you; my flesh faints for you, as in a dry and weary land where there is no water." The wilderness taught him the psalm's great valuation: "Because your steadfast love is better than life, my lips will praise you." Better than life — love weighed against life itself, and life found lighter. Jesus prayed this psalm's thirst in His own wilderness and on His cross, and He is the steadfast love it celebrates: the love that is, in fact, better than life, because it survives death and gives life back. In Him, the dry land becomes a sanctuary, and night watches become feasts: "My soul will be satisfied as with fat and rich food."

**The key verse:**

> "Because your steadfast love is better than life, my lips will praise you." — Psalm 63:3 (ESV)

Everything in the Christian life is decided by what a man puts on the other side of "better than." David put God's love against his own life and chose the love — which is why, in a waterless wilderness, his lips were full of praise instead of complaint.

### Psalm 64 — Hear My Voice, O God, in My Complaint

**Jesus in this psalm:** David's enemies work in secret — they shoot at the blameless from ambush, they "hold fast to their evil purpose," they are sure no one sees. The psalm's turn is one of the Psalter's quiet thunderclaps: "But God shoots his arrow at them; they are wounded suddenly." The ambushers are ambushed. Jesus faced the secret councils and the hidden plots — the night meetings, the paid informer, the trap questions — and the God of this psalm reversed them all at the resurrection, suddenly, before morning. In Christ, the righteous rejoice and take refuge, and every secret plot against God's people is already known to the only marksman who never misses.

**The key verse:**

> "Let the righteous one rejoice in the LORD and take refuge in him! Let all the upright in heart exult!" — Psalm 64:10 (ESV)

The psalm begins with a complaint whispered in fear and ends with exulting in the open. That journey — complaint to exulting, ambush to refuge — is the road God walks His people down, sometimes slowly, never unsuccessfully.

### Psalm 65 — Praise Is Due to You, O God, in Zion

**Jesus in this psalm:** A harvest psalm of overflowing gratitude: God hears prayer, atones for transgressions ("when iniquities prevail against me, you atone for our transgressions"), crowns the year with bounty, and makes the valleys shout for joy. Its portrait of the blessed man is a man *chosen and brought near* to dwell in God's courts — satisfied "with the goodness of your house." Jesus is the atonement this psalm presupposes — the One by whom iniquities that prevail against us are covered — and He is the Lord of the harvest who taught us to see the Father's hand in rain and grain. In Him, the God who answers prayer "to you shall all flesh come" is not a hope but a history already begun.

**The key verse:**

> "Blessed is the one you choose and bring near, to dwell in your courts! We shall be satisfied with the goodness of your house, the holiness of your temple!" — Psalm 65:4 (ESV)

Chosen, brought near, satisfied — the whole gospel in the psalm's own order. The satisfaction is not in the harvest fields, rich as they are; it is in the house, in the nearness. Men are satisfied, finally, not by gifts but by the Giver's company.

### Psalm 66 — Shout for Joy to God, All the Earth

**Jesus in this psalm:** A psalm of corporate and personal thanksgiving braided together: the congregation recounts the Exodus — "He turned the sea into dry land" — and then one voice steps forward with a personal testimony: "Come and hear, all you who fear God, and I will tell what he has done for my soul." The testimony is honest about the process: "For you, O God, have tested us; you have tried us as silver is tried" — and about the condition of answered prayer: "If I had cherished iniquity in my heart, the Lord would not have listened." Jesus is the greater Exodus and the greater tested One — tried as silver, found pure — and the God who brought Israel through fire and water has, in Him, brought His church "out to a place of abundance." The psalm ends with a vow every delivered person recognizes: I will tell.

**The key verse:**

> "Blessed be God, because he has not rejected my prayer or removed his steadfast love from me!" — Psalm 66:20 (ESV)

Two mercies, and either alone would be enough: He did not reject my prayer, and He did not remove His love. The delivered man's whole theology fits in that sentence — and it is the testimony of everyone who has cried to God from the fire and been brought through it.

### Psalm 67 — Let the Peoples Praise You, O God

**Jesus in this psalm:** The missionary psalm — Aaron's blessing ("May God be gracious to us and bless us and make his face to shine upon us") prayed outward until it covers the earth: "that your way may be known on earth, your saving power among all nations." Its refrain tolls twice: "Let the peoples praise you, O God; let all the peoples praise you!" Jesus is the saving power this psalm wants the nations to know — the face of God shining on us is, in the New Testament's language, the light of the knowledge of God's glory in the face of Jesus Christ. And the psalm's logic is the church's charter: we are blessed *in order to be* a blessing; the shining face is given so that the nations may see it. Blessing hoarded stops; blessing shared multiplies to the ends of the earth.

**The key verse:**

> "May God be gracious to us and bless us and make his face to shine upon us, that your way may be known on earth, your saving power among all nations." — Psalm 67:1–2 (ESV)

There is a comma in this prayer that changes everything: blessed *so that*. Ask God to bless you with verse 2 attached — His way known, His saving power among the nations — and you are praying a prayer He has been answering since Abraham.

### Psalm 68 — God Shall Arise: The Victorious Procession

**Jesus in this psalm:** The great procession psalm — God arising, enemies scattering like smoke, the ark ascending the mountain with captives in its train, gifts received among men. Paul quotes its summit in Ephesians 4 about the ascended Christ: "When he ascended on high he led a host of captives, and he gave gifts to men" — the ascension of Jesus, distributing the spoils of His victory as gifts to His church: apostles, prophets, evangelists, shepherds, teachers. And the psalm's daily comfort stands in verse 19: "Blessed be the Lord, who daily bears us up; God is our salvation." The God who rides in procession over the heavens is the same God who, in Christ, carries His people day by day — the conquering King and the burden-bearing Savior are one Person.

**The key verse:**

> "Blessed be the Lord, who daily bears us up; God is our salvation." — Psalm 68:19 (ESV)

*Daily* bears us up — not once, at conversion, and then you're on your own; daily, the way a father carries a child every day of the journey, including the days the child is too tired or too hurt to walk. He has not missed a day yet.

## Psalm 69 — Save Me, O God: The Waters Have Come Up to My Neck

### Jesus in this psalm

Next to Psalm 22, Psalm 69 is the psalm the New Testament quotes most about the sufferings of Jesus — it is, after the psalm of the cross itself, the passion psalm. "Zeal for your house has consumed me" — quoted by the disciples when Jesus cleansed the temple (John 2:17). "The reproaches of those who reproach you have fallen on me" — quoted by Paul of Christ (Romans 15:3). "They gave me poison for food, and for my thirst they gave me sour wine to drink" — fulfilled at the cross, where sour wine was lifted to His lips (John 19:29). "Let their camp be desolate" and "may another take his office" — applied by the apostles to Judas (Acts 1:20, from Psalms 69 and 109). "More in number than the hairs of my head are those who hate me without cause" — the causeless hatred Jesus named (John 15:25). When you read Psalm 69, you are reading the inner life of Christ in His sufferings — the zeal, the reproach, the sinking, the thirst — and, at the end, His confidence that God will build up Zion and that the humble shall see it and be glad.

### Commentary

**The waters have come up to my neck (69:1–4).** The psalm begins underwater: "Save me, O God! For the waters have come up to my neck. I sink in deep mire, where there is no foothold; I have come into deep waters, and the flood sweeps over me. I am weary with my crying out; my throat is parched." No foothold — that is the terror of it: not merely deep water, but nothing solid underfoot, the mire that gives way. And the suffering is causeless: "More in number than the hairs of my head are those who hate me without cause; mighty are those who would destroy me, those who attack me with lies." Jesus took that sentence as His own (John 15:25). There is a suffering that comes with a reason attached, and a suffering that comes with none — and the second kind is the kind the Son of God specialized in, so that no sufferer of it would ever suffer it alone or unheard.

**Zeal for your house (69:5–12).** "O God, you know my folly; the wrongs I have done are not hidden from you." The speaker is honest about his own faults — this is not the prayer of a man claiming perfection — and yet the reproach he bears is chiefly for God's sake: "For it is for your sake that I have borne reproach… I have become a stranger to my brothers, an alien to my mother's sons." His own family does not know him (compare John 7:5 — even Jesus' brothers did not believe in Him). And then the verse the disciples remembered in the temple courts:

> "For zeal for your house has consumed me, and the reproaches of those who reproach you have fallen on me." — Psalm 69:9 (ESV)

*Consumed* — eaten up, burned up. Zeal for God's house was not Jesus' hobby; it was the fire that ate His life, and it carried Him to a cross where the reproaches aimed at God fell on Him instead. Paul quotes the second half of this verse as the pattern of Christ's whole ministry: "Christ did not please himself" (Romans 15:3). There is the biography of Jesus in one line: the reproach was ours and God's enemies'; the bearing of it was His.

**They gave me sour wine (69:13–21).** The middle of the psalm pleads for deliverance "at an acceptable time" — and verse 21 drops into the passion narrative with a detail no one could invent afterward: "They gave me poison for food, and for my thirst they gave me sour wine to drink." At the cross, when Jesus said "I thirst," a sponge full of sour wine was lifted to His mouth on a hyssop branch (John 19:28–29) — the hyssop of Psalm 51, the sour wine of Psalm 69, meeting in one moment. And the psalm's description of the sufferer's isolation reads like a transcript of Golgotha: "Reproach has broken my heart, so that I am in despair. I looked for pity, but there was none, and for comforters, but I found none." The disciples had fled; the women watched from a distance; the comforters were absent. He drank the cup of reproach entirely alone, so that His people would never have to drink theirs entirely alone again.

**The imprecations (69:22–28).** Then the psalm does what makes modern readers flinch: it calls down judgment on the persecutors — let their table become a snare, their eyes darkened, their camp desolate, another to take the office of the betrayer, their names blotted out of the book of life. We should not soften these verses, and we should not skip them: the New Testament itself applies them — to Judas, and (in Romans 11) to those who stumble over Christ in unbelief. Their function is to hand vengeance to God in prayer rather than take it in hand — David prays his rage instead of acting it. And for the church, these verses carry a sober warning with the comfort: God takes the treatment of His Son, and of His suffering people, personally and judicially. The imprecations are the sound of justice being left where it belongs — with the Judge — by a sufferer who refuses to become an executioner.

**The humble shall see this and be glad (69:29–36).** The psalm lands, as Psalm 22 did, on the far side of the suffering: "But I am afflicted and in pain; let your salvation, O God, set me on high! I will praise the name of God with a song; I will magnify him with thanksgiving." And then the congregation-wide promise: "When the humble see it they will be glad; you who seek God, let your hearts revive. For the LORD hears the needy and does not despise his own people who are prisoners." Prisoners — God's own people, in chains, and not despised. The psalm ends with Zion built up and the offspring of God's servants inheriting it: "the children of his servants shall inherit it; those who love his name shall dwell in it." The sufferer who began neck-deep in the flood ends by prophesying the city of God full of his descendants. That is not optimism. That is the resurrection, casting its shadow backward into the psalm.

### Key verses

> "Save me, O God! For the waters have come up to my neck." — Psalm 69:1 (ESV)

> "For zeal for your house has consumed me, and the reproaches of those who reproach you have fallen on me." — Psalm 69:9 (ESV)

> "They gave me poison for food, and for my thirst they gave me sour wine to drink." — Psalm 69:21 (ESV)

> "When the humble see it they will be glad; you who seek God, let your hearts revive. For the LORD hears the needy and does not despise his own people who are prisoners." — Psalm 69:32–33 (ESV)

### The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Psalm 69 is that this is what the world does to God's anointed — and, in smaller measure, to God's people: causeless hatred, reproach for God's sake, isolation in the hour of pain, sour wine for thirst. And the psalm is honest that some of the suffering is entangled with our own folly, which God knows and does not hide from. Left to ourselves we sink in the mire without foothold, and no comforters come. But the good news is that Jesus has sunk in this mire first — all the way down, for us — and God did not leave Him there. The zeal that consumed Him built a house God will never abandon; the reproach that broke His heart fell on Him so that it need not crush us; and the God who hears the needy and does not despise His prisoners has set His afflicted One on high and filled Zion with His offspring. If you are afflicted and in pain tonight, this psalm is your address: the LORD hears the needy. He has the track record — a cross and an empty tomb — to prove it.

**A note from the Reformers.** Calvin read Psalm 69 as David's prayer but Christ's psalm, and he taught the church to handle its imprecations the way David did: as prayer, not permission — the wronged believer brings his cause to God's tribunal and leaves the sentence to the Judge, which is the exact opposite of revenge. Spurgeon called this psalm "the song of the suffering Savior" second only to Psalm 22, and he lingered on verse 33 as a text for the chained and the addicted and the imprisoned of every kind: the LORD *hears* the needy and *does not despise* His prisoners. A God who does not despise prisoners is a God who can be preached in any cell, and believed in any pit.

**Reflection question:** Where are the waters up to your neck right now — and have you been trying to keep your head up by your own effort, or have you cried this psalm's first two words: "Save me"? This week, take one specific reproach or pain you have been carrying silently and do what the psalm does: tell God about it in detail, hand Him the vengeance, and end by saying verse 33 back to Him as His promise to you.

### Psalm 70 — Make Haste, O God, to Deliver Me

**Jesus in this psalm:** Psalm 70 is the urgent appendix of Psalm 40 — its last five verses lifted out and set on their own, for sufferers who need the short version in a hurry: "Make haste, O God, to deliver me! O LORD, make haste to help me!" There is no long introduction, no recital of history — just a hunted man, his joy in God, and a closing confession: "But I am poor and needy; hasten to me, O God! You are my help and my deliverer; O LORD, do not delay!" Jesus prayed haste like this in Gethsemane's urgency, and He is the Deliverer whose haste we still plead — and whose delays, we learn from Psalm 40, are never indifference. Sometimes God's people need a long psalm. Sometimes they need five verses and the word *now*. God gave us both.

**The key verse:**

> "But I am poor and needy; hasten to me, O God! You are my help and my deliverer; O LORD, do not delay!" — Psalm 70:5 (ESV)

Poor and needy is not a disqualification for God's help; in this psalm it is the qualification. The man who knows his poverty can pray "hasten" with a clear conscience, because he is asking the right Person for the right reason.

### Psalm 71 — In You, O LORD, Do I Take Refuge: The Old Man's Psalm

**Jesus in this psalm:** This is the psalm of old age — an elderly believer reviewing a whole life of God's faithfulness: "Upon you I have leaned from before my birth; you are he who took me from my mother's womb." His prayer is the prayer of every aging saint: "Do not cast me off in the time of old age; forsake me not when my strength is spent… So even to old age and gray hairs, O God, do not forsake me, until I proclaim your might to another generation." Jesus is the guarantee of this psalm's answer: the God who leaned a man from birth will not cast him off at the end, because the Son who promised "I am with you always, to the end of the age" does not retire His people. And the old man's purpose — proclaiming God's might to another generation — is the church's oldest and best retirement plan.

**The key verse:**

> "So even to old age and gray hairs, O God, do not forsake me, until I proclaim your might to another generation, your power to all those to come." — Psalm 71:18 (ESV)

Gray hairs are not the end of usefulness; in this psalm they are a pulpit. The man who has leaned on God for seventy years has something the young cannot buy: a lifetime of receipts. God keeps His old servants on purpose — there is another generation that needs their testimony.

## Psalm 72 — Give the King Your Justice: The Reign of the Righteous King

### Jesus in this psalm

Jesus is the King of Psalm 72 — this royal psalm, prayed by Solomon (or by David for Solomon, as its title and its closing line suggest), overflows every earthly king who ever sat on Israel's throne and lands on Christ alone. No son of David before Him reigned from sea to sea and to the ends of the earth; no earthly king's name endured forever and increased as long as the sun; in no earthly king did *all nations* call themselves blessed. In Jesus, every line comes true: He defends the cause of the poor and delivers the needy, He crushes the oppressor, kings fall down before Him and the nations serve Him, and men are blessed in Him. Book Two ends with this psalm deliberately: the prayers of David end, looking forward to a King whose prayers and reign never will. Psalm 72 is the Psalter's portrait of the coming Christ, hung at the close of David's collection like a promise the rest of the book will keep developing.

### Commentary

**Justice for the poor (72:1–4).** The psalm opens as a prayer — the church praying for its king: "Give the king your justice, O God, and your righteousness to the royal son!" And the first mark of the true king's reign is not conquest but *justice*: "May he judge your people with righteousness, and your poor with justice!… May he defend the cause of the poor of the people, give deliverance to the children of the needy, and crush the oppressor!" This is how you recognize God's king in every age: he is found where the poor are, defending causes no one else will take, delivering children no one else counts, and crushing the oppressors everyone else fears or funds. Jesus announced His reign in exactly these terms — good news to the poor, liberty to the captives (Luke 4:18) — and He will complete it when He judges the earth. A king's righteousness is measured at the bottom of his kingdom, not the top.

**From sea to sea (72:5–11).** The scope of the reign widens past every border: "May he live while the sun endures, and as long as the moon, throughout all generations!… May he have dominion from sea to sea, and from the River to the ends of the earth!" The desert tribes bow, the kings of Tarshish and the coastlands render tribute, the kings of Sheba and Seba bring gifts — the church has always thought of the wise men at Bethlehem, the first tribute-bearers arriving early. "For he delivers the needy when he calls, the poor and him who has no helper. He has pity on the weak and the needy, and saves the lives of the needy. From oppression and violence he redeems their life, and precious is their blood in his sight." Precious is their blood — the needy are not a problem to this King; they are a treasure He redeems at cost. That is the character of the reign of Jesus, now in His church and soon over the earth.

**Abundance and prayer (72:12–17).** The reign is fruitful: "May there be abundance of grain in the land; on the tops of the mountains may it wave; may its fruit be like Lebanon." Even the mountaintops grow grain under this King — the barren places become fields. "May his name endure forever, his fame continue as long as the sun! May people be blessed in him, all nations call him blessed!" That last line reaches back to God's promise to Abraham — in your offspring shall all the nations of the earth be blessed — and forward to the church's whole history: nations calling Jesus blessed, people being blessed *in him*, by union with him. There is no blessing from this King at a distance; it is all *in him*.

**The doxology (72:18–20).** And then Book Two closes its covers with praise and a colophon: "Blessed be the LORD, the God of Israel, who alone does wondrous things. Blessed be his glorious name forever; may the whole earth be filled with his glory! Amen and Amen! The prayers of David, the son of Jesse, are ended." The prayers of David end — but they end pointing forward, to a Son whose reign fills the earth with glory. David's prayers are ended; David's hope is not. The whole earth filled with His glory is still the church's prayer — it is, in fact, the Lord's Prayer in older words: Your kingdom come, Your will be done, on earth as it is in heaven.

### Key verses

> "Give the king your justice, O God, and your righteousness to the royal son! May he judge your people with righteousness, and your poor with justice!" — Psalm 72:1–2 (ESV)

> "May he have dominion from sea to sea, and from the River to the ends of the earth!" — Psalm 72:8 (ESV)

> "For he delivers the needy when he calls, the poor and him who has no helper." — Psalm 72:12 (ESV)

> "May his name endure forever, his fame continue as long as the sun! May people be blessed in him, all nations call him blessed!" — Psalm 72:17 (ESV)

> "Blessed be the LORD, the God of Israel, who alone does wondrous things. Blessed be his glorious name forever; may the whole earth be filled with his glory! Amen and Amen!" — Psalm 72:18–19 (ESV)

### The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Psalm 72 is that no human government has ever matched it — and none will. Every earthly reign, however promising, has failed the poor, spared the oppressor, and ended at a grave; the needy still call and find helpers who tire, and blood that is precious to God is still treated as cheap by men. If our hope rests on the next king, the next election, the next system, this psalm is an accusation against our hope. But the good news is that the King has come. Jesus Christ reigns now — risen, ascended, defending the cause of the poor who call on Him, redeeming lives from oppression at the price of His own precious blood — and He is coming to reign visibly, from sea to sea, until the whole earth is filled with the glory of God. Men are blessed in Him, today, in every nation — and the prayers of David, which ended in hope, are being answered in Him still.

**A note from the Reformers.** The Reformers loved Psalm 72 as the Psalter's great missionary and kingdom psalm, and Calvin read its extravagant promises the only way they can be read without embarrassment: as the description of Christ's spiritual reign already begun and awaiting consummation — Solomon a shadow, Christ the substance. They pressed its first verse on rulers and its twelfth on the church: a king is known by his care for the needy who have no helper, and a church that bears this King's name will be found in the same places He is found. And the doxology taught them to end prayer the way Book Two ends — not with our needs but with His glory filling the earth. *Soli Deo gloria*, prayed as an Amen and Amen.

**Reflection question:** Psalm 72 measures a king by what happens to the poor and needy in his reign. Where has your hope lately been resting — on human arrangements that keep disappointing you, or on the King who delivers the needy when he calls? And since you are a citizen of His kingdom, where is one place this week you can stand where He stands: defending a cause, delivering a child of the needy, treating someone's blood as precious?

# Book Three — Psalms 73–89: The Dark Book

*Asaph, the sons of Korah, Heman, and Ethan — faith wrestling in the ruins. The temple falls, the covenant seems broken, and Book Three ends in lament. It closes with Psalm 89:52: "Blessed be the LORD forever! Amen and Amen" — praise, remarkably, still on the lips of a man whose questions have not been answered.*

## Psalm 73 — My Feet Had Almost Stumbled: The Sanctuary Turn

### Jesus in this psalm

Jesus is the nearness of God that Asaph discovers at the end of Psalm 73 — the good that turns out not to be a thing but a Person. Asaph's whole crisis is resolved when he can say, "Whom have I in heaven but you? And there is nothing on earth that I desire besides you… God is the strength of my heart and my portion forever." In Christ, that confession has a face: to have Jesus is to have the portion; to be near God is now possible because God has come near to us in Him, and "it is good to be near God" is the gospel's own summary. Jesus also prayed the dark half of this psalm — He watched the wicked prosper all the way to His own cross, where arrogant men seemed to win — and He rose as the living proof of Asaph's conclusion: the prosperous wicked are on slippery ground, and the afflicted believer's portion outlasts them all.

### Commentary

**God is truly good — but (73:1–3).** The psalm opens with its conclusion stated first, like a man telling you the end of the story before he tells you the story: "Truly God is good to Israel, to those who are pure in heart." And then the confession: "But as for me, my feet had almost stumbled, my steps had nearly slipped. For I was envious of the arrogant when I saw the prosperity of the wicked." *Almost* — the most honest word in the psalm. Asaph, a worship leader in the temple, nearly lost his footing, and the cause was not suffering but *envy*: he looked sideways at the wicked — fat, prosperous, untroubled, arrogant — and his theology buckled. This is one of the most common ways believers fall: not through pain, but through comparison. The wicked man's ease became the measure, and God's goodness became the question.

**The prosperity of the wicked (73:4–12).** Asaph describes them at length, and we should let the description sting: no pangs in their death, bodies fat and sleek, not in trouble as others are, pride as their necklace, violence as their garment. They scoff and speak with malice; they set their mouths against the heavens; and the people turn to them and drink it in. Worst of all is their theology: "How can God know? Is there knowledge in the Most High?" — the wicked prosper, Asaph concludes, precisely because they have decided God neither sees nor cares. And the poison spreads to the believer's own conclusion in verse 13: "All in vain have I kept my heart clean and washed my hands in innocence." *All in vain.* That is the sentence envy always ends with: my obedience was a waste; purity was a bad investment; the wicked were right about how the world works.

**Struck all day long (73:14–15).** "For all the day long I have been stricken and rebuked every morning." While the wicked slept in, the believer got up to rebukes. Asaph is honest about the arithmetic: by every visible measure, the wicked were winning and he was losing, daily. But verse 15 shows what held him: "If I had said, 'I will speak thus,' I would have betrayed the generation of your children." He would not say his doubts aloud in a way that would wreck the faith of others — he brought them to God instead of broadcasting them. There is a godly restraint here: doubts carried to the sanctuary are medicine; doubts marketed to the congregation are poison. Asaph took his to the right address.

**Until I went into the sanctuary (73:16–20).** Then comes the turn — one of the great turning points in all Scripture:

> "But when I thought how to understand this, it seemed to me a wearisome task, until I went into the sanctuary of God; then I discerned their end." — Psalm 73:16–17 (ESV)

He could not think his way out of it at his desk; he had to *go* — into the sanctuary, into worship, into the presence of God among God's people. And there he saw what envy had hidden: not the wicked's present, but their *end*. "Truly you set them in slippery places; you make them fall to ruin. How they are destroyed in a moment, swept away utterly by terrors!" The prosperity was real — and it was ice on a slope. In the sanctuary, time horizons change: this life stops being the whole ledger, eternity enters the accounting, and the envy evaporates — not because the wicked got poorer, but because Asaph got sight.

**Whom have I in heaven but you? (73:21–28).** And then Asaph turns the knife on himself: "When my soul was embittered, when I was pricked in heart, I was brutish and ignorant; I was like a beast toward you." No excuses — envy made him an animal, all appetite and no understanding. And against that confession stands the most astonishing "nevertheless" in the Psalter: "Nevertheless, I am continually with you; you hold my right hand. You guide me with your counsel, and afterward you will receive me to glory." He was brutish; God held his hand anyway. He was envious; God guided him anyway. And afterward — after the guidance, after the life, after the death — "you will receive me to glory." Out of that grip comes the psalm's summit:

> "Whom have I in heaven but you? And there is nothing on earth that I desire besides you. My flesh and my heart may fail, but God is the strength of my heart and my portion forever." — Psalm 73:25–26 (ESV)

And the psalm that began in envy ends in evangelism and rest: "But for me it is good to be near God; I have made the Lord GOD my refuge, that I may tell of all your works." Near God — that is the good the whole psalm was hunting. Not prosperity, not vindication, not the wicked's downfall: nearness. The man who has that has the portion; the man who lacks it has nothing, however fat his portfolio.

### Key verses

> "Truly God is good to Israel, to those who are pure in heart. But as for me, my feet had almost stumbled, my steps had nearly slipped." — Psalm 73:1–2 (ESV)

> "All in vain have I kept my heart clean and washed my hands in innocence." — Psalm 73:13 (ESV)

> "Until I went into the sanctuary of God; then I discerned their end." — Psalm 73:17 (ESV)

> "Whom have I in heaven but you? And there is nothing on earth that I desire besides you." — Psalm 73:25 (ESV)

> "My flesh and my heart may fail, but God is the strength of my heart and my portion forever." — Psalm 73:26 (ESV)

> "But for me it is good to be near God; I have made the Lord GOD my refuge, that I may tell of all your works." — Psalm 73:28 (ESV)

### The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Psalm 73 is that envy is not a small sin — it is a faith-killer that nearly took a worship leader down, and it works on all of us: we measure God's goodness by the wicked man's bank account and our own obedience by its visible returns, and we arrive at Asaph's blasphemy, "All in vain have I kept my heart clean." Worse, left to ourselves we cannot think our way out; the wearisome task stays wearisome at the desk. But the good news is that God receives the envious into His sanctuary and gives them sight. Jesus Christ is the nearness of God made flesh — in Him, God has come near to the brutish and the bitter, held their right hand through their worst seasons, and promised to receive them to glory afterward. Your flesh and your heart may fail — they will — but God is the strength of your heart and your portion forever. The wicked are on the slope; you are in the grip. It is good to be near God, and in Christ, you are.

**A note from the Reformers.** Calvin treated Psalm 73 as the Psalter's masterclass in the warfare between faith and sense: Asaph's error, he said, was judging God's providence by the present moment, as a man might judge a whole book by one page; the sanctuary corrected him by teaching him to judge by the end. Spurgeon urged sufferers to notice where the cure happened — not in solitude with the problem, but in the assembly with the Lord — and he made verse 26 a deathbed text for the church: when flesh fails and heart fails, the believer's portion does not, because the portion is not a thing God gives but God Himself. That is the Reformation's doctrine of God in one verse: *God* is the portion. Everything else is luggage.

**Reflection question:** Whose prosperity has been preaching envy to you lately — and have you been doing your thinking about it alone, at the desk, or have you carried it into the sanctuary: to worship, to the Word, to the people of God? Take one specific envy to God this week and ask Him for Asaph's sight: their end, your portion, and the nearness that is better than both.

### Psalm 74 — Why Do You Cast Us Off Forever?

**Jesus in this psalm:** Asaph laments a destroyed sanctuary — enemies roaring in the meeting place, axes hacking the carved woodwork, the temple burned to the ground, "there is no longer any prophet" and no sign. It is the prayer of a people standing in smoking ruins asking, "How long, O God, is the foe to scoff?" Jesus stands in this psalm as the greater temple: the sanctuary men destroyed in three days and God raised in three days (John 2:19–21), and as the King the psalm pleads with — "Yet God my King is from of old, working salvation in the midst of the earth." Every church that has ever stood in ruins — burned, closed, scattered — has prayed Psalm 74, and its answer is that the King from of old is not finished working salvation in the midst of the earth.

**The key verse:**

> "Yet God my King is from of old, working salvation in the midst of the earth." — Psalm 74:12 (ESV)

"Yet" — in the middle of the ashes, that is faith's favorite word. The ruins are facts; so is the King. And His specialty, from of old, is working salvation not at the edges of the disaster but in the midst of it.

### Psalm 75 — It Is God Who Executes Judgment

**Jesus in this psalm:** A psalm of God's certain judgment: the boastful are told not to lift up their horns, the wicked are warned that promotion comes neither from east nor west — "but it is God who executes judgment, putting down one and lifting up another." In God's hand is a cup of foaming wine, mixed with spices, which the wicked of the earth shall drain to the dregs. Jesus took that cup's imagery into His own mouth — "the cup that I drink" — and in Gethsemane He prayed over the real cup of wrath and drank it, so that His people would never have to drain it. And Jesus is the Judge of this psalm's certainty: the horn that God exalts forever is His, cut off from no one, and every proud horn will meet Him.

**The key verse:**

> "But it is God who executes judgment, putting down one and lifting up another." — Psalm 75:7 (ESV)

Men rise and fall by a hand that is not theirs. That is terror to the proud and rest to the humble: you do not have to promote yourself, and you do not have to fear those who promoted themselves. The Judge is awake, and His calendar is not ours.

### Psalm 76 — In Judah God Is Known: The God Who Breaks the Weapons

**Jesus in this psalm:** After a great deliverance — most think of Sennacherib's army melting away in a night (2 Kings 19) — Asaph celebrates the God who makes Himself known in Judah by breaking the weapons of war: "There he broke the flashing arrows, the shield, the sword, and the weapons of war." The psalm's God is fearsome in judgment and gentle in purpose: He rises to save "all the humble of the earth," and even human wrath is made to praise Him — the wrath that survives His judgment He wears like a belt. Jesus is this God's fullest self-disclosure: the Prince who breaks the weapons of the final war — sin, death, and the devil — not by out-arming them but by disarming them at the cross, and who will one day make wars cease to the ends of the earth.

**The key verse:**

> "Surely the wrath of man shall praise you; the remnant of wrath you will put on like a belt." — Psalm 76:10 (ESV)

Nothing men do in rage escapes God's use of it. The wrath He permits becomes His praise; the wrath He doesn't, He restrains like a belted sword. There is no loose fury in the universe — all of it is either fuel for His glory or stopped by His hand.

### Psalm 77 — I Will Remember the Deeds of the LORD

**Jesus in this psalm:** Asaph's sleepless psalm: "I cry aloud to God… In the day of my trouble I seek the Lord; in the night my hand is stretched out without wearying; my soul refuses to be comforted." His questions are the ones whispered at 3 a.m.: "Will the Lord spurn forever?… Has God forgotten to be gracious?" His turn is not a change of circumstances but a decision of the will: "I will remember the deeds of the LORD; yes, I will remember your wonders of old. I will ponder all your work." Memory becomes medicine — the Red Sea, the thunder, the God who led His people "like a flock by the hand of Moses and Aaron." Jesus is the greater Exodus this remembering points to, and the Good Shepherd who leads the flock now. When feelings refuse comfort, deeds remain: the psalm teaches the sufferer to argue from God's résumé.

**The key verse:**

> "I will remember the deeds of the LORD; yes, I will remember your wonders of old." — Psalm 77:11 (ESV)

"I will" — memory, in this psalm, is not nostalgia; it is warfare. When the night refuses to be comforted, do not wait to feel God's faithfulness. Recite it — out loud, in order, by name — until the reciting does its work.

### Psalm 78 — Tell the Coming Generation

**Jesus in this psalm:** The great history psalm — Asaph retells Israel's story from Egypt to David: plagues, sea, manna, quail, rebellion upon rebellion, judgment and mercy upon mercy — all so that "the next generation" might set their hope in God and not be like their fathers. Its purpose is stated up front: "We will not hide them from their children, but tell to the coming generation the glorious deeds of the LORD." Jesus stands in this psalm twice: Matthew quotes its opening — "I will open my mouth in a parable" — of Jesus' teaching (Matthew 13:35), the true Asaph telling God's story in parables; and the story itself, of a God who keeps choosing and forgiving a stubborn people, finds its climax in the Son of David whom God chose at the psalm's end — the shepherd-king whose greater Son shepherds the flock forever.

**The key verse:**

> "We will not hide them from their children, but tell to the coming generation the glorious deeds of the LORD, and his might, and the wonders that he has done." — Psalm 78:4 (ESV)

Every generation of God's people is one generation from forgetting — unless the stories get told. Your testimony is not yours to keep. Somebody's children are waiting on the other side of your telling.

### Psalm 79 — Help Us, O God of Our Salvation, for the Glory of Your Name

**Jesus in this psalm:** Jerusalem is in ruins, the temple defiled, the dead unburied, the neighbors mocking — "Where is their God?" Asaph's prayer is notable for its ground: not Israel's merit, which he does not claim, but God's glory: "Help us, O God of our salvation, for the glory of your name; deliver us, and atone for our sins, for your name's sake!" Jesus is the atonement this psalm asks for — the One by whom God atones for the sins of a ruined people for His name's sake — and He is the shepherd of its last line: "But we your people, the sheep of your pasture, will give thanks to you forever." The sheep survive the slaughterhouse of history because the Shepherd does.

**The key verse:**

> "Help us, O God of our salvation, for the glory of your name; deliver us, and atone for our sins, for your name's sake!" — Psalm 79:9 (ESV)

"For your name's sake" is the strongest plea a sinner can make, because it asks God to act for the reason He most loves to act: His own glory in showing mercy. When you have no merit to plead — plead His name. It has never been refused.

### Psalm 80 — Restore Us, O God: The Vine and the Son of Man

**Jesus in this psalm:** Three times the refrain rises, each time brighter: "Restore us, O God; let your face shine, that we may be saved!" — then "O God of hosts" — then "O LORD God of hosts." The psalm's image is a vine brought out of Egypt, planted, grown to fill the land — and now ravaged, its walls broken, the boar from the forest wasting it. The prayer narrows to one Man: "Let your hand be on the man of your right hand, the son of man whom you have made strong for yourself!" Jesus is that Son of Man — the true Vine (John 15) in whom the ravaged vine of Israel is replanted and made fruitful again, the Man at God's right hand through whom the face of God shines on His people and saves them.

**The key verse:**

> "Restore us, O God; let your face shine, that we may be saved!" — Psalm 80:3 (ESV)

Restoration, in this psalm, is not first about circumstances; it is about a face — God's face, turned toward His people again in favor. When the face shines, the vine revives. Everything else is gardening.

---

### Psalm 81 — Open Your Mouth Wide, and I Will Fill It

**Jesus in this psalm:** A festival psalm that becomes a sermon: God reminds His people of the Exodus — "I relieved your shoulder of the burden" — and then laments the saddest sound in the Psalter: "But my people did not listen to my voice… So I gave them over to their stubborn hearts." Against that grief stands one of the Bible's most generous promises: "I am the LORD your God, who brought you up out of the land of Egypt. Open your mouth wide, and I will fill it." Jesus is the bread with which God fills the open mouth — "I am the bread of life; whoever comes to me shall not hunger" — and He is the voice the psalm begs Israel to hear: "Oh, that my people would listen to me." The tragedy of Psalm 81 is a full table and closed ears. The promise stands open still.

**The key verse:**

> "I am the LORD your God, who brought you up out of the land of Egypt. Open your mouth wide, and I will fill it." — Psalm 81:10 (ESV)

God's complaint is not that His people ask too much, but that they open their mouths too little. The condition of being filled is not worthiness; it is openness — a mouth wide, like a nestling's, trusting the hand that feeds.

### Psalm 82 — Arise, O God, Judge the Earth

**Jesus in this psalm:** God stands in His own council and puts the lesser "gods" — the rulers and judges of the earth — on trial: "How long will you judge unjustly and show partiality to the wicked? Give justice to the weak and the fatherless; maintain the right of the afflicted and the destitute." Their sentence is mortality: "You are gods, sons of the Most High, all of you; nevertheless, like men you shall die." Jesus quoted this psalm in John 10, in the very act of claiming to be the Son of God — the true Judge standing among the false ones — and He is the answer to its closing cry: "Arise, O God, judge the earth; for you shall inherit all the nations!" He has arisen; He is inheriting; and every unjust bench on earth is a temporary court beneath His permanent one.

**The key verse:**

> "Arise, O God, judge the earth; for you shall inherit all the nations!" — Psalm 82:8 (ESV)

When the courts of men fail the fatherless, this verse is the appeal that cannot be dismissed. The nations are an inheritance, and the Heir has come of age. Unjust judges are squatters in His courtroom, and their lease is short.

### Psalm 83 — O God, Do Not Keep Silence

**Jesus in this psalm:** A confederacy of nations conspires to wipe Israel off the map — "Come, let us wipe them out as a nation; let the name of Israel be remembered no more!" — and Asaph begs God not to keep silence, not to be still, not to be quiet. The prayer that follows is fierce: make them like whirling dust, like chaff, pursue them with Your tempest — "that they may know that you alone, whose name is the LORD, are the Most High over all the earth." Jesus stands behind this psalm as the LORD whose name is at stake in His people's survival, and ahead of it as the King whose church has faced the same conspiracy in every age — and outlived every coalition, because the Most High does not keep silence forever. His silences are pauses, not surrenders.

**The key verse:**

> "That they may know that you alone, whose name is the LORD, are the Most High over all the earth." — Psalm 83:18 (ESV)

Even the imprecations have a missionary aim: *that they may know*. God's judgments are never mere destruction; they are declarations — to enemies and to the watching world — of who is Most High. The church prays this psalm so that the knowledge, not just the victory, will come.

## Psalm 84 — How Lovely Is Your Dwelling Place

### Jesus in this psalm

Jesus is the dwelling place of God that Psalm 84 longs for. The psalmist aches for the courts of the LORD — envies the sparrow and the swallow who nest at God's altars — because the temple is where God is. In Christ, that presence has come out of the courts and into the world: "The Word became flesh and dwelt among us" — literally, *tabernacled* among us — and Jesus pointed to His own body and said, "Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up." Now the longing of Psalm 84 is answered in a Person and a people: Christ dwells in His church by His Spirit, and the believer's heart, far from Jerusalem, is itself a court of the LORD. And the psalm's pilgrim road — the Valley of Baca, the valley of weeping, turned into a place of springs — is the road Jesus walked ahead of us and walks beside us, until we appear before God in the Zion that is above.

### Commentary

**The longing (84:1–4).** The psalm opens with an exclamation that is half praise and half homesickness: "How lovely is your dwelling place, O LORD of hosts! My soul longs, yes, faints for the courts of the LORD; my heart and flesh sing for joy to the living God." Longs — faints — the whole person, heart and flesh together, pulled toward the presence of God like a plant toward light. And then the famous envy: "Even the sparrow finds a home, and the swallow a nest for herself, where she may lay her young, at your altars, O LORD of hosts, my King and my God." Sparrows — worthless birds, two for a penny — live at the altars rent-free, raising their young in the very house the psalmist can only visit. He does not begrudge them; he learns from them: if God houses sparrows at His altar, the longing of His children will not go unhoused. "Blessed are those who dwell in your house, ever singing your praise!" — the first beatitude of the psalm, and it belongs to the residents.

**The pilgrims (84:5–7).** The second beatitude belongs to the travelers: "Blessed are those whose strength is in you, in whose heart are the highways to Zion." Strength in God, and highways in the heart — the pilgrim's inner map is already drawn toward God before his feet move. And the road goes through the valley: "As they go through the Valley of Baca they make it a place of springs; the early rain also covers it with pools." Baca is the valley of weeping — and the pilgrims *make it* a place of springs. They do not merely survive the weeping valley; they dig wells in it, so that those who come after find water where they found tears. "They go from strength to strength; each one appears before God in Zion." Not strength to exhaustion, not strength to collapse — strength to *strength*, the supply renewing as the road lengthens, until the appearing. Every pilgrim who has walked with God a long time knows this arithmetic: the strength was never in the walker; it was renewed at each stage because the destination kept giving it.

**Better is one day (84:8–12).** The prayer rises: "O LORD God of hosts, hear my prayer; give ear, O God of Jacob! Behold our shield, O God; look on the face of your anointed!" — a prayer for the king, the shield of the people, folded into the pilgrim's own longing. And then the psalm's most quoted sentence:

> "For a day in your courts is better than a thousand elsewhere. I would rather be a doorkeeper in the house of my God than dwell in the tents of wickedness." — Psalm 84:10 (ESV)

One day against a thousand — nearly three years of the world's best, weighed against twenty-four hours in God's courts, and the day wins. Doorkeeper — the lowest post in the temple, the man at the threshold — against the comfort of the tents of wickedness: the threshold wins. This is the arithmetic of a man who has tasted both. And the psalm closes with God's character as the ground of it all: "For the LORD God is a sun and shield; the LORD bestows favor and honor. No good thing does he withhold from those who walk uprightly. O LORD of hosts, blessed is the one who trusts in you!" *No good thing withheld* — not no thing, but no *good* thing: the God who is sun and shield may withhold what would harm us and call it mercy, but every genuinely good thing is on its way to those who walk with Him. The psalm ends where trust begins: blessed is the one who trusts in You.

### Key verses

> "How lovely is your dwelling place, O LORD of hosts! My soul longs, yes, faints for the courts of the LORD; my heart and flesh sing for joy to the living God." — Psalm 84:1–2 (ESV)

> "Blessed are those whose strength is in you, in whose heart are the highways to Zion." — Psalm 84:5 (ESV)

> "As they go through the Valley of Baca they make it a place of springs; the early rain also covers it with pools." — Psalm 84:6 (ESV)

> "For a day in your courts is better than a thousand elsewhere. I would rather be a doorkeeper in the house of my God than dwell in the tents of wickedness." — Psalm 84:10 (ESV)

> "For the LORD God is a sun and shield; the LORD bestows favor and honor. No good thing does he withhold from those who walk uprightly." — Psalm 84:11 (ESV)

### The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Psalm 84 is that we are, by nature, pilgrims shut out — the courts of God are lovely, and our sin had barred the door; our hearts, left to themselves, grow highways to everywhere except Zion, and our valleys of weeping stay valleys of weeping. A doorkeeper's place in God's house was more than any of us could claim. But the good news is that the dwelling place of God has come to us. Jesus Christ tabernacled among us, tore the veil by His death, and opened the courts to everyone who trusts in Him — not as doorkeepers tolerated at the threshold, but as children at home, with the Spirit of God dwelling in them on the road. Now the highways to Zion can be in your heart tonight, the Valley of Baca can become springs for the people coming behind you, and no good thing will be withheld — because the best thing, God Himself, has already been given.

**A note from the Reformers.** Spurgeon called Psalm 84 the pearl of the Psalms and said its longing rebukes the coldness of comfortable Christians: a man exiled from the assembly of God's people, fainting for what we yawn through, is a standing sermon to the church. Calvin dwelt on the sparrows: if God prepares a home for worthless birds at His altars, he argued, how much more will He not leave His own children homeless — the argument from the lesser to the greater that Jesus Himself loved to use. And the Reformers together made much of the doorkeeper: in Christ's church, the lowest place in God's house outranks the highest place outside it — a sentence that has comforted obscure, unnoticed believers for five hundred years.

**Reflection question:** Where are the highways in your heart actually pointing right now — and what is your Valley of Baca, the weeping place you are passing through? This week, dig one well in it for whoever comes after you: one prayer, one testimony, one act of praise in the valley itself — and say verse 10 over your circumstances until the arithmetic convinces you: one day in His courts is better than a thousand elsewhere.

### Psalm 85 — Revive Us Again

**Jesus in this psalm:** A psalm of a restored people asking for the restoration to be completed: "You forgave the iniquity of your people; you covered all their sin" — past mercy remembered — "Restore us again, O God of our salvation… Will you not revive us again, that your people may rejoice in you?" Its center is one of the Bible's great pictures of the gospel: "Steadfast love and faithfulness meet; righteousness and peace kiss each other." Where do steadfast love and faithfulness, righteousness and peace, meet and kiss? The church's answer is: at the cross of Jesus, where God is both just and the justifier, where righteousness does not destroy peace but embraces it. Psalm 85 is revival prayed for by people who know where revivals come from — not from technique, but from the meeting of God's attributes in mercy — and its answer arrived in a Person.

**The key verse:**

> "Steadfast love and faithfulness meet; righteousness and peace kiss each other." — Psalm 85:10 (ESV)

In us, love and truth are always quarreling; peace and righteousness seem to cancel each other. At the cross they kiss. That is why the gospel can be preached without lowering God's holiness or abandoning the sinner — both were kept, in an embrace, on a hill outside Jerusalem.

### Psalm 86 — Teach Me Your Way, O LORD: The Poor and Needy Prayer

**Jesus in this psalm:** Uniquely, this is "A Prayer of David" set among the Korah psalms of Book Three — David's voice in the dark book. He prays as "poor and needy," asks to be taught ("Teach me your way, O LORD, that I may walk in your truth; unite my heart to fear your name"), and grounds everything in God's self-proclaimed character: "But you, O Lord, are a God merciful and gracious, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness" — the creed of Exodus 34, prayed back to the God who spoke it. Jesus is the merciful and gracious Lord of this psalm in person, and its prophecy is His mission: "All the nations you have made shall come and worship before you, O Lord" — the nations streaming in, which began at Pentecost and will end with every knee bowed. The united heart David begged for, Jesus gives by His Spirit.

**The key verse:**

> "Teach me your way, O LORD, that I may walk in your truth; unite my heart to fear your name." — Psalm 86:11 (ESV)

*Unite my heart* — David's diagnosis is that his heart is a crowd, pulled in pieces. The prayer of the divided man is not for more information but for unification: one heart, one fear, one way. God still answers that prayer; it is half of sanctification in a single sentence.

### Psalm 87 — Glorious Things of You Are Spoken, O City of God

**Jesus in this psalm:** The shortest and strangest of the Zion psalms: a register of births. God Himself keeps the census of His city, and the astonishing thing is who is in it — Rahab (Egypt) and Babylon, Philistia and Tyre and Cush: "This one was born there." The enemies of Israel, written into Zion's birth register by God's own hand. Jesus is the fulfillment of this register: in Him, the God of Israel adopts the nations into His city — "you are no longer strangers and aliens, but fellow citizens with the saints" — and the book of life is exactly this, a birth register kept by God, full of unlikely hometowns. Glorious things are spoken of this city because of who does the registering, and who was born in a stable to make the registration possible.

**The key verse:**

> "Glorious things of you are spoken, O city of God." — Psalm 87:3 (ESV)

The glory of the city is not her walls but her register — the names God has written there, from places no one expected. If Babylon can be in the book, your hometown can. If Rahab can be born there, so can you — and in Christ, you have been.

### Psalm 88 — The Darkest Psalm: O LORD, God of My Salvation

**Jesus in this psalm:** Psalm 88 is the one psalm that never turns. Heman the Ezrahite cries day and night, counts himself among the dead, feels God's wrath lying heavy, is shut in and cannot escape, watches loved ones and friends withdraw — and the psalm's last word, in the Hebrew ordering of the thought, is "darkness": "my companions have become darkness." There is no praise at the end, no "yet I will rejoice." And that is precisely why this psalm is in the Bible — and why it matters that Jesus prayed in this register and went further into the dark than Heman ever did: forsaken, wrath-bearing, cut off from the land of the living, with darkness as His only companion. The church keeps Psalm 88 because some nights have no turn in them this side of morning — and because the God who let this prayer into His book has shown, in the resurrection of the One who prayed its depths, that He hears even the prayers that end in darkness. Heman's psalm is unfinished. Easter finished it.

**The key verse:**

> "O LORD, God of my salvation, I cry out day and night before you. Let my prayer come before you; incline your ear to my cry!" — Psalm 88:1–2 (ESV)

Even here — especially here — he calls Him "God of my salvation." The title is faith's last grip, and it holds. If your prayer tonight has no praise in it, only crying, pray this psalm as written: it is Scripture, it is addressed to the God of salvation, and He inclines His ear to exactly this kind of cry.

## Psalm 89 — Where Is Your Steadfast Love of Old? The Covenant Question

### Jesus in this psalm

Jesus is the answer to Psalm 89's unanswered question. The psalm spends its first thirty-seven verses celebrating God's covenant with David — "I have made a covenant with my chosen one; I have sworn to David my servant: 'I will establish your offspring forever, and build your throne for all generations'" — and its last movement standing in the ruins of that covenant: the crown in the dust, the throne overturned, the king shamed, and the plea, "Lord, where is your steadfast love of old, which by your faithfulness you swore to David?" The psalm ends without an answer — Book Three ends without an answer. The answer came centuries later, in a manger in David's town and an empty tomb outside Jerusalem: the offspring of David raised imperishable, the throne built for all generations by resurrection. God kept the covenant the only way it could finally be kept — in a Son of David who could not die again. Psalm 89 is the Psalter's great open question, and the New Testament is God's reply: the steadfast love of old is exactly where it always was — on oath — and it is now located in a risen King.

### Commentary

**I will sing of the steadfast love of the LORD, forever (89:1–4).** Ethan the Ezrahite begins with a vow to sing — and what he sings first is the covenant: "I will sing of the steadfast love of the LORD forever; with my mouth I will make known your faithfulness to all generations." The covenant is quoted in God's own words: "I have made a covenant with my chosen one; I have sworn to David my servant: 'I will establish your offspring forever, and build your throne for all generations.'" Sworn. Forever. Twice. The psalm plants the promise as deep as it can go before it shows us the ruins — because the whole weight of the psalm's lament depends on the promise being real. A broken promise only hurts if it was truly made.

**Who in the skies can be compared to the LORD? (89:5–18).** The middle of the psalm is pure praise of God's incomparable power and character: the heavens praise His wonders; He rules the raging of the sea; He crushed Rahab (Egypt) like a carcass; heaven and earth are His; righteousness and justice are the foundation of His throne, and steadfast love and faithfulness go before Him. "Blessed are the people who know the festal shout, who walk, O LORD, in the light of your face." This is not filler. Ethan is building the case for his lament the only way lament can be built: on the character of the God he is about to question. The God who founded His throne on righteousness and justice cannot be indifferent to a broken oath. Ethan knows it. He is reminding God — and himself — of it.

**You have spoken in a vision (89:19–37).** Then the covenant is rehearsed at full length — the longest telling of the Davidic promise in the Psalter: God found David, anointed him with holy oil, promised to set his hand on the sea, to make him the firstborn, the highest of the kings of the earth; "My steadfast love I will keep for him forever, and my covenant will stand firm for him. I will establish his offspring forever and his throne as the days of the heavens." Even the discipline clause is merciful: if David's sons forsake God's law, God will punish their transgression with the rod — "but I will not remove from him my steadfast love or be false to my faithfulness. I will not violate my covenant or alter the word that went forth from my lips. Once for all I have sworn by my holiness; I will not lie to David." Once for all. By His holiness. *I will not lie to David.* Ethan lays the oath on the table in full — and then turns the page.

**But now you have cast off (89:38–45).** "But now you have cast off and rejected; you are full of wrath against your anointed. You have renounced the covenant with your servant; you have defiled his crown in the dust." The king — in Ethan's day, likely Jehoiachin or the last kings of Judah — is shamed: his throne overturned, his strongholds ruined, his enemies rejoicing, his youth cut short, covered with shame. Every clause contradicts an earlier clause of the same psalm. Offspring forever — but the king is childless in exile. Throne as the days of the heavens — but the throne is in the dust. Steadfast love kept forever — but wrath is what we see. Psalm 89 does not resolve this contradiction. It *files* it — as a formal complaint, in God's own court, citing God's own oath. That is what biblical lament is: not unbelief, but the refusal to let go of the promise when the evidence turns.

**How long, O LORD? (89:46–52).** "How long, O LORD? Will you hide yourself forever? How long will your wrath burn like fire? Remember how short my time is!" And then the question that ends Book Three: "Lord, where is your steadfast love of old, which by your faithfulness you swore to David?" No answer is recorded. The psalm — and the book — close with the doxology, praise stapled, as it were, to an unanswered question: "Blessed be the LORD forever! Amen and Amen." That is one of the most instructive endings in the Bible: God's people can bless Him in the dark, with the question still open, because the question is addressed to the One who swore. The answer to Psalm 89 is not in Psalm 89. It is in Luke 1, where an angel tells a virgin that her Son will receive "the throne of his father David" and reign "forever" — Gabriel is Ethan's answer, delivered centuries late and exactly on time. And it is in Acts 13, where Paul preaches the resurrection as God's keeping of "the holy and sure blessings of David." The crown that lay in the dust is on a living head now, and it will never be defiled again.

### Key verses

> "I will sing of the steadfast love of the LORD forever; with my mouth I will make known your faithfulness to all generations." — Psalm 89:1 (ESV)

> "I have made a covenant with my chosen one; I have sworn to David my servant: 'I will establish your offspring forever, and build your throne for all generations.'" — Psalm 89:3–4 (ESV)

> "I will not violate my covenant or alter the word that went forth from my lips. Once for all I have sworn by my holiness; I will not lie to David." — Psalm 89:34–35 (ESV)

> "But now you have cast off and rejected; you are full of wrath against your anointed. You have renounced the covenant with your servant; you have defiled his crown in the dust." — Psalm 89:38–39 (ESV)

> "Lord, where is your steadfast love of old, which by your faithfulness you swore to David?" — Psalm 89:49 (ESV)

### The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Psalm 89 is that God's people can come to a place where the promise and the evidence flatly contradict each other — where the crown is in the dust, the throne is overturned, and the most honest prayer available is "Where is your steadfast love of old?" The psalm does not rebuke that prayer; it canonizes it, which means God expects His people to have such seasons and has given them words for the inside of them. But the good news is that the oath outlived the ruins. God did not lie to David: He raised up David's offspring — Jesus Christ, crucified under the curse the covenant's discipline clause described, and raised to the throne the covenant promised — and in Him the steadfast love of old is not a memory but a reign. If you are praying Ethan's question tonight, you are praying a question God has already answered in the resurrection; the answer just may not have reached your circumstances yet. Hold the oath. He has never once altered the word that went forth from His lips.

**A note from the Reformers.** Calvin honored Psalm 89 as proof that the church's faith rests on God's promise, not on present appearances: Ethan, he said, does not deny the ruin or the oath — he sets them side by side and pleads the oath against the ruin, which is the exact shape of faith in a fallen world. The Reformers also noted the discipline clause with gratitude: God promised to punish David's sons' sins with the rod but never to remove His steadfast love — a distinction between fatherly discipline and covenant rejection that carried many a believer through seasons when they could not tell the difference. And they read the psalm's ending as instruction: when God has not yet answered, the church's last word is still "Blessed be the LORD forever." Amen and Amen is not denial. It is defiance — faith blessing God in advance of the explanation.

**Reflection question:** What promise of God looks contradicted by your circumstances right now — and have you filed it, Ethan-style, as a formal plea: the oath quoted back to the God who swore it? This week, write down one promise and one ruin, side by side, and pray the psalm's question honestly — then close, as Book Three closes, with the doxology anyway: "Blessed be the LORD forever! Amen and Amen."

## Psalm 90 — Teach Us to Number Our Days: The Prayer of Moses

### Jesus in this psalm

Jesus is the dwelling place and the eternal life that Moses' prayer reaches for. Moses, who watched a whole generation die in the wilderness, prays as a man surrounded by death: "You return man to dust… The years of our life are seventy, or eighty if we are strong; yet their span is but toil and trouble; they are soon gone, and we fly away." Against that brevity he sets one hope: "Lord, you have been our dwelling place in all generations… From everlasting to everlasting you are God." Jesus is that dwelling place made flesh — the eternal God in a mortal body — and He is the answer to Moses' arithmetic: He numbered His own days perfectly, finished the work given Him, and rose to give His people days that do not end. "Teach us to number our days that we may get a heart of wisdom" finds its completion in the One who teaches us, by His resurrection, to number them toward eternity. And Moses' closing plea — "Let the favor of the Lord our God be upon us, and establish the work of our hands" — is answered in Christ, in whom our labor is not in vain and the work of our hands is established beyond the grave.

### Commentary

**Our dwelling place in all generations (90:1–2).** Book Four opens with the oldest psalm in the book — "A Prayer of Moses, the man of God" — and Moses begins not with himself but with God's eternity: "Lord, you have been our dwelling place in all generations. Before the mountains were brought forth, or ever you had formed the earth and the world, from everlasting to everlasting you are God." A dwelling place — through forty years of wilderness, with no land, no houses, no graves they could point to as home, God's people had an address: the LORD Himself. Moses, who never entered the promised land, knew the secret: the land was never the dwelling place. God was. Before the mountains — before the oldest, most permanent things men know — God *is*, present tense, from everlasting to everlasting. Every human story in this psalm is short; the frame around them all is not.

**Dust, grass, and a thousand years (90:3–6).** "You return man to dust and say, 'Return, O children of man!'" Genesis 3, quoted by the man of Genesis' sequel: dust you are, and the sentence stands. "For a thousand years in your sight are but as yesterday when it is past, or as a watch in the night." To God, a millennium is a yesterday — or less, a three-hour watch in the night. And man, by contrast, is grass: "You sweep them away as with a flood; they are like a dream, like grass that is renewed in the morning: in the morning it flourishes and is renewed; in the evening it fades and withers." Flourish at breakfast, withered by supper — that is the human span, set beside God's. Moses is not being morbid. He is being accurate, and the accuracy is the beginning of the wisdom the psalm will ask for.

**Our iniquities before You (90:7–11).** Why do we die? Moses does not let the question stay abstract: "For we are brought to an end by your anger; by your wrath we are dismayed. You have set our iniquities before you, our secret sins in the light of your countenance." Death is not merely biology; it is judgment — God's wrath against sin, working itself out in dust and brevity. Our *secret* sins, the ones no one else has ever seen, stand in the light of His face. That is the psalm's darkest line, and its most honest. "The years of our life are seventy, or eighty if we are strong; yet their span is but toil and trouble; they are soon gone, and we fly away. Who considers the power of your anger, and your wrath according to the fear that is due you?" Almost nobody — that is the implied answer, and it is the problem verse 12 exists to fix.

**Teach us to number our days (90:12).** Here is the psalm's famous prayer, and it is worth seeing exactly what it asks:

> "So teach us to number our days that we may get a heart of wisdom." — Psalm 90:12 (ESV)

Not to number our *years* — years are too big to feel; a man can waste a decade and call it a phase. *Days.* Numbering our days means receiving each one as a counted, finite, unrepeatable gift — and the fruit of that arithmetic is not anxiety but *wisdom*, a heart that knows what matters. The man who knows his days are numbered stops spending them on what will not survive them. Moses, who had watched six hundred thousand graves dug in the sand, earned the right to teach us this prayer. It is the Bible's own answer to denial: we do not need to be told we will live forever. We need to be taught to count, so that we live wisely in the time that is actually ours.

**Satisfy us in the morning (90:13–17).** And then the psalm turns to pleading — "Return, O LORD! How long? Have pity on your servants!" — and lands on requests as warm as the opening was sobering:

> "Satisfy us in the morning with your steadfast love, that we may rejoice and be glad all our days. Make us glad for as many days as you have afflicted us, and for as many years as we have seen evil." — Psalm 90:14–15 (ESV)

Satisfy us *in the morning* — early, before the day's toil, with steadfast love as the meal the soul eats first. And the audacious arithmetic of verse 15: gladness measured against affliction, day for day, year for year — Moses asks God to balance the ledger in joy. The psalm ends with the work of our hands: "Let your work be shown to your servants, and your glorious power to their children. Let the favor of the Lord our God be upon us, and establish the work of our hands upon us; yes, establish the work of our hands!" Twice — because a man who knows he is dust knows his work is dust too, unless God establishes it. Moses built nothing that lasted in the wilderness except a people and a book — and both are still here. God establishes the work of hands that are open, mortal, and His.

### Key verses

> "Lord, you have been our dwelling place in all generations. Before the mountains were brought forth, or ever you had formed the earth and the world, from everlasting to everlasting you are God." — Psalm 90:1–2 (ESV)

> "For a thousand years in your sight are but as yesterday when it is past, or as a watch in the night." — Psalm 90:4 (ESV)

> "You have set our iniquities before you, our secret sins in the light of your countenance." — Psalm 90:8 (ESV)

> "So teach us to number our days that we may get a heart of wisdom." — Psalm 90:12 (ESV)

> "Satisfy us in the morning with your steadfast love, that we may rejoice and be glad all our days." — Psalm 90:14 (ESV)

> "Let the favor of the Lord our God be upon us, and establish the work of our hands upon us; yes, establish the work of our hands!" — Psalm 90:17 (ESV)

### The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Psalm 90 is that we are dust on a deadline: seventy or eighty years of toil and trouble, soon gone, and we fly away — and the brevity is not an accident of biology but the sentence of a holy God whose countenance exposes even our secret sins. No one numbers his days honestly and comes out comfortable. But the good news is that the eternal God has made Himself a dwelling place for mortal people — and in Jesus Christ He has done more: He has entered our brevity, numbered His own days among us, died our dust-death under the wrath our secret sins deserved, and risen to make our days endless and our work established. In Him, numbering our days is not morbid; it is the beginning of wisdom and even of gladness — because the days are counted by a Father, satisfied each morning by steadfast love, and followed by days that cannot be counted at all.

**A note from the Reformers.** Calvin called Psalm 90 a mirror in which every man may see his own mortality and be cured of the madness of living as if he would never die; the numbering of days, he said, is the schoolmaster that drives us from every false refuge to God alone. Spurgeon treasured the psalm's tenderness after its terror — the same Moses who wrote "you return man to dust" wrote "satisfy us in the morning with your steadfast love" — and he urged believers to pray verse 12 not once but habitually, as the daily cure for wasted life. The Reformers agreed on the order: eternity first, then brevity, then wisdom, then gladness. Get the frame right — from everlasting to everlasting, God — and the short picture inside it becomes, of all things, a reason to rejoice.

**Reflection question:** If you honestly numbered your days — not in fear, but in Moses' arithmetic — what would you stop spending them on, and what would you start? Take one day this week and live it deliberately numbered: satisfied in the morning with His steadfast love before anything else, and your hands' work consciously given to Him to establish. What changed?

## Psalm 91 — Under His Wings: The Psalm of Protection

### Jesus in this psalm

Jesus is the dwelling place of Psalm 91 — and He is also the psalm's tested pray-er, in a way that teaches us how to read it. In the wilderness temptation, Satan quoted this very psalm to Jesus — "He will command his angels concerning you… on their hands they will bear you up, lest you strike your foot against a stone" — urging Him to throw Himself from the temple and claim the protection presumptuously (Matthew 4:6). Jesus refused: "You shall not put the Lord your God to the test." That refusal is part of the psalm's meaning: Psalm 91 is a promise for the road of obedience, not a charm for stunts of pride. Jesus walked the road — through pestilence and terror, through the snare and the adder — trusting the Father without testing Him, and the Father kept Him through death itself and out the other side. In Christ, the psalm's promises belong to those who dwell — who make their home, not their experiment — in the shelter of the Most High.

### Commentary

**The shelter and the shadow (91:1–2).** The psalm begins with an address and a confession: "He who dwells in the shelter of the Most High will abide in the shadow of the Almighty. I will say to the LORD, 'My refuge and my fortress, my God, in whom I trust.'" *Dwells* — not visits, not camps for the weekend. The shelter of the Most High is a residence, and the shadow of the Almighty is what a resident lives under: the shadow of God's nearness, cast over those who stay close. And the confession is personal — *my* refuge, *my* fortress, *my* God — the psalm must be said in the first person before it can be leaned on. This is not a psalm about God's people in general being safe in theory. It is about a man who has moved in.

**The snare and the pestilence (91:3–6).** The dangers are named without flinching: "the snare of the fowler" — the trap set in secret; "deadly pestilence" — the disease no one sees coming; "the terror of the night"; "the arrow that flies by day"; "the pestilence that stalks in darkness"; "the destruction that wastes at noonday." Night and day, hidden and open, disease and war — the catalogue covers the whole clock and the whole map of human fear. And against all of it, one covering: "He will cover you with his pinions, and under his wings you will find refuge; his faithfulness is a shield and buckler." A mother bird's wings, and a soldier's shield — tenderness and armor in the same verse. The God of Psalm 91 is not less than either picture; He is both at once, and His faithfulness — not our courage — is the shield.

**A thousand may fall (91:7–13).** "A thousand may fall at your side, ten thousand at your right hand, but it will not come near you. You will only look with your eyes and see the recompense of the wicked." This is the verse that has been carried into battles and plagues by God's people for centuries — and it must be carried with the psalm's own condition: "Because you have made the LORD your dwelling place — the Most High, who is my refuge — no evil shall be allowed to befall you, no plague come near your tent." The promise is real, and it is addressed to dwellers. Then the angels: "For he will command his angels concerning you to guard you in all your ways. On their hands they will bear you up, lest you strike your foot against a stone." Satan quoted these verses accurately and applied them wickedly — protection turned into a dare. Jesus' refusal teaches us the difference between trusting God's guard and scripting God's miracles. The angels guard us *in all our ways* — the ways of obedience, the ordinary road — not in leaps we invent to force God's hand. "You will tread on the lion and the adder; the young lion and the serpent you will trample underfoot" — the protected man is not kept *from* the dangerous road; he is kept *on* it, walking over the very things that threatened him.

**Because he holds fast to me in love (91:14–16).** The psalm ends with God Himself speaking — the only voice that can make promises like these: "Because he holds fast to me in love, I will deliver him; I will protect him, because he knows my name. When he calls to me, I will answer him; I will be with him in trouble; I will rescue him and honor him. With long life I will satisfy him and show him my salvation." Note the conditions, all of them relational: holding fast *in love*, knowing *His name*, calling *to Me*. And note the center of the promises: "I will be with him *in trouble*" — not instead of trouble, but in it. Deliverance, presence, rescue, honor, long life, and at the end the greatest gift in the psalm: "I will show him my salvation." God's salvation, shown — that is what Jesus is. The psalm's last promise is a Person, displayed to everyone who holds fast in love.

### Key verses

> "He who dwells in the shelter of the Most High will abide in the shadow of the Almighty." — Psalm 91:1 (ESV)

> "He will cover you with his pinions, and under his wings you will find refuge; his faithfulness is a shield and buckler." — Psalm 91:4 (ESV)

> "You will not fear the terror of the night, nor the arrow that flies by day." — Psalm 91:5 (ESV)

> "For he will command his angels concerning you to guard you in all your ways." — Psalm 91:11 (ESV)

> "When he calls to me, I will answer him; I will be with him in trouble; I will rescue him and honor him. With long life I will satisfy him and show him my salvation." — Psalm 91:15–16 (ESV)

### The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Psalm 91 is that the dangers are not imaginary: snares are set, pestilence stalks, arrows fly, and terrors own the night — and a thousand do fall, at our side and at our right hand. This psalm has never been a promise that God's people are exempt from the world's troubles; Jesus Himself was quoted this psalm on the way to a cross. And presumption — using God's promises as a dare — is itself one of the snares. But the good news is that there is a shelter, and it is occupied. Jesus Christ dwells in the shelter of the Most High on our behalf and brings us in with Him: in Him, God is with us *in* trouble, answers when we call, commands His angels concerning us on the road of obedience, and will at last show us His salvation with our own eyes. The shadow of the Almighty is a real place. Move in. The wings are wide enough, and the faithfulness that shields is His, not yours.

**A note from the Reformers.** Spurgeon called Psalm 91 a psalm for plague-times and battlefields alike, and he was careful with its promises in the way Jesus was: they belong, he said, to those who *dwell* — the psalm is a house to live in, not a talisman to carry into presumption. Calvin likewise stressed the first verse as the key that locks or unlocks the rest: the protection is promised to the man whose ordinary residence is God — his trust, his love, his calling upon the name — and such a man, Calvin said, may walk through pestilence with a quiet heart, not because the disease is not deadly, but because his life is hid with God. Faith, in this psalm, is an address before it is an argument.

**Reflection question:** Where do you actually dwell — what is your soul's ordinary residence: the news, the fear, the what-ifs, or the shelter of the Most High? And is there any place where you have been tempted to use God's promises as a dare rather than a dwelling? Move one daily habit this week — the first ten minutes of the morning, say — into the shelter on purpose, and say verse 2 aloud until it sounds like your address: "My refuge and my fortress, my God, in whom I trust."

### Psalm 92 — It Is Good to Give Thanks: The Sabbath Psalm

**Jesus in this psalm:** A psalm for the Sabbath day — the only psalm with a day of the week in its title — and its theme is the goodness of gratitude: "It is good to give thanks to the LORD, to sing praises to your name, O Most High; to declare your steadfast love in the morning and your faithfulness by night." Morning love, night faithfulness — a whole day, and a whole life, bookended by the character of God. Jesus is the Lord of the Sabbath this psalm serves, and its picture of the flourishing righteous — "They still bear fruit in old age; they are ever full of sap and green" — is His promise to His planted people: fruitfulness is not a young man's game in the house of the LORD. Planted in His house, the gray-haired flourish.

**The key verse:**

> "The righteous flourish like the palm tree and grow like a cedar in Lebanon. They are planted in the house of the LORD; they flourish in the courts of our God." — Psalm 92:12–13 (ESV)

Flourishing is a location before it is a condition: *planted in the house of the LORD*. Uprooted Christians wither and blame the weather. Stay planted — in the Word, in the assembly, in the courts — and the sap keeps rising, in every season, into old age.

### Psalm 93 — The LORD Reigns: Robed in Majesty

**Jesus in this psalm:** The first of the great reign psalms, and the shortest: "The LORD reigns; he is robed in majesty." The floods lift up their voice — the sea, chaos, the raging of nations — "mightier than the thunders of many waters… is the LORD on high." His throne is established from of old; He is from everlasting; His decrees stand firm; holiness befits His house forever. Jesus is this reigning LORD in the flesh — the One who rebuked the floods and they obeyed, proving on Galilee what this psalm sings about the throne — and His resurrection and ascension are the public installation of the reign Psalm 93 announces. When the floods are loudest, this psalm is five verses of ballast: the throne is older than the flood, and it is occupied.

**The key verse:**

> "The LORD reigns; he is robed in majesty; the LORD is robed; he has put on strength as a belt. Indeed, the world is established; it shall never be moved." — Psalm 93:1 (ESV)

The world feels movable — shaken daily by news and loss. The psalm says otherwise: established, never moved, because the Reigner is robed and belted with strength. Your sense of shaking is real; so is the establishment underneath it. One of them is older.

### Psalm 94 — O LORD, God of Vengeance: The Judge Who Hears the Cries

**Jesus in this psalm:** A psalm for the oppressed who are tired of waiting: "O LORD, God of vengeance… Rise up, O judge of the earth; repay to the proud what they deserve! How long shall the wicked exult?" The proud crush God's people, murder the widow and the sojourner, and say, "The LORD does not see." The psalm answers with theology — the God who planted the ear hears; the God who formed the eye sees — and with testimony: "When the cares of my heart are many, your consolations cheer my soul… the LORD has become my stronghold." Jesus is the Judge this psalm awaits, who will repay and who meanwhile consoles; and He is the proof that God hears — the Father heard the cries of His crushed Son, and raised Him, and will not finally ignore the cries of any of His crushed children.

**The key verse:**

> "When the cares of my heart are many, your consolations cheer my soul." — Psalm 94:19 (ESV)

Many cares, many consolations — God matches the count. The verse does not say the cares went away; it says the consolations cheered *in the middle of the many*. That is the usual order of God's comfort: not subtraction of trouble, but addition of Himself.

## Psalm 95 — Today, If You Hear His Voice: The Warning of the Wilderness

### Jesus in this psalm

Jesus is the voice of Psalm 95 — and the rest the psalm's warning is about. Hebrews 3–4 takes this psalm as its text and preaches Christ from it: the "today" of the psalm is still today; the voice speaking is the Holy Spirit's; and the rest that Israel forfeited in the wilderness is the rest that remains open, entered by faith in Jesus — "For we who have believed enter that rest." Jesus is also the great God and King above all gods whom the psalm calls us to worship, the Maker of the sea and the dry land, the Shepherd whose sheep we are — "we are the people of his pasture, and the sheep of his hand." And He is the anti-Meribah: where Israel tested God in the wilderness and hardened, Jesus was tested in the wilderness and trusted — so that hard-hearted testers could be forgiven and soft-hearted believers could enter His rest.

### Commentary

**Come, let us sing (95:1–5).** The psalm opens as an invitation to worship, shouted: "Oh come, let us sing to the LORD; let us make a joyful noise to the rock of our salvation! Let us come into his presence with thanksgiving; let us make a joyful noise to him with songs of praise!" Worship here is loud, corporate, and thankful — and it is grounded in theology: "For the LORD is a great God, and a great King above all gods. In his hand are the depths of the earth; the heights of the mountains are his also. The sea is his, for he made it, and his hands formed the dry land." Depths and heights, sea and land — the whole vertical and horizontal of creation is in His hand and from His hand. This is the God we are invited to make a joyful noise to: not a tribal deity, not a household god, but the Maker of the abyss and the Alps.

**Come, let us worship and bow down (95:6–7).** The second invitation goes lower: "Oh come, let us worship and bow down; let us kneel before the LORD, our Maker! For he is our God, and we are the people of his pasture, and the sheep of his hand." Singing stands; worship kneels. And the ground of the kneeling is covenant: He is *our* God; we are *His* people — His pasture, His sheep, in His hand. The Maker of the depths of the earth is the Shepherd of this flock; omnipotence and tenderness are the same God. That combination — the hand that formed the dry land is the hand that holds the sheep — is the whole comfort of the Christian life in one verse.

**Today — do not harden your hearts (95:7–11).** And then, mid-worship, the psalm turns into a warning that has thundered down the centuries:

> "Today, if you hear his voice, do not harden your hearts, as at Meribah, as on the day at Massah in the wilderness, when your fathers put me to the test and put me to the proof, though they had seen my work." — Psalm 95:7–9 (ESV)

Meribah and Massah — the place where Israel, freshly delivered from Egypt, quarreled and tested: "Is the LORD among us or not?" (Exodus 17). They had *seen His work* — the plagues, the sea, the manna — and tested Him anyway. Evidence is not the cure for hardness; the generation with the most evidence fell in the wilderness. "For forty years I loathed that generation and said, 'They are a people who go astray in their heart, and they have not known my ways.' Therefore I swore in my wrath, 'They shall not enter my rest.'" The warning is terrifying precisely because the people warned were the redeemed people — brought out of Egypt, fed in the wilderness, and shut out of rest by unbelief. Hebrews takes the warning at full strength and aims it at the church: "Take care, brothers, lest there be in any of you an evil, unbelieving heart, leading you to fall away from the living God" (Hebrews 3:12).

**The rest that remains.** But the psalm's warning, read through Hebrews, has a gospel inside it: the fact that God still says "Today" means the door is still open. David, centuries after Joshua, still says *today* — so the rest was never exhausted by Canaan; a Sabbath rest remains for the people of God, and it is entered the opposite way from Meribah: not by testing God, but by trusting Him — by believing the voice instead of hardening against it. "So then, there remains a Sabbath rest for the people of God, for whoever has entered God's rest has also rested from his works as God did from his. Let us therefore strive to enter that rest" (Hebrews 4:9–11). Strive to rest — the Christian life's holy paradox. Psalm 95 begins with singing and ends with a sworn oath; it teaches us that worship and warning belong in the same service, and that the time to bow down and believe is never tomorrow. It is today.

### Key verses

> "Oh come, let us sing to the LORD; let us make a joyful noise to the rock of our salvation!" — Psalm 95:1 (ESV)

> "Oh come, let us worship and bow down; let us kneel before the LORD, our Maker! For he is our God, and we are the people of his pasture, and the sheep of his hand." — Psalm 95:6–7 (ESV)

> "Today, if you hear his voice, do not harden your hearts, as at Meribah, as on the day at Massah in the wilderness." — Psalm 95:7–8 (ESV)

> "Therefore I swore in my wrath, 'They shall not enter my rest.'" — Psalm 95:11 (ESV)

### The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Psalm 95 is that it is possible to be delivered from Egypt, fed with manna, and led by the pillar — and still fall in the wilderness with an unbelieving heart. Seeing God's work is not the same as trusting God's word; a whole generation proved that privileges do not save, and the oath "they shall not enter my rest" fell on the redeemed. Hardness is not a sudden event; it is a slow calcification of the heart that says "Is the LORD among us or not?" after a lifetime of evidence. But the good news is that God is still saying *Today*. Jesus Christ has entered the rest as our forerunner and opened it to believers: we who have believed enter that rest — not by our grip on God, but by His word believed and His voice obeyed. The Shepherd's hand that formed the dry land holds His sheep, and the invitation stands this very day: come, bow down, hear His voice, and do not harden your heart. Today is not too late. Today is the point.

**A note from the Reformers.** The Reformers preached Psalm 95 through Hebrews 3–4 as the church's standing orders: the promise of rest remains, the danger of unbelief remains, and the day of grace is always *today* — never a presumption for tomorrow. Calvin warned that the wilderness generation is set before us as a mirror, not a museum: the same voice, the same testing, the same choice runs through every sermon ever preached. And they joined the psalm's two halves as one doctrine: the God who is to be worshiped with joyful noise is the God who is to be feared with soft hearts — reverence and warning are not opposites but one posture, kneeling. *Sola fide* is the door into the rest; unbelief, even in the privileged, is the door out.

**Reflection question:** Where is your Meribah — the place where, despite everything God has done, your heart keeps asking, "Is the LORD among us or not?" What would it mean to answer that question today, with belief instead of testing: one act of obedience, one softening, one bowing down — before the day is over?

### Psalm 96 — Sing to the LORD a New Song: Tell of His Salvation

**Jesus in this psalm:** The missionary anthem of Book Four: "Oh sing to the LORD a new song; sing to the LORD, all the earth! Sing to the LORD, bless his name; tell of his salvation from day to day." The gods of the peoples are worthless idols, "but the LORD made the heavens" — and the psalm summons the families of the peoples to ascribe to Him glory and strength, to bring an offering, to worship in the splendor of holiness, and to say among the nations, "The LORD reigns!" Jesus is the salvation to be told of — the new song is His, sung now in every language on earth — and He is the coming Judge of the psalm's close: "For he comes to judge the earth. He will judge the world in righteousness, and the peoples in his faithfulness." The church sings Psalm 96 as a work order: declare, ascribe, say among the nations — the Judge is coming, and the news about Him is good.

**The key verse:**

> "Sing to the LORD, bless his name; tell of his salvation from day to day." — Psalm 96:2 (ESV)

"From day to day" — evangelism as a daily rhythm, not an annual event. And the content is not ourselves, our church, or our experience, though all may illustrate it: the content is *his salvation*. Tell of that, today, to somebody.

### Psalm 97 — The LORD Reigns: Light Is Sown for the Righteous

**Jesus in this psalm:** Clouds and thick darkness around Him, fire before Him, lightning lighting up the world — the LORD reigns, and the earth rejoices, and the coastlands are glad. Zion hears and is glad because of God's judgments; the idols are put to shame; and the psalm ends with two gifts for the righteous: "Light is sown for the righteous, and joy for the upright in heart." Jesus is the reigning LORD of this psalm, whose judgments the church celebrates and whose appearing will be lightning to the world — and He is the light sown for the righteous, planted now in the dark soil of this age, germinating in secret, and certain to spring up. The psalm's last command is the righteous man's response to a reigning God: "Rejoice in the LORD, O you righteous, and give thanks to his holy name!"

**The key verse:**

> "Light is sown for the righteous, and joy for the upright in heart." — Psalm 97:11 (ESV)

*Sown* — like seed, light for God's people is often underground before it is visible. If your life feels dark right now, this verse gives the darkness a different name: not abandonment, but a field. What God sows, God harvests. The light is already in the ground.

### Psalm 98 — O Sing to the LORD a New Song: He Has Done Marvelous Things

**Jesus in this psalm:** Psalm 98 is Psalm 96's twin — the new song again — but with the reason spelled out: "for he has done marvelous things; his right hand and his holy arm have worked salvation for him. The LORD has made known his salvation; he has revealed his righteousness in the sight of the nations." Israel's Exodus was the old marvel; the psalm looks to a salvation made known before the nations' eyes, remembered "to the house of Israel" and seen "to the ends of the earth." Jesus is that salvation made known — God's righteousness revealed publicly, on a cross and out of a tomb, in the sight of the nations — and the psalm's riot of instruments (lyre, trumpets, the horn, the roaring sea and the clapping rivers) is creation warming up for His appearing as Judge, which the earth is told to greet with joy, not dread.

**The key verse:**

> "The LORD has made known his salvation; he has revealed his righteousness in the sight of the nations." — Psalm 98:2 (ESV)

God did not save in a corner. The cross was public, the tomb was inspected, the resurrection was preached in the capital cities of the world. When your faith feels like a private opinion, remember: it began as public news, announced in the sight of the nations.

### Psalm 99 — The LORD Reigns; Let the Peoples Tremble

**Jesus in this psalm:** The last of the enthronement psalms tolls "holy" three times: "The LORD reigns; let the peoples tremble!… The LORD is great in Zion; he is exalted over all the peoples. Let them praise your great and awesome name! Holy is he!" Moses, Aaron, and Samuel — men who called on His name and were answered — are held up as the pattern of priestly prayer, and the psalm ends with the church's marching orders: "Exalt the LORD our God, and worship at his holy mountain; for the LORD our God is holy!" Jesus is the holy LORD enthroned between the cherubim, and He is the greater Moses, Aaron, and Samuel in one Person — the intercessor who calls on the Father and is always answered, and through whom our calling is answered too.

**The key verse:**

> "Exalt the LORD our God, and worship at his holy mountain; for the LORD our God is holy!" — Psalm 99:9 (ESV)

The psalm's argument for worship is not that God is useful, or near, or kind — though He is all three — but that He is *holy*. Holiness is not one attribute among others here; it is the atmosphere of the throne. Worship that forgets it becomes casual; worship that remembers it becomes glad and trembling at once.

### Psalm 100 — Make a Joyful Noise: Enter His Gates with Thanksgiving

**Jesus in this psalm:** The best-known invitation in the Psalter: "Make a joyful noise to the LORD, all the earth! Serve the LORD with gladness! Come into his presence with singing!" Its theology is creation and covenant in one breath: "Know that the LORD, he is God! It is he who made us, and we are his; we are his people, and the sheep of his pasture." And its door is thanksgiving: "Enter his gates with thanksgiving, and his courts with praise!" Jesus is the gate this psalm enters by — "I am the door" — and the Shepherd of the pasture it names, and the reason its last verse is true forever: "For the LORD is good; his steadfast love endures forever, and his faithfulness to all generations." Five verses, and the church has sung them at a thousand tables and services: the whole duty and delight of man, small enough to memorize, large enough to live in.

**The key verse:**

> "For the LORD is good; his steadfast love endures forever, and his faithfulness to all generations." — Psalm 100:5 (ESV)

Good, steadfast, faithful — and the time signature is *forever* and *to all generations*, which means the verse your grandmother sang is the same verse your grandchildren will. Some truths expire. This one is the climate the church lives in.

### Psalm 101 — I Will Sing of Steadfast Love and Justice: The King's Household

**Jesus in this psalm:** David's royal resolve: to sing of steadfast love *and* justice, to walk with integrity within his house, to set nothing worthless before his eyes, to cut off slanderers and the haughty from his court. It is the constitution of a king who knows his household is where his reign is proven. No earthly king kept it — David least of all, in the matter of his own house. Jesus is the King who did: integrity without shadow, eyes that never lingered on worthlessness, a household — His church — being purified toward the day when He presents her blameless. In Christ, Psalm 101 becomes both the believer's household rule (what we watch, whom we welcome, what we cut off) and our comfort: the King we failed to be, we have.

**The key verse:**

> "I will sing of steadfast love and justice; to you, O LORD, I will make melody." — Psalm 101:1 (ESV)

Steadfast love *and* justice — the two notes of God's character that the cross harmonizes, sung together by the king before he issues a single order. Rule that begins in that song has a chance. Households that sing it have a constitution no election can amend.

## Psalm 102 — A Prayer of One Afflicted: You Will Arise and Have Pity on Zion

### Jesus in this psalm

Jesus is the eternal Creator of Psalm 102's second half — the writer to the Hebrews quotes its closing verses as God's own address to the Son: "You, Lord, laid the foundation of the earth in the beginning, and the heavens are the work of your hands; they will perish, but you remain… you are the same, and your years will have no end" (Hebrews 1:10–12). And Jesus is also the afflicted pray-er of its first half: the one whose days pass like smoke, whose bones burn, whose heart is stricken and withered like grass, who eats ashes like bread and mingles tears with his drink, who is lifted up and cast down under God's indignation and anger. The psalm holds both in one prayer — the afflicted man and the eternal God — because in Jesus Christ they are one Person: the sufferer who poured out his complaint is the same Lord who laid the earth's foundations and will outlast the heavens. That is why the afflicted can pray this psalm with hope: the Man who suffered it is the God who answers it.

### Commentary

**The title and the complaint (102:1–11).** No psalm has a longer title: "A prayer of one afflicted, when he is faint and pours out his complaint before the LORD." Pouring out — like water from a jar, everything in the heart emptied before God. And what pours out is desolation: "For my days pass away like smoke, and my bones burn like a furnace. My heart is struck down like grass and has withered; I forget to eat my bread… I am like a desert owl of the wilderness, like an owl of the waste places; I lie awake; I am like a lonely sparrow on the housetop." A lonely sparrow on the housetop — the same bird that nested at God's altars in Psalm 84, here alone on a roof, awake, watching. His enemies taunt him all day; his tears are in his drink; and he names the cause without flinching: "because of your indignation and anger; for you have taken me up and thrown me down. My days are like an evening shadow; I wither away like grass." This is affliction experienced as God's own act — and prayed to God's own face. The afflicted man does not go elsewhere with this. He pours it out *before the LORD*, which is where complaints become prayers instead of poisons.

**But you, O LORD, are enthroned forever (102:12–22).** Then the psalm turns on its hinge: "But you, O LORD, are enthroned forever; you are remembered throughout all generations." My days are smoke; Yours are forever. My name is forgotten; Yours is remembered in every generation. And from God's eternity the afflicted man draws a conclusion about time: "You will arise and have pity on Zion; it is the time to favor her; the appointed time has come." God's pity has appointments. The ruins of Zion are loved by God's servants — "your servants hold her stones dear and have pity on her dust" — and the nations will fear the LORD's name when He builds up Zion and appears in His glory. And here is the verse the whole church should underline: "He regards the prayer of the destitute and does not despise their prayer." The destitute — the stripped, the empty, the ones with nothing — have a regarded prayer. God does not despise it. He registers it: "Let this be recorded for a generation to come, so that a people yet to be created may praise the LORD." The afflicted man's prayer becomes a document in the archive of praise, filed for people not yet born. Your poured-out complaint tonight may be somebody else's reason to praise God in fifty years.

**Of old you laid the foundation of the earth (102:23–28).** The psalm returns briefly to the affliction — "He has broken my strength in midcourse; he has shortened my days" — and then rises to its summit, addressing the afflicted man's God with words Hebrews tells us belong to the Son:

> "Of old you laid the foundation of the earth, and the heavens are the work of your hands. They will perish, but you will remain; they will all wear out like a garment. You will change them like a robe, and they will pass away, but you are the same, and your years will have no end." — Psalm 102:25–27 (ESV)

The heavens themselves are a garment God will one day fold and change — creation wears out; the Creator does not. "You are the same" — the unchangingness of God, and according to Hebrews, of Jesus Christ (Hebrews 13:8 picks up the theme: "Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever"). And the psalm's last line brings eternity down to an address: "The children of your servants shall dwell secure; their offspring shall be established before you." The afflicted man who began as a lonely sparrow ends by prophesying security for grandchildren. That is what happens when a sufferer prays long enough to reach God's eternity: he stops measuring his life by his days and starts measuring it by God's years — which have no end, and in Christ, neither do ours.

### Key verses

> "Hear my prayer, O LORD; let my cry come to you! Do not hide your face from me in the day of my distress! Incline your ear to me; answer me speedily in the day when I call!" — Psalm 102:1–2 (ESV)

> "But you, O LORD, are enthroned forever; you are remembered throughout all generations. You will arise and have pity on Zion; it is the time to favor her; the appointed time has come." — Psalm 102:12–13 (ESV)

> "He regards the prayer of the destitute and does not despise their prayer." — Psalm 102:17 (ESV)

> "But you are the same, and your years will have no end." — Psalm 102:27 (ESV)

### The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Psalm 102 is that affliction can reduce a life to smoke, ashes, and sleeplessness — bones burning, bread forgotten, tears in the cup — and that God's own people are not exempt; indeed, this sufferer traces his pain to God's indignation and anger, and his days are shortened in midcourse. We are grass, and we wither, and no one who has watched a strong body fail will call that a small thing. But the good news is that the sufferer and the Sovereign are not strangers. The afflicted Man of this psalm is the eternal Creator Himself, who laid the earth's foundations and will outlast the heavens — and He has entered our affliction, not merely observed it. God regards the prayer of the destitute; He does not despise it; He records it for a generation to come. And the God who is the same, whose years have no end, has joined His endless years to our shortened ones in Jesus Christ — so that the children of His servants dwell secure, established before Him, in a kingdom whose King does not wither like grass.

**A note from the Reformers.** Hebrews 1 made this psalm a proof-text of Christ's deity, and the Reformers followed gladly: words spoken in the Psalms *to* the afflicted one's God are quoted in the New Testament as spoken *to the Son* — therefore the Son is God, eternal and unchanging, and therefore (Calvin's pastoral turn) the afflicted who flee to Christ are fleeing to no mere fellow-sufferer but to their Creator. Spurgeon, himself often afflicted and faint, loved the title of this psalm and called it a directory for the depressed: pour out the complaint — all of it — but pour it *before the LORD*, and stay in the prayer until you can say "But you, O LORD." The turn from smoke to sovereignty, he said, is the whole art of praying in affliction.

**Reflection question:** What complaint have you been pouring out everywhere except before the LORD — to friends, to the ceiling, to no one? Pour it out to Him this week, in words, all of it — and then stay in the prayer past the complaint, until you can say the psalm's turn with your own mouth: "But you, O LORD, are enthroned forever." What is your "but you" tonight?

## Psalm 103 — Bless the LORD, O My Soul: Forget Not All His Benefits

### Jesus in this psalm

Jesus is the benefits of Psalm 103 — every one of them arrives through Him. "Who forgives all your iniquity" — through His blood. "Who heals all your diseases" — the Great Physician, whose earthly ministry was this verse in action and whose resurrection is its completion. "Who redeems your life from the pit" — the Redeemer who went into the pit for us. "Who crowns you with steadfast love and mercy" — the crown is His gift. "Who satisfies you with good so that your youth is renewed like the eagle's" — His life, renewing ours. And the psalm's great revelation of God's character — "The LORD is merciful and gracious, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love… He does not deal with us according to our sins, nor repay us according to our iniquities" — is Exodus 34's creed fulfilled in a cross: God does not deal with us according to our sins because He dealt with His Son according to them. As far as the east is from the west — that is where our transgressions are, and Jesus carried them there.

### Commentary

**Bless the LORD, O my soul (103:1–5).** The psalm begins with a man preaching to himself — Psalm 42's discipline, now in the key of praise: "Bless the LORD, O my soul, and all that is within me, bless his holy name! Bless the LORD, O my soul, and forget not all his benefits." *Forget not* — because forgetfulness, not ingratitude, is the ordinary leak in a believer's joy. And then David makes the list every believer should make — five benefits, each with its "who":

> "Who forgives all your iniquity, who heals all your diseases, who redeems your life from the pit, who crowns you with steadfast love and mercy, who satisfies you with good so that your youth is renewed like the eagle's." — Psalm 103:3–5 (ESV)

All your iniquity — not most of it. All your diseases — in this life in part, in the resurrection in full, but the Physician's intention is *all*. Redeems your life from the pit — the pit of Psalm 40, the bog, the grave. Crowns you — the forgiven are not merely released; they are *crowned*, with steadfast love and mercy as the diadem. Satisfies you with good — the mouth filled, the desires met, the youth renewed like the eagle's. David counts the benefits like a man counting money he did nothing to earn. That is the exercise: benefits, listed, remembered, blessed for.

**The LORD is merciful and gracious (103:6–14).** The psalm now grounds the benefits in the character of God, quoting the creed God Himself proclaimed to Moses on the mountain: "The LORD is merciful and gracious, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love. He will not always chide, nor will he keep his anger forever." And then the verse that may be the most comforting measurement in the Bible:

> "He does not deal with us according to our sins, nor repay us according to our iniquities. For as high as the heavens are above the earth, so great is his steadfast love toward those who fear him; as far as the east is from the west, so far does he remove our transgressions from us." — Psalm 103:10–12 (ESV)

Not according to our sins — if God dealt with us according to our sins, there would be no psalm, no David, no church, no reader. The measurement of His love is astronomical (heavens above earth) and the removal of our sins is geographical and absolute: east from west is not a distance that can be walked back; east and west never meet. (North and south meet at the poles; east and west do not — go east forever and you are still going east.) That is where your confessed sins are: unreachably removed. And the Father knows our frame underneath it all: "As a father shows compassion to his children, so the LORD shows compassion to those who fear him. For he knows our frame; he remembers that we are dust." He *remembers* our dust — our fragility is not news to Him, not a disappointment, not a discovery. He factored it in before He loved us.

**From everlasting to everlasting (103:15–22).** Man's days are grass again — Psalm 90's theme — "the wind passes over it, and it is gone, and its place knows it no more." But against the grass stands the covenant: "But the steadfast love of the LORD is from everlasting to everlasting on those who fear him, and his righteousness to children's children, to those who keep his covenant and remember to do his commandments." Grass above, everlasting underneath — the believer's life is short and his standing is eternal, and both are true at once. And the psalm that began with one man preaching to his own soul ends with the whole creation conscripted into the choir: "Bless the LORD, O you his angels, you mighty ones who do his word… Bless the LORD, all his hosts… Bless the LORD, all his works, in all places of his dominion. Bless the LORD, O my soul!" The circle widens from one soul to angels to all works in all places — and then closes, deliberately, back on the one soul: *Bless the LORD, O my soul.* Cosmic praise still has to be sung by somebody, in particular, tonight. It might as well be you.

### Key verses

> "Bless the LORD, O my soul, and forget not all his benefits." — Psalm 103:2 (ESV)

> "Who forgives all your iniquity, who heals all your diseases, who redeems your life from the pit, who crowns you with steadfast love and mercy." — Psalm 103:3–4 (ESV)

> "The LORD is merciful and gracious, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love." — Psalm 103:8 (ESV)

> "As far as the east is from the west, so far does he remove our transgressions from us." — Psalm 103:12 (ESV)

> "For he knows our frame; he remembers that we are dust." — Psalm 103:14 (ESV)

> "But the steadfast love of the LORD is from everlasting to everlasting on those who fear him." — Psalm 103:17 (ESV)

### The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Psalm 103 is in its background: we are dust and grass — frail, fleeting, diseased, guilty, and pit-bound — and our iniquities are real enough to need removing to an infinite distance. Forgetfulness is our other disease: left to ourselves, we forget the benefits faster than we receive them, and our praise dries up at the source. But the good news is the psalm itself: God does not deal with us according to our sins. In Jesus Christ, He has dealt with our sins according to *Christ's* cross — removed as far as east is from west — and He deals with us according to His steadfast love, which runs from everlasting to everlasting and remembers our dust with a Father's compassion. The benefits are not wages; they are benefits — forgiveness, healing, redemption, crowning, satisfaction — given to be remembered and blessed for. So preach to your soul tonight. Make the list. Forget not.

**A note from the Reformers.** Spurgeon called Psalm 103 a psalm that should be sung "with the whole heart" and noted that David here does what every believer must learn: he *commands* his soul to bless, because blessing is too important to wait for feelings. Calvin dwelt on verse 14 — "he knows our frame" — as one of Scripture's tenderest grounds of hope: God's compassion is not the surprise of a God who expected more from us, but the settled kindness of a Father who knew our dust before He adopted us. And the Reformers read verses 10–12 as the gospel's arithmetic in the Old Testament: not according to our sins (justice deflected), according to His steadfast love (grace applied) — the deflection landing, as Hebrews and the Gospels show, on the Son who bore the dealing we deserved.

**Reflection question:** David's command is "forget not all his benefits" — so make the list, in writing, this week: forgiveness, healings, redemptions from pits, crowns, satisfactions, in your own life, by name and date. Which benefit have you most forgotten — and what happens to your soul when you say verse 12 over your worst sin, slowly: "as far as the east is from the west, so far does he remove our transgressions from us"?

### Psalm 104 — O LORD My God, You Are Very Great: The Creation Hymn

**Jesus in this psalm:** Psalm 104 is Genesis 1 sung — the Creator praised through His works: light as His garment, the heavens His tent, the waters His servants, springs for the beasts, wine to gladden man's heart, oil and bread, the moon for seasons, the sea with Leviathan formed to play in it. And the New Testament tells us whose hands did this work: the Son, "by whom also God created the world," who "upholds the universe by the word of his power" (Hebrews 1:2–3). Every sunrise since the resurrection has been sustained by the crucified and risen Jesus. The psalm's meditation — "I will sing to the LORD as long as I live" — and its first occurrence of the Psalter's great shout, "Praise the LORD!" (Hallelujah appears here for the first time), belong to the church of the Creator-Redeemer, who feeds every creature and will one day renew the face of the ground He made.

**The key verse:**

> "O LORD, how manifold are your works! In wisdom have you made them all; the earth is full of your creatures." — Psalm 104:24 (ESV)

Manifold — too many to count, too varied to classify — and every one of them *wise*. The man who learns to see creation this way is never without a sermon: every creature is a sentence in a book God is still publishing daily.

---

### Psalm 105 — Tell of All His Wondrous Works: The Covenant Remembered

**Jesus in this psalm:** The companion to Psalm 106: where 106 confesses Israel's forgetting, 105 celebrates God's remembering — "He remembers his covenant forever, the word that he commanded, for a thousand generations." The psalm retells the story from Abraham to the Exodus as one long proof that God keeps covenant: the wandering patriarchs protected, Joseph sent ahead into slavery to preserve life, the plagues, the sea, the manna, the land. Jesus is the covenant's goal and guarantor — the offspring of Abraham in whom the thousand generations are blessed, the greater Joseph who was sent ahead through suffering to preserve life for many, the Passover Lamb of the true Exodus. "Seek the LORD and his strength; seek his presence continually" — that seeking ends, now, at a Person who said, "Seek, and you will find."

**The key verse:**

> "He remembers his covenant forever, the word that he commanded, for a thousand generations." — Psalm 105:8 (ESV)

God's memory is the church's security. We forget our covenants by Friday; He remembers His for a thousand generations — and the generation reading this page is inside the count. The covenant you are under tonight is older than your failures and longer than your life.

### Psalm 106 — We Have Sinned with Our Fathers: The Confession of a Nation

**Jesus in this psalm:** Book Four ends with the longest confession in the Psalter: Israel's history retold as a catalogue of rebellion — the Red Sea doubted, cravings in the wilderness, Dathan's rebellion, the golden calf, Baal-peor, the waters of Meribah, Canaan's idols learned and served, children sacrificed — "both Psalm 105 and 106 tell the same story; 105 from God's side, 106 from man's." And through it runs the refrain of mercy: "Nevertheless, he looked upon their distress… he remembered his covenant, and relented according to the abundance of his steadfast love." Jesus is the greater Moses this psalm keeps needing — the intercessor who stands in the breach (as Moses did at the calf, and Phinehas after him) and does not step back: He ever lives to make intercession, and His breach-standing, unlike Moses', can never be withdrawn. The psalm's cry — "Save us, O LORD our God, and gather us from among the nations" — is answered in the gathering of the church from every nation to the Son.

**The key verse:**

> "Praise the LORD! Oh give thanks to the LORD, for he is good, for his steadfast love endures forever!" — Psalm 106:1 (ESV)

A psalm that will spend forty verses confessing national sin opens with thanksgiving for steadfast love — because confession that does not start there will not survive its own honesty. First the love, then the sins, then the love again: that is the only order in which a true confession can be prayed.

# Book Five — Psalms 107–150: The Road Home

*The redeemed gathered from the four corners, the pilgrims ascending to Zion, the great acrostic of the Word, and the final crescendo: five closing psalms that begin and end with "Praise the LORD!" Book Five ends — and the Psalter ends — not with a verse but with everything that has breath praising the LORD.*

---

### Psalm 107 — Let the Redeemed of the LORD Say So

**Jesus in this psalm:** The charter psalm of Book Five gathers the redeemed from east and west, north and south, in four companies: wanderers in desert wastes, hungry and thirsty; prisoners in darkness and chains; fools who suffered for their sinful way, loathing food, near the gates of death; and sailors storm-tossed until they reeled and staggered like drunken men. Each company does the same thing — "Then they cried to the LORD in their trouble" — and meets the same mercy: "and he delivered them from their distress." Jesus walks through every stanza: He is the bread for the wanderers, the chain-breaker of the prisoners, the healer of the fools (it is hard not to hear the recovery meeting in this psalm), and the storm-stiller of the sailors — Mark's Gospel shows Him doing this psalm's fourth stanza in person on Galilee. The refrain is the redeemed's assignment: "Let the redeemed of the LORD say so."

**The key verse:**

> "Let the redeemed of the LORD say so, whom he has redeemed from trouble." — Psalm 107:2 (ESV)

*Say so* — redemption that stays silent is only half-finished. The psalm's four companies all end in thanksgiving and testimony. Whatever your company was — wanderer, prisoner, fool, or sailor — the deliverance is the same, and so is the duty: tell it.

---

### Psalm 108 — My Heart Is Steadfast: A Song Stitched from Victories

**Jesus in this psalm:** Psalm 108 is a mosaic — its first half is Psalm 57's confidence ("My heart is steadfast, O God! I will sing and make melody") and its second half is Psalm 60's battle prayer ("Who will bring me to the fortified city?"). The church has always read its stitching as a lesson: old mercies are meant to be re-sung into new battles; the God of the cave is the God of the campaign. Jesus is the steadfast heart of this psalm in person — unwavering from Gethsemane to the cross — and the answer to its closing line, which the Psalter now repeats like a creed: "With God we shall do valiantly; it is he who will tread down our foes." The King who treads down the final foes — sin, death, the grave — does so for a people whose hearts He has made steadfast by His own.

**The key verse:**

> "For your steadfast love is great above the heavens; your faithfulness reaches to the clouds." — Psalm 108:4 (ESV)

David measures God's love against the largest thing he knows — the heavens — and finds the love larger: *above* the heavens. When your troubles feel sky-high, this verse corrects the measurement: something is higher, and it is not the trouble. It is the love.

---

### Psalm 109 — Be Not Silent, O God of My Praise: The Psalm of the Betrayed

**Jesus in this psalm:** The most severe of the imprecatory psalms — David, accused by lying mouths and repaid evil for good and hatred for love, calls down God's judgment in terms that make readers tremble. The apostles applied its center to Judas: "May another take his office" (Acts 1:20) — the betrayer who was given a bishopric of trust and sold it. Jesus prayed in this psalm's situation more deeply than David ever did: accused falsely, loved and hated in return, betrayed by an officer of His own circle. And the psalm's own ending is where Christ leaves His people: not executing the curses, but standing at the right hand of the needy — "For he stands at the right hand of the needy one, to save him from those who condemn his soul to death." The Judge of the betrayers is also the defender of the betrayed, and He stands closer than the accusers.

**The key verse:**

> "Help me, O LORD my God! Save me according to your steadfast love!" — Psalm 109:26 (ESV)

Even in the fiercest psalm in the Psalter, the sufferer's own plea is not "save me according to my innocence" but "according to your steadfast love." Imprecation and mercy live in the same prayer because both are appeals to the same Judge — and the pray-er knows which ground he himself stands on.

## Psalm 110 — Sit at My Right Hand: The Most-Quoted Psalm

### Jesus in this psalm

Jesus is not merely in Psalm 110 — Psalm 110 is, by the New Testament's own count, the most-quoted psalm in its pages, and Jesus Himself made it the center of His self-revelation. "David himself calls him Lord," Jesus said, quoting its first verse: "The LORD said to my Lord, 'Sit at my right hand, until I make your enemies your footstool'" (Matthew 22:44) — and silenced the Pharisees with the question it raises: if David calls him Lord, how is he his son? Peter preached it at Pentecost as the proof of the ascension: God has made this Jesus, whom you crucified, both Lord and Christ, seated at the right hand until His enemies are made His footstool (Acts 2:34–36). Hebrews builds its doctrine of Christ's priesthood on its fourth verse: "You are a priest forever after the order of Melchizedek." Paul, the creeds, the Revelation — all drink from this psalm. It is seven verses long, and the whole New Testament is its commentary: the Son of David who is David's Lord, the King at God's right hand, the Priest forever, the coming Judge who will shatter kings on the day of His wrath.

### Commentary

**The LORD said to my Lord (110:1).** The psalm opens with an oracle overheard — David listening in on a conversation inside the Godhead:

> "The LORD says to my Lord: 'Sit at my right hand, until I make your enemies your footstool.'" — Psalm 110:1 (ESV)

The LORD (Yahweh) speaks to David's Lord (Adonai) — and David, the king, calls this second Lord *my* Lord, which means the one addressed is greater than David. Jesus' question in Matthew 22 is unanswerable on any merely human reading of the Messiah: David's descendant cannot be David's superior unless He is more than David's descendant. He is David's Son according to the flesh and David's Lord according to His divine nature — and the Father's word to Him is the word of enthronement: *Sit at my right hand*. The right hand is the place of honor and of power, and the sitting is the posture of finished work: priests never sat (their work was never done), but this King-Priest sits, because His sacrifice is complete. "Until I make your enemies your footstool" — the session is temporary in one respect: there is an *until*, a day coming when the seated King rises and every enemy is under His feet. The last enemy to be destroyed is death (1 Corinthians 15:25–26, Paul preaching this very verse).

**The scepter and the willing people (110:2–3).** "The LORD sends forth from Zion your mighty scepter. Rule in the midst of your enemies!" Rule *in the midst of* them — not after their removal, not in their absence, but surrounded by them, now. That is the present reign of Christ: a kingdom advancing inside enemy territory, one converted rebel at a time. And the method of the advance is in verse 3: "Your people will offer themselves freely on the day of your power, in holy garments; from the womb of the morning, the dew of your youth will be yours." Freely — the King's army is made of volunteers, people who offer themselves, dressed in holy garments He provides, numerous and fresh as the dew of the morning. No conscription, no coercion: the scepter of this King conquers by making enemies *willing*. Every conversion since Pentecost is verse 3 happening again.

**A priest forever (110:4).** Then the oath — and it is an oath, sworn by God and never to be revoked:

> "The LORD has sworn and will not change his mind, 'You are a priest forever after the order of Melchizedek.'" — Psalm 110:4 (ESV)

Melchizedek — the mysterious king of Salem who blessed Abraham, king and priest in one person, without recorded beginning or end (Genesis 14) — is the pattern of a priesthood older and greater than Aaron's. Aaron's priests died and were replaced; this Priest is *forever*. Aaron's priests offered animals; this Priest offered Himself. Aaron's priests stood daily; this one sat down. Hebrews 5–7 is one long meditation on this single verse: because Jesus is a priest forever after Melchizedek's order, He can save completely those who draw near to God through Him, since He always lives to make intercession for them. The King of verse 1 intercedes in verse 4. The throne and the altar are the same Person.

**The day of His wrath (110:5–7).** The psalm's last movement is judgment: "The Lord is at your right hand; he will shatter kings on the day of his wrath. He will execute judgment among the nations, filling them with corpses; he will shatter chief men over the wide earth." The seated, interceding King is also the coming Judge, and the psalm does not apologize for it: the footstool of verse 1 will be made, one way or another — enemies become footstool by conversion or by conquest, but the reign is not negotiable. And then the final verse, quiet and strange: "He will drink from the brook by the way; therefore he will lift up his head." The warrior-King, mid-campaign, stoops to drink from a brook — refreshed, unhurried, certain — and lifts up His head. The church has heard in it the humility and the triumph of Christ in one image: the King who drank the bitter cup first, and the brook of God's refreshment after, and whose head — bowed on the cross — is lifted up forever. David's seven verses contain the whole career of Christ: enthroned, reigning, interceding, judging, refreshed, and exalted. No wonder the apostles could not stop quoting it.

### Key verses

> "The LORD says to my Lord: 'Sit at my right hand, until I make your enemies your footstool.'" — Psalm 110:1 (ESV)

> "The LORD sends forth from Zion your mighty scepter. Rule in the midst of your enemies!" — Psalm 110:2 (ESV)

> "Your people will offer themselves freely on the day of your power, in holy garments." — Psalm 110:3 (ESV)

> "The LORD has sworn and will not change his mind, 'You are a priest forever after the order of Melchizedek.'" — Psalm 110:4 (ESV)

### The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Psalm 110 is that every person is currently on one side of a sentence: "until I make your enemies your footstool." To remain Christ's enemy is to be scheduled for the day of wrath, when kings are shattered and judgment fills the nations — and by nature, Paul says, we were enemies. There is no neutral ground in this psalm, and the Judge's campaign does not stall. But the good news is how this King makes His footstool: "Your people will offer themselves freely." Jesus Christ conquers His enemies, in this age of grace, by dying for them and converting them — by making rebels into volunteers in holy garments. And the King who will judge is, at this moment, the Priest who intercedes — forever, without pause, for everyone who draws near to God through Him. The right hand of God is occupied by your Intercessor. Come and offer yourself freely, while the day of His power is still the day of His patience.

**A note from the Reformers.** The Reformers treated Psalm 110 as the Psalter's theological summit: Calvin called its first verse a clear testimony both to Christ's deity and to His present reign, and he insisted the church take comfort in the session — Christ is not idle in heaven, but reigning and interceding, and His enemies' continued noise does not disturb a throne that has an *until* attached to it by the Father's own oath. Luther loved verse 4 as the death of every other priesthood: if Christ is priest forever by God's unchangeable oath, then no other mediator is needed or permitted — *solus Christus* with an oath under it. And they noted the oath itself: God swore and will not change His mind. The priesthood of Jesus is not a policy. It is a sworn constitution of the universe.

**Reflection question:** Christ is seated — His work finished — and interceding — His work continuing — at the same time. Which of those two truths do you most need today: the sitting (your salvation does not depend on your unfinished work) or the interceding (you are being prayed for, by name, right now)? And are you among the enemies He is making into volunteers — have you offered yourself freely, or are you still negotiating the terms?

### Psalm 111 — Great Are the Works of the LORD: The Alphabet of Praise

**Jesus in this psalm:** An acrostic psalm — each line beginning with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet, praise from A to Z — celebrating the works of the LORD: His deeds studied by all who delight in them, His covenant remembered, His provision given, His people redeemed. Its center is a sentence Jesus embodied: "He sent redemption to his people; he has commanded his covenant forever. Holy and awesome is his name!" The redemption God sent reached its fullness when He sent His Son — redemption not by power alone but by a Person, arriving in the flesh. And the psalm's famous last line is the doorway of all wisdom, which Jesus is: "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom" — and Christ, Paul says, has become for us wisdom from God.

**The key verse:**

> "He sent redemption to his people; he has commanded his covenant forever. Holy and awesome is his name!" — Psalm 111:9 (ESV)

*Sent* redemption — redemption is not found, achieved, or stumbled upon; it is dispatched, from heaven, by name, to a people. In the fullness of time the sending got a face: God sent forth His Son. The covenant is forever; the Sent One is Jesus.

---

### Psalm 112 — Blessed Is the Man Who Fears the LORD: The Righteous Man's Portrait

**Jesus in this psalm:** Psalm 112 is Psalm 111's mirror — the same acrostic form, the same opening beatitude — describing not God's works but the godly man's: he fears the LORD, delights in His commandments, is gracious and merciful and righteous, lends generously, conducts his affairs with justice, is not afraid of bad news ("his heart is firm, trusting in the LORD"), and distributes freely to the poor — a verse Paul quotes in 2 Corinthians 9 about Christian generosity. Jesus is the perfect original of this portrait, the truly righteous man whose heart was firm through the worst news ever delivered; and believers are its copies, being traced by the Spirit. The psalm's promise stands behind every quiet act of generosity no one sees: "his righteousness endures forever."

**The key verse:**

> "Praise the LORD! Blessed is the man who fears the LORD, who greatly delights in his commandments!" — Psalm 112:1 (ESV)

Fear and delight in one man — reverence that is not dread, delight that is not casual. That combination is the temperature of a healthy soul, and it is produced, not performed: it grows in people who stay near the God of Psalm 111 until His character becomes their character.

---

### Psalm 113 — Who Is Like the LORD Our God, Who Looks Far Down?

**Jesus in this psalm:** The first of the Hallel psalms (113–118), sung at Passover — which means Jesus sang this psalm at the Last Supper, hours before the cross. Its wonder is the stooping of God: "Who is like the LORD our God, who is seated on high, who looks far down on the heavens and the earth?" — and what does the far-down-looking God do? "He raises the poor from the dust and lifts the needy from the ash heap, to make them sit with princes." Jesus is that stoop incarnate: the God seated on high who looked far down — all the way down, into a manger, onto a cross, into a tomb — to raise the poor from the dust. The barren woman given children, the ash-heap beggar seated with princes: Hannah sang this, Mary sang this ("he has lifted up the humble"), and the church sings it still, because the stooping has a name.

**The key verse:**

> "He raises the poor from the dust and lifts the needy from the ash heap, to make them sit with princes, with the princes of his people." — Psalm 113:7–8 (ESV)

From ash heap to princes' table — that is the gospel's social geography in one verse, and it is not a metaphor the church invented; it is a description of what the stooping God has been doing since Hannah. If you are on the ash heap tonight, you are at the address where He does His favorite work.

---

### Psalm 114 — When Israel Went Out of Egypt: The Earth Trembles

**Jesus in this psalm:** Eight tight verses on the Exodus, sung at Passover as part of the Hallel — the sea fled, the Jordan turned back, the mountains skipped like rams, the rock turned into a pool of water. The psalm's question is its theology: "What ails you, O sea, that you flee? O Jordan, that you turn back?" — and the answer is presence: "Tremble, O earth, at the presence of the Lord, at the presence of the God of Jacob." Jesus is the presence that makes seas flee in the greater Exodus: at His death the earth shook and rocks split, at His resurrection the stone fled from the tomb, and He leads His people now through a Jordan into a promised land the first Exodus only sketched. The mountains skipped then; the whole creation will dance at the last appearing of the Presence.

**The key verse:**

> "Tremble, O earth, at the presence of the Lord, at the presence of the God of Jacob." — Psalm 114:7 (ESV)

Creation's convulsions at the Exodus were not the sea's mood; they were the sea's manners — nature recognizing its Maker on the march. The Presence that parted water for slaves is the Presence that walks with you: the trembling is on the other side now.

### Psalm 115 — Not to Us, O LORD, Not to Us

**Jesus in this psalm:** The psalm of transferred glory: "Not to us, O LORD, not to us, but to your name give glory, for the sake of your steadfast love and your faithfulness!" Against the nations' taunt — "Where is their God?" — it sets the idols' obituary: silver and gold, with mouths that do not speak, eyes that do not see, hands that do not feel — and the warning that "those who make them become like them." Jesus is the glory this psalm guards: the living God made visible, whose mouth speaks, whose eyes see, whose hands were pierced healing — everything the idols are not. And He is the help and shield the psalm three times pronounces over Israel, over Aaron's house, over all who fear the LORD: "He is their help and their shield."

**The key verse:**

> "Not to us, O LORD, not to us, but to your name give glory, for the sake of your steadfast love and your faithfulness!" — Psalm 115:1 (ESV)

Twice "not to us" — the psalm will not let the disclaimer be mumbled once and forgotten. Every blessing the church receives gets routed, by this verse, to the right address: His name, His steadfast love, His faithfulness. Glory is a package that must not be signed for by the wrong hands.

### Psalm 116 — I Love the LORD: The Cup of Salvation

**Jesus in this psalm:** A delivered man's thanksgiving, also part of the Hallel — also sung by Jesus on the night of His arrest, which gives its lines a weight beyond any other singing of them: "I love the LORD, because he has heard my voice and my pleas for mercy… The snares of death encompassed me; the pangs of Sheol laid hold on me." The psalm asks the question every rescued person asks: "What shall I render to the LORD for all his benefits to me?" — and answers it with a cup and a vow: "I will lift up the cup of salvation and call on the name of the LORD." At the Last Supper, Jesus lifted a cup and gave it a new name — "this cup is the new covenant in my blood" — and the psalm's line "Precious in the sight of the LORD is the death of his saints" was fulfilled in a death God counted so precious that He would not leave it in a tomb. When believers take the cup now, they are singing Psalm 116 with its Author.

**The key verse:**

> "Precious in the sight of the LORD is the death of his saints." — Psalm 116:15 (ESV)

Precious — not common, not careless, not unnoticed. The death of God's saints costs Him something in His own heart, and He attends it personally. If you fear dying forgotten, this verse is God's answer filed in advance: your death is on His calendar, marked precious.

### Psalm 117 — Praise the LORD, All Nations: The Shortest Psalm

**Jesus in this psalm:** The shortest chapter in the Bible — two verses — and one of the largest: "Praise the LORD, all nations! Extol him, all peoples! For great is his steadfast love toward us, and the faithfulness of the LORD endures forever. Praise the LORD!" Paul quotes it in Romans 15 as a proof that the Gentiles were always in the plan — the root of Jesse coming "to rule the Gentiles; in him will the Gentiles hope." Jesus is the steadfast love and the faithfulness this psalm celebrates, and His church — gathered from all nations, extolling Him in all languages — is the psalm's ongoing fulfillment. Two verses: the Bible's smallest psalm contains its widest invitation. No one is outside the word *all*.

**The key verse:**

> "For great is his steadfast love toward us, and the faithfulness of the LORD endures forever. Praise the LORD!" — Psalm 117:2 (ESV)

The whole gospel in one verse, small enough for a child to memorize and large enough for the nations to live in: great love, endless faithfulness, and the only fitting response — praise. If you learned nothing else from the Psalter, this verse would carry you home.

## Psalm 118 — The Stone the Builders Rejected: His Steadfast Love Endures Forever

### Jesus in this psalm

Jesus is the rejected Stone of Psalm 118 — He said so Himself, quoting this psalm as the capstone of the parable of the tenants and the verdict on His own rejection: "The stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone; this was the Lord's doing, and it is marvelous in our eyes" (Matthew 21:42). The crowds sang this psalm at Him on Palm Sunday — "Hosanna! Blessed is he who comes in the name of the LORD!" (Psalm 118:25–26) — and He sang it Himself at the end of the Last Supper, when He and the disciples "sang a hymn" and went out to the Mount of Olives (Matthew 26:30): the Hallel's last psalm, on His lips, on His way to become the rejected stone. Peter preached it at Pentecost and before the Sanhedrin ("This Jesus is the stone that was rejected by you, the builders, which has become the cornerstone. And there is salvation in no one else," Acts 4:11–12), and the church has built on it ever since: the gate of the LORD, the day the LORD has made, the festival sacrifice bound to the horns of the altar — all of it is Christ.

### Commentary

**His steadfast love endures forever (118:1–4).** The psalm opens with the Psalter's great refrain, sounded four times like bells: "Oh give thanks to the LORD, for he is good; for his steadfast love endures forever! Let Israel say… Let the house of Aaron say… Let those who fear the LORD say, 'His steadfast love endures forever.'" Before the psalm tells us anything about the speaker's trouble, it settles the atmosphere: whatever is coming in the next verses — distress, enemies, rejection — it is all happening inside a universe where God's steadfast love endures forever. That is not the conclusion of the psalm; it is the premise. The church sings the premise first so that the trouble is interpreted by the love, and not the other way around.

**Out of my distress I called (118:5–13).** "Out of my distress I called on the LORD; the LORD answered me and set me in a broad place." Distress is a narrow place — walls closing, options shrinking; God's answer is breadth. And the confession that follows is one of the Psalter's most portable: "The LORD is on my side; I will not fear. What can man do to me? The LORD is on my side as my helper; I shall look in triumph on those who hate me." Then the comparison that has steadied centuries of believers: "It is better to take refuge in the LORD than to trust in man. It is better to take refuge in the LORD than to trust in princes." Man, princes — every human refuge ranked and found wanting next to the LORD. The enemies come in the psalm's most vivid image: "They surrounded me, surrounded me on every side… They surrounded me like bees; they went out like a fire among thorns; in the name of the LORD I cut them off!" Bees — swarming, stinging, innumerable — and thorn-fire, which blazes fast and dies fast. "I was pushed hard, so that I was falling, but the LORD helped me." Pushed to the very edge of the fall — and helped. That is the testimony of the psalm, and of the cross and resurrection inside it: pushed hard by all the bees of hell and earth, falling — and the LORD helped Him, on the third day, in a garden.

**The gate of the LORD (118:14–21).** "The LORD is my strength and my song; he has become my salvation." Glad songs of salvation sound in the tents of the righteous: "The right hand of the LORD does valiantly, the right hand of the LORD exalts, the right hand of the LORD does valiantly!" Three times — the right hand again, the hand of Psalm 110's session. And then the sentence every survivor of a death sentence underlines: "I shall not die, but I shall live, and recount the deeds of the LORD. The LORD has disciplined me severely, but he has not given me over to death." Disciplined severely — the psalm does not call suffering anything else — but not given over. There is a line God draws inside His discipline, and death is on the other side of it for His Son, and in His Son, for His people. "Open to me the gates of righteousness, that I may enter through them and give thanks to the LORD. This is the gate of the LORD; the righteous shall enter through it." The procession reaches the temple gate — the delivered man coming up with the congregation — and Jesus will take this gate-language onto His own lips: "I am the door. If anyone enters by me, he will be saved" (John 10:9).

**The stone the builders rejected (118:22–29).** And now the verses that made this psalm the New Testament's favorite:

> "The stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone. This is the LORD's doing; it is marvelous in our eyes. This is the day that the LORD has made; let us rejoice and be glad in it." — Psalm 118:22–24 (ESV)

The builders — the experts, the professionals, the men with the plans — examined this stone and threw it on the scrap heap. God retrieved it and made it the cornerstone: the stone that determines the lines of the whole building. That is the cross and resurrection in the language of a building site. The religious experts rejected Jesus; God installed Him as the foundation of everything. "This is the LORD's doing" — the psalm insists the reversal was not luck, not politics, not the stone's own campaigning: the LORD did it, and it is marvelous — wonder-worthy — in our eyes. "This is the day that the LORD has made" — in context, *this* day is the day of the stone's installation, the day of deliverance; the church rightly sings it of Easter morning first, and then of every day God makes after it. Then the cry the crowds would shout at the Triumphal Entry: "Save us, we pray, O LORD!" — *Hosanna* — "Blessed is he who comes in the name of the LORD!" And the psalm ends at the altar: "Bind the festal sacrifice with cords, up to the horns of the altar!" — the delivered procession arriving with its offering — "You are my God, and I will give thanks to you; you are my God; I will extol you. Oh give thanks to the LORD, for he is good; for his steadfast love endures forever." The bells of the opening ring again at the close. Between them stands a rejected stone, become the cornerstone. That is the whole gospel, and the singer of this psalm at the Last Supper knew exactly what He was singing.

### Key verses

> "Oh give thanks to the LORD, for he is good; for his steadfast love endures forever!" — Psalm 118:1 (ESV)

> "The LORD is on my side; I will not fear. What can man do to me?" — Psalm 118:6 (ESV)

> "It is better to take refuge in the LORD than to trust in man. It is better to take refuge in the LORD than to trust in princes." — Psalm 118:8–9 (ESV)

> "I shall not die, but I shall live, and recount the deeds of the LORD." — Psalm 118:17 (ESV)

> "The stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone. This is the LORD's doing; it is marvelous in our eyes." — Psalm 118:22–23 (ESV)

> "Save us, we pray, O LORD! O LORD, we pray, give us success! Blessed is he who comes in the name of the LORD!" — Psalm 118:25–26 (ESV)

### The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Psalm 118 is that the builders rejected the stone — which means the world's expertise, left to itself, examines Jesus Christ and throws Him away: too weak, too slow, too offensive, not the foundation we wanted. And every one of us has been a builder with a reject pile; our own hearts examined Him and found reasons. The psalm is also honest that the righteous are pushed hard, disciplined severely, and surrounded like bees — deliverance is not the absence of the swarm. But the good news is that God's building does not depend on the builders' verdict. The rejected stone is the cornerstone now — by the LORD's doing — and there is salvation in no one else. The gate of the LORD stands open, the day of His making dawns every morning, and everyone who comes in the name of the LORD is blessed. You do not have to be the stone the builders chose. You only have to be built on the Stone God chose — and He is marvelous in our eyes.

**A note from the Reformers.** No psalm was more on the Reformers' lips in controversy: Luther, rejected and outlawed by the builders of his day, took Psalm 118 as a personal banner — he called verse 17 ("I shall not die, but I shall live") his own verse and had it painted where he would see it. Calvin read the cornerstone as the church's whole foundation doctrine: the building of God rests on a stone men rejected, which is why the church must never measure Christ's worth by the experts' consensus in any age. And the Reformers pressed the psalm's refrain on the conscience: if His steadfast love endures forever, then the believer's thanksgiving is never out of season and never out of material. *Solus Christus* is the cornerstone; *soli Deo gloria* is the song sung around it.

**Reflection question:** Where have you been trusting in man or in princes — in people's approval, in systems, in your own building plans — instead of taking refuge in the LORD? And what would it mean to rejoice in *this* day as the day the LORD has made: not the day you would have chosen, but the one whose cornerstone was laid by rejection and resurrection?

### Psalm 119 — Your Word Is a Lamp to My Feet: The Great Acrostic of the Word

**Jesus in this psalm:** The longest chapter in the Bible is a love song to the Word of God — 176 verses in twenty-two stanzas, one for each letter of the Hebrew alphabet, nearly every verse naming the Word under one of its titles: law, testimonies, precepts, statutes, commandments, rules, word, promise. The psalmist delights in it, weeps over those who forsake it, meditates on it day and night, and finds it sweeter than honey and better than thousands of gold and silver pieces. Jesus is this psalm's fulfillment in two directions. He is the Word the psalm loves — the Word made flesh, in whom all God's testimonies and promises stand embodied. And He is the psalm's perfect pray-er: the One who could say without exaggeration, "I delight to do your will, O my God; your law is within my heart," whose food was to do the will of the Father, who hid the Word in His heart and answered every temptation from it in the wilderness — "It is written." Believers pray Psalm 119 in Him: loving the Word because the Word is a Person we love, and asking, with the psalm's great prayer, "Give me life according to your word" — life that He gives according to His promise, freely, again and again.

**The key verse:**

> "Your word is a lamp to my feet and a light to my path." — Psalm 119:105 (ESV)

Not a floodlight for the whole journey — a lamp, for the feet, for the next step. God rarely shows the whole road; He shows the step, and the step is enough, because the Lamp-bearer walks it with us. Men perish not for lack of a map of the future but for refusing the light they have for tonight.

### Psalm 120 — In My Distress I Called to the LORD: The First Step Up

**Jesus in this psalm:** The Songs of Ascents (Psalms 120–134) begin far from Jerusalem, in exile among warlike peoples — Meshech and Kedar — with a man sick of lying lips and a deceitful tongue: "Woe to me, that I sojourn in Meshech… Too long have I had my dwelling among those who hate peace. I am for peace, but when I speak, they are for war!" Jesus prayed this psalm's distress in truth — He sojourned among liars, was accused by false witnesses, loved peace and was met with war — and He is the LORD who answers the distress call: "In my distress I called to the LORD, and he answered me." The pilgrim road starts exactly where every returning sinner starts: in distress, far away, calling. And the first thing the psalm promises the caller is not the destination. It is an answer.

**The key verse:**

> "In my distress I called to the LORD, and he answered me." — Psalm 120:1 (ESV)

Eight words that have started a million journeys home. The pilgrim is still in Meshech when the verse ends — circumstances unchanged — but he has been *answered*, and a man with an answer can start walking. Call first. The road appears under moving feet.

## Psalm 121 — I Lift Up My Eyes to the Hills: The Keeper of Israel

### Jesus in this psalm

Jesus is the Keeper of Psalm 121 — and He is also the help the pilgrim's eyes are lifted to find. "From where does my help come? My help comes from the LORD, who made heaven and earth." In Christ, that Maker has come near: the LORD who made heaven and earth is the same Lord who was lifted up on a hill outside Jerusalem, so that pilgrims lifting their eyes to the hills would find, on one of them, a cross — and beyond it, an empty tomb. Jesus prayed this psalm's trust through His own journey to Jerusalem, the ascent of ascents, keeping His eyes on the Father who neither slumbers nor sleeps; and now He is Himself the unsleeping Keeper of His people: "I am with you always" is Psalm 121 in the first person. The pilgrim who sings this psalm travels under a guard who does not rotate shifts, does not doze, and does not lose a single charge: He keeps your going out and your coming in, from this time forth and forevermore.

### Commentary

**From where does my help come? (121:1–2).** The pilgrim looks up: "I lift up my eyes to the hills. From where does my help come?" The hills around Jerusalem could mean several things — the high places where idols were worshiped, the heights where robbers waited, or Zion itself, the hill of God's house toward which the pilgrim climbs. The question hangs for one line — *from where?* — and then the psalm answers it with the whole gospel in two sentences: "My help comes from the LORD, who made heaven and earth." Not from the hills, not from the shrines on them, not from the strength of my own legs for the climb: from the LORD — and lest the LORD seem small, He is identified by His largest credential: Maker of heaven and earth. The God who made everything is not going to be outmatched by anything. Every pilgrim's journey, and every believer's hard season, begins with getting the *from where* right.

**He will not let your foot be moved (121:3–4).** Then the blessing begins — most of the psalm is spoken *to* the pilgrim, by a companion or a priest sending him off under guard: "He will not let your foot be moved; he who keeps you will not slumber. Behold, he who keeps Israel will neither slumber nor sleep." *Keep* — the psalm's drumbeat word, six times in eight verses: keeps you, keeper, keep. On mountain paths, a moved foot — a slipped step — can be the last one; the Keeper's first promise is footing. And His qualification for the job is His sleeplessness: human guards rotate and doze — Elijah mocked Baal's prophets with exactly this taunt, "perhaps he is asleep and must be awakened" — but Israel's Keeper runs no such risk. He neither slumbers nor sleeps. There is no hour of the night, no watch of your life, at which heaven's guard is down. The insomniac believer can rest his own sleeplessness on God's: one of us is awake, and it is not required to be me.

**The LORD is your keeper (121:5–6).** "The LORD is your keeper; the LORD is your shade on your right hand. The sun shall not strike you by day, nor the moon by night." Shade — in that climate, shade is not a luxury; it is survival, and God stations Himself between His pilgrim and the striking sun, at the right hand, the place of work and of vulnerability. Day dangers and night dangers are both named and both answered — the psalm is realistic that both shifts have their hazards, and insistent that both shifts have the same Keeper. Nothing in the twenty-four hours is unguarded time.

**He will keep your life (121:7–8).** The psalm closes by widening the promise to everything: "The LORD will keep you from all evil; he will keep your life. The LORD will keep your going out and your coming in from this time forth and forevermore." *All evil* — not all hardship, not all sorrow, but all evil: nothing that can finally harm the kept one gets through the Keeper. Going out and coming in — the two ends of every journey and every day, the doorstep in both directions — are inside the promise. And the term is the longest available: from this time forth *and forevermore*. The keeping does not expire at death; it is precisely at death that the kept discover how well they were kept, when the Keeper brings them in through the last door into the house where the keeping becomes sight. Eight verses, and pilgrims have sung them at the start of journeys for three thousand years — at hospital doors, at airport gates, at gravesides, at the beginnings of things they could not see the end of. The psalm fits all of them, because the Keeper does.

### Key verses

> "I lift up my eyes to the hills. From where does my help come? My help comes from the LORD, who made heaven and earth." — Psalm 121:1–2 (ESV)

> "He will not let your foot be moved; he who keeps you will not slumber. Behold, he who keeps Israel will neither slumber nor sleep." — Psalm 121:3–4 (ESV)

> "The LORD is your keeper; the LORD is your shade on your right hand." — Psalm 121:5 (ESV)

> "The LORD will keep your going out and your coming in from this time forth and forevermore." — Psalm 121:8 (ESV)

### The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Psalm 121 is that the road is genuinely dangerous: feet slip on mountain paths, the sun strikes by day and terrors own the night, and we are not our own keepers — left to ourselves, we fall asleep at our own guard posts, and our help, sought in the hills of idols and self-reliance, never arrives. We cannot keep our own lives; the history of every relapse, every fall, every lost pilgrim proves it. But the good news is that keeping is God's job description, and He does not sleep on it. In Jesus Christ, the Maker of heaven and earth has become our Keeper in person — He walked the pilgrim road ahead of us, He guards the road beneath us, and He has promised the whole journey: going out, coming in, this time forth, forevermore. You are kept — not by your grip on Him, but by His grip on you — and the Keeper has never lost one He was keeping.

**A note from the Reformers.** Calvin loved this psalm's order: the question of verse 1 is asked by every anxious heart, and the answer of verse 2 must be learned until it is reflex — help comes not from the hills, however religious they look, but from the LORD who made them. He also noted the pastoral genius of the sleeplessness: God does not merely watch over us as one duty among many; keeping is what He *is* to His people, day and night, without interval. Spurgeon called Psalm 121 a psalm for travelers in every sense and urged that it be read aloud over departing friends — a blessing to be *spoken*, since most of it is addressed to the pilgrim, not about God. The church sends its people out under this psalm because the church believes it: the Keeper is awake, so the pilgrims can sleep.

**Reflection question:** Where have you been looking for help — which hills have your eyes been on — and what would change if the *from where* of verse 2 became your reflex? Take one journey, one appointment, one night of this week and consciously hand it to the Keeper beforehand: "The LORD will keep my going out and my coming in." Say it at the door. Mean it by the mile.

### Psalm 122 — I Was Glad When They Said to Me

**Jesus in this psalm:** The pilgrim has arrived: "I was glad when they said to me, 'Let us go to the house of the LORD!' Our feet have been standing within your gates, O Jerusalem!" David's psalm loves the city because of the house, and the house because of the LORD — and it commands the pilgrims' homework: "Pray for the peace of Jerusalem!" Jesus is the peace of the greater Jerusalem: He wept over the city that did not know the things that make for peace, and He became, by His cross, the peace Himself — the one in whom Jew and Gentile are built into a single house of God, the Jerusalem above, whose gates the church's feet will stand within forever. The gladness of arrival in this psalm is a rehearsal: every Lord's Day gathering of God's people is a small Psalm 122, glad to be summoned, standing inside the gates, praying for the peace of the city that is to come.

**The key verse:**

> "I was glad when they said to me, 'Let us go to the house of the LORD!'" — Psalm 122:1 (ESV)

*Glad* — the summons to worship as good news, not interruption. When the invitation to God's house stops sounding glad, something in the pilgrim needs the rest of this psalm: the feet standing, the tribes gathered, the peace prayed for. Gladness is recoverable; it usually returns by going.

### Psalm 123 — To You I Lift Up My Eyes: The Servant's Look

**Jesus in this psalm:** A short, aching ascent: the pilgrims have "had more than enough of contempt" — the scorn of the arrogant, the contempt of the proud — and they do the only thing servants know to do: they look. "Behold, as the eyes of servants look to the hand of their master, as the eyes of a maidservant to the hand of her mistress, so our eyes look to the LORD our God, till he has mercy upon us." Jesus is the master whose hand is watched — and He is also the supreme servant who prayed this psalm's look toward the Father through every contempt of His passion, despising the shame, waiting for the mercy that came on the third day. The psalm teaches the posture of the despised church: not answering contempt with contempt, but eyes up, on the hand that gives, till mercy comes.

**The key verse:**

> "Behold, as the eyes of servants look to the hand of their master, as the eyes of a maidservant to the hand of her mistress, so our eyes look to the LORD our God, till he has mercy upon us." — Psalm 123:2 (ESV)

A servant's eyes on the master's hand — watching for the signal, the gift, the direction. That is a whole theology of prayer in one picture: not shouting orders upward, but looking, steadily, at the hand that has never yet failed to move in mercy. *Till* — the look has a duration, and the duration is God's to choose.

### Psalm 124 — If It Had Not Been the LORD Who Was on Our Side

**Jesus in this psalm:** Israel's great might-have-been: "If it had not been the LORD who was on our side — let Israel now say — if it had not been the LORD who was on our side when people rose up against us, then they would have swallowed us up alive." The flood would have gone over the soul; the proud waters would have covered it. But the trap broke: "We have escaped like a bird from the snare of the fowlers; the snare is broken, and we have escaped!" Jesus is the LORD on our side in the flesh — God for us, in a body, against whom men rose up and whom the proud waters could not swallow — and He is the escape of every bird since: the snare of sin and death broken at His cross and empty tomb, not by our fluttering but by His breaking.

**The key verse:**

> "Our help is in the name of the LORD, who made heaven and earth." — Psalm 124:8 (ESV)

The church has used this verse to open its worship for centuries, and it is the right door: help is located, by name, before anything else happens. Not in our numbers, our history, or our deserving — in the *name* of the Maker of heaven and earth, spoken over us.

### Psalm 125 — Those Who Trust in the LORD Are Like Mount Zion

**Jesus in this psalm:** The pilgrims look at the mountains around Jerusalem and see their own portrait: "Those who trust in the LORD are like Mount Zion, which cannot be moved, but abides forever. As the mountains surround Jerusalem, so the LORD surrounds his people, from this time forth and forevermore." Trust makes people geological — unmovable, not because they are strong, but because of what surrounds them. Jesus is the LORD who surrounds His people — the greater Jerusalem's wall and mountain range in person — and He is the scepter-holder whose reign guarantees the psalm's justice: the scepter of wickedness shall not rest forever on the land allotted to the righteous. In Him, the surrounded can sing on the road, because the surrounding that matters most is not the enemy's.

**The key verse:**

> "Those who trust in the LORD are like Mount Zion, which cannot be moved, but abides forever." — Psalm 125:1 (ESV)

Movability is a property of what a thing rests on. A believer rests on the LORD who surrounds him like mountains — so the believer's stability is not a mood; it is a location. You may feel movable today. Feelings are weather. The mountains are not.

### Psalm 126 — Those Who Sow in Tears Shall Reap with Shouts of Joy

**Jesus in this psalm:** The return from exile sung as a dream come true: "When the LORD restored the fortunes of Zion, we were like those who dream. Then our mouth was filled with laughter, and our tongue with shouts of joy." And then the psalm does something brave — it prays for the *next* restoration while still weeping in the present one: "Restore our fortunes, O LORD, like streams in the Negeb!" — and gives the law of the kingdom's agriculture: sowing in tears, reaping in joy, the seed-basket carried out weeping and the sheaves carried home shouting. Jesus is the great Sower of this psalm: He sowed Himself in tears — Gethsemane, the cross — and rose as the first sheaf of the harvest, guaranteeing the rest. Every tear shed in faithful sowing — in prayer, in witness, in endurance — is seed, not waste, in His economy.

**The key verse:**

> "Those who sow in tears shall reap with shouts of joy!" — Psalm 126:5 (ESV)

Shall — God's own future tense, attached to the saddest present tense in farming. The weeping sower is not told to feel better; he is told to keep sowing, because the harvest is scheduled. Tears are not the opposite of the harvest. They are, mysteriously, its irrigation.

### Psalm 127 — Unless the LORD Builds the House

**Jesus in this psalm:** Solomon's psalm — the builder-king's confession that building is God's work or it is vanity: "Unless the LORD builds the house, those who build it labor in vain. Unless the LORD watches over the city, the watchman stays awake in vain." Its second half turns to the other building project, children: "Behold, children are a heritage from the LORD, the fruit of the womb a reward" — arrows in a warrior's hand. Jesus is the LORD who builds the true house — "I will build my church" — and He is the Son in whom the Father's house is finally built of living stones. The psalm rebukes the anxious builder in all of us: "It is in vain that you rise up early and go late to rest, eating the bread of anxious toil; for he gives to his beloved sleep." Sleep, in this psalm, is a theological act — the builder's confession that the building is not finally his.

**The key verse:**

> "Unless the LORD builds the house, those who build it labor in vain. Unless the LORD watches over the city, the watchman stays awake in vain." — Psalm 127:1 (ESV)

Twice "in vain" — the psalm is not against building or watching; it is against building and watching *without* the LORD, which converts good work into anxious vanity. Work hard, watch carefully — and sleep like someone who knows whose project it is.

### Psalm 128 — Blessed Is Everyone Who Fears the LORD: The Fruitful Home

**Jesus in this psalm:** The companion to Psalm 127 — the blessed household from the inside: the man who fears the LORD eats the fruit of the labor of his hands; his wife is a fruitful vine within his house; his children are olive shoots around his table; "thus shall the man be blessed who fears the LORD." It is Eden's pattern restored at a kitchen table. Jesus is the LORD whose fear produces this fruitfulness, and He is the true Husband whose household — the church — is the fruitful vine spreading far beyond any one table: His blessing of Zion "all the days of your life" and the sight of "your children's children" reach their fullness in a family gathered from every nation, seated at a table in a kingdom. The psalm's last word is its benediction: "Peace be upon Israel!"

**The key verse:**

> "Blessed is everyone who fears the LORD, who walks in his ways!" — Psalm 128:1 (ESV)

*Everyone* — the blessing of the fruitful life is not reserved for the gifted or the lucky; it is addressed to everyone who fears the LORD and walks in His ways. The doorway is reverence, and it stands open to the ordinary believer with an ordinary table.

### Psalm 129 — The LORD Is Righteous: He Has Cut the Cords of the Wicked

**Jesus in this psalm:** Israel's scar tissue sings: "Greatly have they afflicted me from my youth — let Israel now say — greatly have they afflicted me from my youth, yet they have not prevailed against me. The plowers plowed upon my back; they made long their furrows." The back of God's people as a field for enemy plows — and over the furrows, the verdict: "The LORD is righteous; he has cut the cords of the wicked." Jesus bore this psalm's image in His own flesh — His back given to those who struck Him, furrowed by the scourge — and He is the righteous LORD who cuts cords: of oppressors, of addictions, of every yoke plowed into His people's backs. The psalm ends wishing the haters of Zion the fruitlessness of grass on housetops — withered before it grows — because blessing and Zion stand or fall together: "Peace be upon Israel!"

**The key verse:**

> "The LORD is righteous; he has cut the cords of the wicked." — Psalm 129:4 (ESV)

The furrows are real — the psalm does not airbrush the scars. But the cords are cut: the plow team no longer owns the field. Some believers carry furrows they will show forever; the cords that made them, in Christ, are severed history. Scars remain. Slavery doesn't.

## Psalm 130 — Out of the Depths: If You, O LORD, Should Mark Iniquities

### Jesus in this psalm

Jesus is the redemption at the end of Psalm 130 — the reason a sinner in the depths can wait for the LORD with more certainty than a watchman waits for a morning that has never once failed to come. The psalm's center is a gospel syllogism: "If you, O LORD, should mark iniquities, O Lord, who could stand? But with you there is forgiveness, that you may be feared." Forgiveness *with God* — located in Him, available from Him — exists because God provided what His own marking of iniquities required: a Savior who was marked with our iniquities in our place. "And he will redeem Israel from all his iniquities" — the psalm's last line is a prophecy, and its fulfillment has a name. Jesus, whose very name means "he will save his people from their sins" (Matthew 1:21), is the plenteous redemption (in the older wording) that Psalm 130 promises: "with him is plentiful redemption." Luther, who loved this psalm above most, called it a Pauline psalm — justification by faith, prayed from the bottom of the sea.

### Commentary

**Out of the depths (130:1–2).** The psalm begins at the bottom: "Out of the depths I cry to you, O LORD! O Lord, hear my voice! Let your ears be attentive to the voice of my pleas for mercy!" The depths — the Hebrew word is the deep waters, the sea-bottom of Psalm 69's flood and Jonah's descent. This is the sixth of the seven penitential psalms, and its depth is not merely circumstantial; it is the depth of a conscience that knows where it stands. There is no small talk in the depths, no bargaining position, no resume. There is only a voice, crying upward, pleading for the one thing the depths cannot produce: mercy. And the psalm's first comfort is directional: from the depths, the cry goes *up* — to a LORD whose ears can be attentive at any depth. No one has ever cried from a depth too deep for that hearing.

**If you should mark iniquities (130:3–4).** Then the psalm states the problem with terrifying clarity:

> "If you, O LORD, should mark iniquities, O Lord, who could stand?" — Psalm 130:3 (ESV)

*Mark* — keep a record, enter in a ledger, hold to account. If God ran the universe on strict ledger-keeping, the answer to the psalm's question is: nobody. No one could stand — not the psalmist, not the priest, not the reader. That is the law, and the psalm does not soften it. But verse 4 turns on the Psalter's great hinge-word — *but*: "But with you there is forgiveness, that you may be feared." Forgiveness is *with* God — it is something He has, in stock, in Himself, before we ask. And its purpose surprises: *that you may be feared*. Forgiveness does not make God casual to us; it makes Him awesome — the forgiven fear Him more, not less, because they have seen what their pardon cost His holiness. Cheap grace produces contempt; real forgiveness produces reverence. That is how you can tell them apart.

**More than watchmen for the morning (130:5–6).** "I wait for the LORD, my soul waits, and in his word I hope; my soul waits for the Lord more than watchmen for the morning, more than watchmen for the morning." Watchmen on a city wall in wartime, in the last black hour before dawn, longing for the light that means survival and relief — the psalmist waits *more* than that. The repetition is the sound of a man saying it twice because once cannot hold it. And notice what the waiting stands on: "in his *word* I hope." Not in feelings, not in signs — in a spoken promise. The watchman's certainty is the psalm's logic: morning is not a hope against the odds; it is a certainty on a schedule. The watchman does not wonder *whether* the sun will rise, only *when* — and the soul waiting on God's word has the same kind of certainty with a longer night.

**He will redeem Israel from all his iniquities (130:7–8).** The private prayer becomes a public sermon — the man in the depths ends by preaching to the congregation: "O Israel, hope in the LORD! For with the LORD there is steadfast love, and with him is plentiful redemption. And he will redeem Israel from all his iniquities." *Plentiful* redemption — the older translations' word catches it: redemption in bulk, more than enough, a supply that exceeds the inventory of sins. *All* his iniquities — the psalm that began with the terror of God marking iniquities ends with God redeeming from all of them. The ledger is not balanced; it is closed — by redemption, which is a purchase, which means a price was paid. The psalm does not name the price. The New Testament does: "In him we have redemption through his blood, the forgiveness of our trespasses" (Ephesians 1:7). Psalm 130 is the Old Testament's Romans, prayed from the sea floor, ending at the cross it could not yet see.

### Key verses

> "Out of the depths I cry to you, O LORD!" — Psalm 130:1 (ESV)

> "If you, O LORD, should mark iniquities, O Lord, who could stand? But with you there is forgiveness, that you may be feared." — Psalm 130:3–4 (ESV)

> "I wait for the LORD, my soul waits, and in his word I hope." — Psalm 130:5 (ESV)

> "My soul waits for the Lord more than watchmen for the morning, more than watchmen for the morning." — Psalm 130:6 (ESV)

> "O Israel, hope in the LORD! For with the LORD there is steadfast love, and with him is plentiful redemption. And he will redeem Israel from all his iniquities." — Psalm 130:7–8 (ESV)

### The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Psalm 130 is the question of verse 3, and it has no comfortable answer: if God marked iniquities — if the ledger were the last word — no one could stand. Not one. The depths are where honest people end up when they stop negotiating with that fact, and some of us are there tonight, with a record we cannot amend and a conscience that will not be talked out of it. But the good news is the *but* of verse 4: with God there is forgiveness. Not with our penance, our promises, or our improvements — *with Him*, in His keeping, provided at His cost. Jesus Christ is that forgiveness embodied: God marked our iniquities — on Him, at the cross — so that He would never mark them on us. And the redemption is plentiful: enough for the whole inventory, all his iniquities, yours included, with steadfast love left over. So wait for the LORD, more than watchmen for the morning. The morning has a name, and it has never once failed to rise.

**A note from the Reformers.** Luther ranked Psalm 130 among the greatest of the Psalms and called it Pauline — the doctrine of justification by faith alone, he said, is nowhere in the Old Testament clearer than in its "if you should mark iniquities" answered by "but with you there is forgiveness." He turned to it in his own depths and had his students sing it; his hymn "Out of the Depths I Cry to You" carried the psalm into the Reformation's bloodstream. Calvin stressed the psalm's order: the forgiveness is *with God* before it is *in us* — it does not wait to be manufactured by our sorrow; our sorrow waits on it, as watchmen on a morning that exists already. That order — God's supply before our seeking — is *sola gratia* in eight verses.

**Reflection question:** What depths are you crying from — and have you been waiting on your feelings to change, or on His word, which cannot? Take the psalm's logic personally this week: name the iniquities that would sink you if God marked them, then say verse 4 and verse 8 over them by name — "with you there is forgiveness… he will redeem Israel from *all* his iniquities." Where does the word *all* need to land in your record?

### Psalm 131 — I Have Calmed and Quieted My Soul: The Weaned Child

**Jesus in this psalm:** The shortest ascent and one of the most mature prayers in the Bible: "O LORD, my heart is not lifted up; my eyes are not raised too high; I do not occupy myself with things too great and too marvelous for me. But I have calmed and quieted my soul, like a weaned child with its mother." A weaned child does not grab; he rests against his mother, content with *her*, not merely her gifts. Jesus is the pattern of this humility — the Son who did not grasp, who received the cup and the cross from the Father's hand without occupying Himself with the why — and He is the LORD in whom Israel is told to hope "from this time forth and forevermore." Ambition, anxiety, and the need to understand everything are the unweaned soul's diet. This psalm is three verses of growing up.

**The key verse:**

> "But I have calmed and quieted my soul, like a weaned child with its mother; like a weaned child is my soul within me." — Psalm 131:2 (ESV)

*I have calmed* — the quiet was done, deliberately, the way a man stills a frightened animal: by staying near. Some peace is not found but practiced, one un-grasping at a time, until the soul learns the difference between being fed and being held.

### Psalm 132 — The LORD Swore to David a Sure Oath

**Jesus in this psalm:** The ascent that remembers David's vow — he would give his eyes no sleep until he found a place for the LORD — and answers it with God's greater oath: "The LORD swore to David a sure oath from which he will not turn back: 'One of the sons of your body I will set on your throne.'" Peter preached this oath at Pentecost: David, being a prophet, knew that God had sworn to set his descendant on his throne, "and he foresaw and spoke about the resurrection of the Christ" (Acts 2:30–31). Jesus is the son of David's body on David's throne — forever, because risen. And the psalm's picture of Zion chosen and blessed, her priests clothed with righteousness and her saints shouting for joy, is the church's own portrait in advance: a city God chose as His resting place forever, because the Ark — His presence, now Christ in the midst — has come home.

**The key verse:**

> "The LORD swore to David a sure oath from which he will not turn back: 'One of the sons of your body I will set on your throne.'" — Psalm 132:11 (ESV)

An oath God will not turn back from — the throne of David is not a historical artifact; it is a current address, occupied by a risen King. Every promise that hangs on that throne hangs on a swearing God, and He has never once turned back.

### Psalm 133 — How Good and Pleasant: The Oil and the Dew

**Jesus in this psalm:** Three verses on unity: "Behold, how good and pleasant it is when brothers dwell in unity!" It is like the oil running down Aaron's beard — consecration overflowing — and like the dew of Hermon falling on Zion — refreshment arriving from above. Jesus prayed this psalm's substance in His high-priestly prayer — "that they may all be one… so that the world may believe" (John 17) — and He is the head from whom the oil flows and the Christ whose blessing, commanded by the LORD, is "life forevermore." Unity among God's people is not manufactured by agreement; it descends, like dew and oil, from the Head to the body. Where Christ is honored as head, brothers find dwelling together not merely possible but pleasant.

**The key verse:**

> "Behold, how good and pleasant it is when brothers dwell in unity!" — Psalm 133:1 (ESV)

*Good and pleasant* — right and delightful at once, which is a rarer combination than it should be. The psalm does not say unity is easy; it says it is good, and that the blessing of life forevermore is commanded by God to land exactly there. Unity is not the church's decoration. It is her climate.

### Psalm 134 — Come, Bless the LORD: The Night Watch Blessing

**Jesus in this psalm:** The last of the ascents — the pilgrims, departing, bless the night-shift servants of the temple: "Come, bless the LORD, all you servants of the LORD, who stand by night in the house of the LORD! Lift up your hands to the holy place and bless the LORD!" And the servants answer with the pilgrims' traveling blessing: "May the LORD bless you from Zion, he who made heaven and earth!" Jesus is the true Servant who stood by night in God's house — praying through nights His disciples slept through — and He is the LORD who blesses from Zion, the Maker of heaven and earth, whose blessing now travels with every pilgrim out of the sanctuary and down the road home. Worship ends, in this psalm, the way it should: with mutual blessing, and God having the last word.

**The key verse:**

> "May the LORD bless you from Zion, he who made heaven and earth!" — Psalm 134:3 (ESV)

The Maker of heaven and earth, blessing *you*, from Zion — the largest God and the smallest word in the psalm, joined. Take the blessing out the door with you: the road home is traveled under a spoken benediction, not a vague hope.

### Psalm 135 — Praise the LORD, for the LORD Is Good: The God Who Does What He Pleases

**Jesus in this psalm:** A mosaic psalm of praise stitched from Exodus, Deuteronomy, and earlier psalms: "Praise the LORD, for the LORD is good; sing to his name, for it is pleasant! For the LORD has chosen Jacob for himself, Israel as his own possession." Its theology is sovereignty: "Whatever the LORD pleases, he does, in heaven and on earth, in the seas and all deeps" — set against the idols of silver and gold, made by human hands, that can do nothing at all. Jesus is the LORD who does what He pleases in the flesh — and what pleased Him was to save: it pleased the LORD to bruise Him for our iniquities, and the pleasure of the LORD prospers in His hand (Isaiah 53:10). The sovereignty that crushes idols is the same sovereignty that chose Jacob, chose a cross, and chooses sinners still.

**The key verse:**

> "Praise the LORD, for the LORD is good; sing to his name, for it is pleasant!" — Psalm 135:3 (ESV)

Goodness is the reason given for praise — not usefulness, not fear, not habit: He is *good*, and His name is *pleasant*. Praise that has forgotten the goodness becomes performance. This verse is the reset button: sing because He is good, and the singing will become pleasant again.

### Psalm 136 — His Steadfast Love Endures Forever: The Great Thanksgiving

**Jesus in this psalm:** The Great Hallel of thanksgiving — twenty-six verses, twenty-six times the same refrain: "for his steadfast love endures forever." Creation, Exodus, wilderness, conquest, and daily provision are each named and each answered with the refrain, because the psalm's doctrine is that every act of God, from making the heavens to giving food to all flesh, runs on one fuel: steadfast love that endures forever. Jesus is that love's fullest act — the greater Exodus this psalm's pattern predicts, led out not by a pillar but by a Person, struck down not as Pharaoh was but as the Lamb was — and the refrain reaches its final meaning at His table: a love that endures forever because the Lover rose and cannot die again. The church that sings all twenty-six refrains is not being repetitive. It is being accurate.

**The key verse:**

> "Give thanks to the LORD, for he is good, for his steadfast love endures forever." — Psalm 136:1 (ESV)

One refrain for creation and redemption alike — because in God's economy they are one story, told by one love. When you cannot find words for your own thanksgiving, borrow the psalm's method: name one act of God, then say the refrain. Repeat until the heart catches up. It will.

### Psalm 137 — By the Waters of Babylon: The Exiles' Tears

**Jesus in this psalm:** The exile's lament, sung — or rather, not sung: "By the waters of Babylon, there we sat down and wept, when we remembered Zion. On the willows there we hung up our lyres. For there our captors required of us songs." The exiles could not sing the LORD's song as entertainment in a foreign land, and their homesickness became holy defiance: "If I forget you, O Jerusalem, let my right hand forget its skill!" The psalm's ending — blessing pronounced on whoever dashes Babylon's infants against the rock — is the Psalter's hardest imprecation, and it must be read as what it is: the cry of a violated people handing vengeance to God in the language of the lex talionis, not a command for God's people to execute. Jesus stands in this psalm as the greater Exile — weeping over Jerusalem as they wept by the rivers, carrying His people's captivity into His own forsakenness — and as the Judge to whom all such cries are finally referred, who will repay Babylon in righteousness so that His people never repay her themselves.

**The key verse:**

> "By the waters of Babylon, there we sat down and wept, when we remembered Zion." — Psalm 137:1 (ESV)

Weeping *when we remembered* — memory is the exile's pain and the exile's hope in one act: you cannot weep for a Zion you have forgotten. If your heart still aches for the home you have not yet seen, the ache itself is evidence: exiles who remember are exiles who will sing again.

### Psalm 138 — I Give You Thanks, O LORD, with My Whole Heart

**Jesus in this psalm:** David's thanksgiving before the gods — bold, public, wholehearted — grounded in God's answer to prayer: "On the day I called, you answered me; my strength of soul you increased." Its reach is royal and global: "All the kings of the earth shall give you thanks, O LORD, for they have heard the words of your mouth" — and its comfort is God's lowliness: "For though the LORD is high, he regards the lowly, but the haughty he knows from afar." Jesus is the lowly-regarding LORD in person — high, and regarding the lowly at close range — and the psalm's great closing confidence is His promise to every believer: "The LORD will fulfill his purpose for me; your steadfast love, O LORD, endures forever. Do not forsake the work of your hands." The work of God's hands is His people; He does not forsake His own workmanship.

**The key verse:**

> "The LORD will fulfill his purpose for me; your steadfast love, O LORD, endures forever. Do not forsake the work of your hands." — Psalm 138:8 (ESV)

*Will fulfill* — the purpose is God's, so the fulfilling is God's, and the tense is certain. And the plea underneath it is unanswerable: do not forsake the work of *your* hands. God has never once abandoned a project He started. You are not going to be the first.

## Psalm 139 — You Have Searched Me and Known Me: Fearfully and Wonderfully Made

### Jesus in this psalm

Jesus is the God who searches and knows in Psalm 139 — and He is also the Man who was searched, known, knit together, and written in God's book before He was born. The psalm's omniscience ("You know when I sit down and when I rise up; you discern my thoughts from afar"), its omnipresence ("If I ascend to heaven, you are there! If I make my bed in Sheol, you are there!"), and its embryology ("Your eyes saw my unformed substance; in your book were written, every one of them, the days that were formed for me") all describe the God who, in Jesus, came to be known by us as He knows us. And the incarnation means the searched life was lived from inside: Jesus was knit together in Mary's womb under the same watchful eyes, His days written in the same book — including the day of the cross, which was in the book before the foundation of the world. When the psalm ends, "Search me, O God, and know my heart!" — it is praying to a God who has already searched, already knows, and in Christ has already answered for what He found. There is no fact about you that can surprise Him into leaving.

### Commentary

**You have searched me and known me (139:1–6).** The psalm opens with a confession that is either terrifying or comforting, depending on where you stand with the Searcher: "O LORD, you have searched me and known me! You know when I sit down and when I rise up; you discern my thoughts from afar. You search out my path and my lying down and are acquainted with all my ways. Even before a word is on my tongue, behold, O LORD, you know it altogether." Searched — the word is mining language, dug through. God does not know us the way we know facts; He knows us the way a searcher knows what he has excavated. Every thought, discerned from afar before it forms; every word, known before it is spoken; every way, already familiar to Him. "You hem me in, behind and before, and lay your hand upon me." Hemmed in — surrounded by knowledge, front and back — and a hand laid on: the touch of ownership and of care. David's verdict on all this omniscience is not fear but wonder: "Such knowledge is too wonderful for me; it is high; I cannot attain it." The psalm begins where theology should begin: on its knees, astonished that it is known.

**Where shall I go from your Spirit? (139:7–12).** Then the famous flight that cannot be flown: "Where shall I go from your Spirit? Or where shall I flee from your presence? If I ascend to heaven, you are there! If I make my bed in Sheol, you are there! If I take the wings of the morning and dwell in the uttermost parts of the sea, even there your hand shall lead me, and your right hand shall hold me." Heaven and Sheol, dawn and the far sea — the vertical and horizontal extremes of the ancient world — and in every coordinate, the same discovery: *you are there*; and more, *your hand shall lead me, your right hand shall hold me*. This is not the lament of a man trying to escape an eye; it is the amazement of a man discovering there is no outside to God's presence — Jonah learned it in the fish, Adam and Eve learned it behind the trees, and every runaway since has learned it at the end of the road: the presence we flee is the presence that saves us when we stop. Even the darkness is disqualified as a hiding place: "Even the darkness is not dark to you; the night is bright as the day, for darkness is as light with you." Your darkest chapter is, to God, fully lit. Nothing in it is hidden from Him — and nothing in it is beyond His working in it.

**Fearfully and wonderfully made (139:13–16).** The psalm now goes back before memory, to the womb: "For you formed my inward parts; you knitted me together in my mother's womb. I praise you, for I am fearfully and wonderfully made. Wonderful are your works; my soul knows it very well." Knitted — handcrafted, stitch by stitch, by God Himself, in the dark, before anyone else saw. The sanctity of every human life is grounded here, before any question of usefulness or wantedness: a person is God's workmanship from the first cell. "My frame was not hidden from you, when I was being made in secret, intricately woven in the depths of the earth. Your eyes saw my unformed substance; in your book were written, every one of them, the days that were formed for me, when as yet there was none of them." A book, written before birth, containing every day — the psalm's doctrine of providence at its most personal: your days are not improvised. They were written — by a hand that also wrote the day of your deliverance, and the day of your homecoming, in the same book.

**How precious are your thoughts (139:17–18).** David turns from God's knowledge of him to his knowledge of God's thoughts: "How precious to me are your thoughts, O God! How vast is the sum of them! If I would count them, they are more than the sand. I awake, and I am still with you." More than the sand — God's thoughts toward His people outnumber the shore. And the last line of the section is a whole spirituality in nine words: "I awake, and I am still with you." Sleep — our nightly rehearsal of death, our hours of total helplessness — does not interrupt the presence. He does not leave when we lose consciousness. We wake, and the first fact of the day is not our to-do list; it is *still with you*.

**Search me, O God (139:19–24).** The psalm's final movement startles modern readers: David prays against the wicked — "Oh that you would slay the wicked, O God!… Do I not hate those who hate you, O LORD?" — the zeal of a man who has just contemplated God's holiness and cannot be neutral toward those who rise up against Him. Read honestly, it is loyalty, not personal vendetta: the enemies in view are God's. And then the psalm lands on the prayer that makes the whole thing safe to pray — David turns the searching on himself: "Search me, O God, and know my heart! Try me and know my thoughts! And see if there be any grievous way in me, and lead me in the way everlasting!" The man who began by confessing "you have searched me and known me" ends by *asking* to be searched. That is the journey of the whole psalm: from being known, to wanting to be known — from omniscience as a fact, to omniscience as an invitation. And the destination is the way everlasting: searched, known, corrected, and led, by a God from whom the psalmist no longer wishes to flee a single step.

### Key verses

> "O LORD, you have searched me and known me! You know when I sit down and when I rise up; you discern my thoughts from afar." — Psalm 139:1–2 (ESV)

> "Where shall I go from your Spirit? Or where shall I flee from your presence? If I ascend to heaven, you are there! If I make my bed in Sheol, you are there!" — Psalm 139:7–8 (ESV)

> "For you formed my inward parts; you knitted me together in my mother's womb. I praise you, for I am fearfully and wonderfully made." — Psalm 139:13–14 (ESV)

> "Your eyes saw my unformed substance; in your book were written, every one of them, the days that were formed for me, when as yet there was none of them." — Psalm 139:16 (ESV)

> "Search me, O God, and know my heart! Try me and know my thoughts! And see if there be any grievous way in me, and lead me in the way everlasting!" — Psalm 139:23–24 (ESV)

### The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Psalm 139 is that there is nowhere to hide and nothing hidden: God has searched us and knows us — every thought from afar, every word before it is spoken, every grievous way we have explained away to ourselves. Before this God, anonymity is impossible and amnesia is not a defense; our days are in a book we did not write, and some of its pages shame us. But the good news is that the Searcher is the Savior. The God who knows us altogether — who knew the worst before He called us — has in Jesus Christ laid His hand on us not to expose us to ruin but to hem us in for keeping: behind and before, in heaven and in Sheol, in the womb and in the morning after our worst night, "I awake, and I am still with you." In Christ, being fully known and being fully loved are the same experience. So the psalm's last prayer can be prayed without flinching: search me — because the One searching has already paid for everything He will find, and He searches in order to lead: in the way everlasting.

**A note from the Reformers.** Calvin called Psalm 139 one of the most profound meditations on God's providence in all Scripture, and he drew the pastoral line the church has drawn ever since: God's knowledge of us is not the surveillance of a tyrant but the acquaintance of a Creator and Father — He is "acquainted with all my ways" the way a maker knows his work and a shepherd his sheep. Spurgeon lingered on "I awake, and I am still with you" as the believer's morning text for life: we do not re-establish contact with God each day; we wake into a presence that never broke. And the Reformers together read the psalm's ending as the right use of all this doctrine: omniscience rightly believed produces not speculation but the prayer "Search me" — doctrine that does not end in self-examination and surrender has not been believed, only admired.

**Reflection question:** Which part of being fully known by God still feels like a threat to you — and what would it change to receive it, through Christ, as a comfort? Pray the psalm's last prayer this week, slowly and specifically: "Search me, O God… see if there be any grievous way in me" — and when He shows you one, do not run (there is nowhere to run). Confess it, and let Him lead you in the way everlasting instead.

### Psalm 140 — Deliver Me, O LORD, from Evil Men

**Jesus in this psalm:** David prays against violent men whose hearts devise evil, whose tongues are sharp as a serpent's, with the venom of asps under their lips — language Paul quotes in Romans 3 as part of the universal indictment of mankind. The psalm's confidence is God's partiality toward the poor: "I know that the LORD will maintain the cause of the afflicted, and will execute justice for the needy." Jesus prayed this psalm's situation — evil men devising, tongues sharpened, traps and cords spread along His path — and He is both its answer and its pattern: the afflicted One whose cause the Father maintained at the resurrection, and the coming Judge who executes justice for the needy. The upright shall dwell in God's presence; the violent are already sentenced by the God who hears the afflicted first.

**The key verse:**

> "I know that the LORD will maintain the cause of the afflicted, and will execute justice for the needy." — Psalm 140:12 (ESV)

"I know" — in a psalm full of traps and venom, the sufferer's knowledge is the still point. The afflicted often have no advocate anyone can see. This verse says they have the only Advocate who cannot lose, and that He has a known bias: toward the afflicted and the needy.

---

### Psalm 141 — Let My Prayer Be Counted as Incense

**Jesus in this psalm:** David's evening prayer — a prayer about praying, and about the mouth: "Set a guard, O LORD, over my mouth; keep watch over the door of my lips! Do not let my heart incline to any evil." He refuses the delicacies of the wicked, welcomes the righteous man's rebuke as oil on his head, and keeps his eyes on the LORD while traps are set around him. Its opening became the church's evening liturgy: "Let my prayer be counted as incense before you, and the lifting up of my hands as the evening sacrifice!" In Revelation, the prayers of the saints *are* the incense in golden bowls before the throne (Revelation 5:8) — and Jesus is the reason they rise: His intercession perfumes them, His sacrifice is the true evening offering, and His guarded lips — silent before His accusers — are the pattern for the mouth this psalm asks God to guard.

**The key verse:**

> "Let my prayer be counted as incense before you, and the lifting up of my hands as the evening sacrifice!" — Psalm 141:2 (ESV)

Your prayer, offered in Jesus' name, arrives in heaven as incense arrives in a sanctuary — as something fragrant, welcomed, and attended to. The hands you lift may shake; the counting is God's, and He counts them as worship.

---

### Psalm 142 — No One Cares for My Soul: The Cave Psalm

**Jesus in this psalm:** Another cave psalm — David in the cave of Adullam or En Gedi, at the end of his resources: "With my voice I cry out to the LORD… I pour out my complaint before him; I tell my trouble before him. When my spirit faints within me, you know my way!" The loneliest line in the Psalter is here: "Look to the right and see: there is none who takes notice of me; no refuge remains to me; no one cares for my soul." Jesus prayed this psalm's desolation in full — no one took notice; His own cared and fled — and He is the answer to its turn: "I cry to you, O LORD; I say, 'You are my refuge, my portion in the land of the living.'" When every human refuge fails, the portion remains. The psalm ends with a promise spoken from inside the cave: "The righteous will surround me, for you will deal bountifully with me" — the cave-dweller prophesying a congregation.

**The key verse:**

> "I cry to you, O LORD; I say, 'You are my refuge, my portion in the land of the living.'" — Psalm 142:5 (ESV)

When the count of your helpers reaches zero, the arithmetic of this verse still works: refuge and portion are not people, and they cannot flee. A man with God as his portion is not a man with nothing. He is a man with the one thing the cave cannot take.

---

### Psalm 143 — My Spirit Faints Within Me: The Last Penitential Psalm

**Jesus in this psalm:** The seventh and last penitential psalm: David, pursued, dwelling in darkness like those long dead, spirit fainting and heart appalled — and pleading on the only ground a sinner can: "Enter not into judgment with your servant, for no one living is righteous before you." Paul built the doctrine of justification on that sentence (Romans 3:20; Galatians 2:16). The psalm reaches back — "I remember the days of old; I meditate on all that you have done" — and reaches forward: "Let me hear in the morning of your steadfast love, for in you I trust." Jesus is the righteous Servant who *could* enter into judgment and stand — and who instead stood in judgment for servants who could not, so that David's plea is answered in Him: God does not enter into judgment with us, because He entered into judgment with His Son. The morning hearing of steadfast love is now the daily bread of the justified.

**The key verse:**

> "Let me hear in the morning of your steadfast love, for in you I trust. Make me know the way I should go, for to you I lift up my soul." — Psalm 143:8 (ESV)

The first sound of the day, by this prayer, is not the news or the dread but the steadfast love — heard, in the Word, before the feet touch the floor. And the lifted soul is shown the way: guidance in this psalm is not given to the self-sufficient, but to the upturned.

---

### Psalm 144 — Blessed Be the LORD, My Rock: What Is Man?

**Jesus in this psalm:** David's royal war psalm borrows Psalm 8's wonder and aims it at the battlefield: "Blessed be the LORD, my rock, who trains my hands for war… O LORD, what is man that you regard him, or the son of man that you think of him? Man is like a breath; his days are like a passing shadow." Between the rock and the breath stands the whole gospel: the eternal God regarding breath-like man, stooping — "Stretch out your hand from on high; rescue me" — and the psalm ends not in war but in a beatitude over a peaceful people: sons like full-grown plants, daughters like corner pillars, barns full, sheep multiplying, no cry of distress in the streets. "Blessed are the people to whom such blessings fall! Blessed are the people whose God is the LORD!" Jesus is that LORD — the Rock who trains hands, the hand stretched out from on high, and the King whose reign turns war psalms into harvest songs.

**The key verse:**

> "O LORD, what is man that you regard him, or the son of man that you think of him?" — Psalm 144:3 (ESV)

Psalm 8 asked it under the stars; Psalm 144 asks it under enemy fire — and the wonder survives both settings. God regards breath-like man: that is either the most arrogant thought ever thought or the plain truth of the gospel. The cross settles which.

---

### Psalm 145 — I Will Extol You, My God and King: The Great Acrostic of Praise

**Jesus in this psalm:** David's last psalm in the Psalter's arrangement — an acrostic of pure praise, and the doorway to the final Hallelujah: "I will extol you, my God and King, and bless your name forever and ever. Every day I will bless you and praise your name forever and ever." It celebrates a kingdom that outlasts generations, works that one generation commends to another, and a character summed in Exodus' creed: "The LORD is gracious and merciful, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love. The LORD is good to all, and his mercy is over all that he has made." Its tenderness is practical: "The LORD upholds all who are falling and raises up all who are bowed down… You open your hand; you satisfy the desire of every living thing… The LORD is near to all who call on him." Jesus is this King — gracious, upholding the falling, opening His hand, near to callers — and His kingdom is the one of which this psalm says, "Your kingdom is an everlasting kingdom, and your dominion endures throughout all generations."

**The key verse:**

> "The LORD is gracious and merciful, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love." — Psalm 145:8 (ESV)

The creed of Exodus 34, sung by David, embodied by Jesus. When every other description of God is forgotten, the church keeps this one in front: gracious, merciful, slow to anger, abounding in steadfast love. It is not a summary of our experience of Him on our best days. It is His name, on all His days.

---

### Psalm 146 — Put Not Your Trust in Princes: The First Hallelujah

**Jesus in this psalm:** The final five psalms each begin and end with "Praise the LORD!" — and the first of them begins with a warning against misplaced trust: "Put not your trust in princes, in a son of man, in whom there is no salvation. When his breath departs, he returns to the earth; on that very day his plans perish." Against perishing princes stands the Maker of heaven and earth, who keeps faith forever — and whose résumé is a list of mercies: justice for the oppressed, food for the hungry, freedom for prisoners, sight for the blind, lifting for the bowed down, watch over sojourners, upholding of widow and fatherless. Jesus read His own ministry off a list like this (Luke 7:22 — the blind receive their sight, the lame walk, the poor have good news preached to them): He is the LORD who executes this psalm, reigning from Zion to all generations — beginning now, in every act of His church that copies the résumé.

**The key verse:**

> "Blessed is he whose help is the God of Jacob, whose hope is in the LORD his God." — Psalm 146:5 (ESV)

Help and hope — the psalm locates both in a Person, not in a prince. Every generation gets to choose its trustee again, and every generation of princes perishes on schedule. The God of Jacob — who helped a deceiver and hoped in no one — is still open for trust.

---

### Psalm 147 — He Heals the Brokenhearted: The Rebuilder's Praise

**Jesus in this psalm:** Jerusalem rebuilt, exiles gathered — and the God who does it is described in the Psalter's most tender juxtaposition: "He heals the brokenhearted and binds up their wounds. He determines the number of the stars; he gives to all of them their names." The star-counter is the wound-binder; the hands that run the galaxies dress the injuries of the brokenhearted. Isaiah took this psalm's lines for the anointed one's job description — "He has sent me to bind up the brokenhearted" — and Jesus stood up in Nazareth and claimed the reading (Luke 4:18). He is the healer of this psalm in person, the LORD who builds up Jerusalem into a people gathered from every exile, who strengthens the bars of her gates and fills her with the finest of wheat — and who still takes pleasure not in the strength of horses but in those who fear Him and hope in His steadfast love.

**The key verse:**

> "He heals the brokenhearted and binds up their wounds." — Psalm 147:3 (ESV)

Binding is slow, close work — bandage by bandage, hand on the injury. That is the verb the star-naming God chooses for broken hearts. Your wound is not beneath His notice; it is on His rounds. He does this personally, and He is good at it.

---

### Psalm 148 — Praise the LORD from the Heavens: The Universal Choir

**Jesus in this psalm:** The praise widens to the whole creation: angels and hosts, sun and moon and shining stars, the highest heavens and the waters above them — "Let them praise the name of the LORD! For he commanded and they were created." Then the earth answers: sea monsters and deeps, fire and hail, snow and mist, mountains and cedars, beasts and birds — and finally man, in every rank and season: kings and peoples, princes and rulers, young men and maidens, old men and children. Jesus is the LORD whose name is alone exalted in this psalm, the Creator through whom every voice in the choir was made — and He is the "horn" God has raised for His people, "the praise of all his saints, for the people of Israel who are near to him." The choir is tuning: every creature named in this psalm will be present at the final concert, and the church is its rehearsing section.

**The key verse:**

> "Let them praise the name of the LORD, for his name alone is exalted; his majesty is above earth and heaven." — Psalm 148:13 (ESV)

*His name alone* — in a choir of everything, the solo belongs to one name. Earth and heaven together are not high enough to be above His majesty; they are merely its footstool and its sounding board. Praise is not the creation flattering its Maker. It is the creation telling the truth at full volume.

---

### Psalm 149 — Sing to the LORD a New Song: The Saints' High Praises

**Jesus in this psalm:** The second-to-last psalm arms the choir: "Praise the LORD! Sing to the LORD a new song, his praise in the assembly of the godly! Let Israel be glad in his Maker; let the children of Zion rejoice in their King!" — with dancing, tambourine, and lyre, because "the LORD takes pleasure in his people; he adorns the humble with salvation." And then the startling image: "Let the high praises of God be in their throats and two-edged swords in their hands" — the saints executing the written judgment, binding kings with chains. The church reads this soberly and spiritually: our sword is the Word of God, our warfare is not against flesh and blood, and the judgment executed is the gospel's verdict on the powers — already written, already accomplished at the cross, and to be completed when the King returns. Jesus is the King rejoiced in, the pleasure-taking LORD, and the coming executor of the written judgment — which is why His people can sing now with full throats: the outcome is not in doubt, only the date is unannounced.

**The key verse:**

> "For the LORD takes pleasure in his people; he adorns the humble with salvation." — Psalm 149:4 (ESV)

*Takes pleasure* — God is not merely patient with His people; He delights in them, and His delight takes the form of adorning: He dresses the humble in salvation the way a bride is dressed. If you are His, heaven's opinion of you is not tolerance. It is pleasure, with ornaments.

---

### Psalm 150 — Let Everything That Has Breath Praise the LORD

**Jesus in this psalm:** The Psalter's final word is a crescendo with no doctrine left to argue — only praise to perform: "Praise the LORD! Praise God in his sanctuary; praise him in his mighty heavens! Praise him for his mighty deeds; praise him according to his excellent greatness!" Trumpet, lute, harp, tambourine, dance, strings, pipe, clashing cymbals — every instrument in the ancient world is conscripted, loud cymbals and *loud* clashing cymbals, as if the volume itself were a doctrine: this God is to be praised at full strength. And the final verse gathers the whole book's journey into one sentence: "Let everything that has breath praise the LORD! Praise the LORD!" Jesus is the LORD praised here — the mighty deeds are His deeds, the excellent greatness is His — and He is the leader of this choir, the risen Singer in the midst of the congregation (Psalm 22:22), whose breath was given back to Him in resurrection so that the praise would never stop. The Psalter began with one blessed man meditating day and night. It ends with everything that breathes, praising. That is where the road goes. Breathe, then — and praise.

**The key verse:**

> "Let everything that has breath praise the LORD! Praise the LORD!" — Psalm 150:6 (ESV)

Breath is the only qualification, and breath is the one thing every reader of this page currently has. The book that taught the afflicted to cry, the guilty to confess, and the dying to hope ends by spending its last breath the way all breath is finally meant to be spent: praising the LORD. Hallelujah. Amen.

---

## Closing — The Psalms in One Sentence

One hundred and fifty psalms, and they can be carried in a single sentence:

**Jesus Christ prayed this book, fulfilled this book, and now leads His people in praying it — the Shepherd and the sheep's true voice, the King and the sufferer, the forsaken One who was heard and the risen One who hears — so that everyone who prays the Psalms in His name finds, in every season of the soul, a God who has already been there in the flesh and has come back with mercy.**

In the "Jesus in Every Book of the Bible" walkthrough, this is the testimony of the Psalter: **Jesus in Psalms is the Singer and the Song — the One who prayed our prayers before us, bore our sins beneath them, and now puts His own words in our mouths for every night of the soul.** Other books tell us what God has done; the Psalms teach us what to *say* to God about it — in praise and in protest, in confession and in dancing, out of the depths and at the top of the ascent. And at every point on that road, the same Christ is revealed: the blessed Man of Psalm 1 and the King of Psalm 2, the forsaken of Psalm 22 and the Shepherd of Psalm 23, the clean heart's Creator longed for in Psalm 51 and the cornerstone rejected and raised in Psalm 118, the Keeper of Psalm 121 and the Redeemer of Psalm 130 — until the last page, where everything that has breath is praising Him, and the singing does not stop.

If you have walked through this commentary psalm by psalm, you have heard the five watchwords of the Reformers sound on every page, because they are the Psalter's watchwords first: Scripture alone, as the Word is lamp and light through the longest psalm and the darkest night; grace alone, as forgiveness meets the confessed before the confession is cold; faith alone, as trust — the Psalms' own heartbeat word — receives what hands could never earn; Christ alone, as every road in the book, from the King's highway to the exile's tears, arrives at Him; and glory to God alone, as the final five psalms spend everything on praise. Hold those five, keep the law and the gospel distinct, and you will pray not only the Psalms but the whole Christian life the way they ask to be prayed.

The last word belongs to the book itself, and it is an invitation more than an ending. The Psalms were not given to be admired from a distance. They were given to be prayed — by you, tonight, in whatever season your soul is in. The depths have a psalm. The sickbed has a psalm. The cave, the battlefield, the sleepless night, the morning of deliverance, the day of dancing — all of them have psalms, and every one of them has been prayed by Jesus ahead of you and is now prayed by Jesus alongside you. Open the book. Borrow the words. They are yours in Him.

### A Closing Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, our Shepherd and our Song,

We thank You that You prayed this book before we ever opened it — that You thirsted with Psalm 42, trusted with Psalm 27, confessed the sin of the world as if it were Your own, cried Psalm 22 from the cross, and committed Your spirit with Psalm 31 on Your lips. We thank You that there is no depth in these pages You have not sounded, and no height of praise You do not now lead.

We confess that our own prayers are often wordless, wandering, or silent — that we have let our souls do the talking when we should have preached to them, and that we have forgotten Your benefits more times than we can count. Teach us this book. Put its words in our mouths at 2 a.m. and at the kitchen table. Make us people who bless You at all times, who cast our burdens instead of carrying them, who wait for You more than watchmen for the morning, and who end — as Your book ends — with everything we have breathing praise.

Keep us, Keeper of Israel, in our going out and our coming in, from this time forth and forevermore. Search us, and know our hearts, and lead us in the way everlasting. And let all that we are and all that we do be Yours alone — Scripture our rule, grace our ground, faith our hand, Christ our Savior, and glory to God alone, forever.

Christ Proclaimed. Jesus is Lord.

Amen.
