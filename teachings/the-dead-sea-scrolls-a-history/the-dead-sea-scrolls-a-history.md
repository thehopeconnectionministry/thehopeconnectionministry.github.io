# The Dead Sea Scrolls — A History

### The Word God Hid in Jars, and Brought Out at the Right Time

*The Hope Connection — Lewis Fuller*

---

## 1. A Stone, a Cave, and a Thousand Years

Sometime in late 1946 or early 1947 — the finders themselves were never precise about the date, and honest history admits it — a Bedouin shepherd boy of the Ta'amireh tribe was searching the cliffs northwest of the Dead Sea for a stray goat. The place was a wilderness of rock and heat near the ruins the Arabs called Khirbet Qumran, some thirteen miles east of Jerusalem, in one of the lowest and driest landscapes on the face of the earth. According to the story the shepherds themselves told afterward, the boy threw a stone into the opening of a cave — and instead of the sound of rock on rock, he heard pottery break.

What he and his companions found, when they came back and climbed inside, were tall clay jars, some of them intact, and in the jars were scrolls: leather scrolls, wrapped in linen, dark with age. The boys had no idea what they were holding. They carried seven of them out, and in time the scrolls passed to a Bethlehem cobbler and part-time antiquities dealer named Kando, and from him to scholars — among them Eleazar Sukenik of the Hebrew University, who obtained three of the scrolls in November of 1947, in the very days when the United Nations was voting a Jewish state back into existence, and who understood at once what he was touching. It took years for the full size of the discovery to come clear, as cave after cave gave up its contents, but the headline was there from the beginning: the oldest copies of the Hebrew Bible the world had ever seen, hidden in jars for two thousand years, had come back into the light in the lifetime of people still living today.

Men have thrown a great many stones in that wilderness across the centuries. That one struck pottery. We do not have to call it luck. The God who inspired the Scriptures had also, in His providence, seen to their keeping — and part of His keeping was done in clay jars, in the dark, in caves, by men whose names we do not know, for readers who would not be born for two thousand years. The prophet Isaiah had written the promise into the very book that cave preserved best:

> "The grass withers, the flower fades, but the word of our God will stand forever." — Isaiah 40:8 (ESV)

This is the story of those scrolls: who hid them, what was in them, what they proved, and why a believer today can hold his Bible with a steadier hand because a shepherd boy had a good throwing arm.

## 2. Qumran and the Essenes: The Men Who Hid the Scrolls

The ruins at Qumran were not a natural fortress or a trading post. Excavated in the 1950s, they proved to be the settlement of a Jewish religious community that lived there from roughly 150 years before Christ until the year 68 after Him — a community most scholars identify, on strong evidence, with the Essenes, the third great party of ancient Judaism alongside the Pharisees and Sadducees. The ancient writers describe them: Josephus, who knew them well; Philo of Alexandria; and Pliny the Elder, who locates a celibate community of Essenes on the western shore of the Dead Sea, above Ein Gedi — which is to say, exactly here. The men of Qumran called themselves the Yahad, the Community, and in their own writings they called themselves the Sons of Light.

They had come to the desert in protest and in hope. In their judgment, the priesthood in Jerusalem had become corrupt — the high priesthood seized by the Hasmonean kings, the temple calendar wrongly kept, the holy place defiled by compromise. Under a leader they remembered as the Teacher of Righteousness, they withdrew to the wilderness to, in Isaiah's words as they read them, prepare the way of the Lord. What the excavators found matches the writings: ritual baths cut into the rock and fed by aqueducts, for the constant washings their purity rules required; a pantry stacked with the remains of hundreds of dishes, beside a long assembly room where the community ate its common meals in ranked silence; a cemetery of more than a thousand graves stretching toward the sea; and a room with the remains of long plastered tables and two inkwells — identified by the excavators as the scriptorium, the room where the scrolls were copied. Not every detail of that identification is beyond dispute, and honesty says so; but that this community treasured, studied, and copied Scripture is beyond dispute entirely. Their library was their life.

And it was a library under a death sentence, though they could not have known the date. In the year 66 the Jewish revolt against Rome began, and in 68 the Roman legions under Vespasian swept down the Jordan valley, taking Jericho and closing on the Dead Sea. The community at Qumran was destroyed that year and never rebuilt. Before the end came — this is the only reasonable reading of the evidence — the scrolls of the community's library, and scrolls perhaps brought from elsewhere for safekeeping, were wrapped in linen, sealed in jars, and hidden in the caves in the cliffs and marl terraces round about. It was what you did with holy things when the army was coming. The men who hid them presumably expected to return. They never did. The jars waited in the dark, in the driest air in the world, while Rome fell and Islam rose and crusaders and Ottomans and empires passed over the land above them — waited, in fact, until the generation that would dig up the Great Isaiah Scroll was the same generation that most needed the assurance it carried.

## 3. What Was Found: Eleven Caves and Nine Hundred Scrolls

The first cave was only the beginning. Between 1947 and 1956, searchers — Bedouin first, then archaeologists racing them from cave to cave — found eleven caves in the Qumran cliffs that contained scrolls. Cave 4, discovered in 1952, held the mother lode: the shattered remains of some three quarters of the whole library, tens of thousands of fragments that scholars have been piecing together, like the world's largest jigsaw puzzle, ever since. Cave 3 gave up the Copper Scroll, two rolls of engraved metal listing buried treasure that has never been found. Cave 11, found in 1956, held the Temple Scroll, the longest of them all at more than twenty-eight feet, and a magnificent scroll of the Psalms.

All told, the caves yielded the remains of roughly nine hundred manuscripts. About a quarter of them are copies of Scripture — some two hundred and thirty biblical scrolls, written between about 250 years before Christ and the year 68. Every book of the Old Testament is represented, with one famous exception: Esther. And here honesty requires the small print, gladly given. Ezra survives only in scraps, and Nehemiah — which in that age was usually reckoned one book with Ezra, "Ezra-Nehemiah" — is represented by, at most, a single tiny fragment identified among the Cave 4 pieces many years after the first finds; so the plain short answer remains: every book but Esther. Why Esther is missing, no one knows. It may be chance — not everything survived. It may be that this community, which quarreled with the Jerusalem calendar, had little love for a book that institutes a feast not commanded in the Law and never names the name of God. We can note the question honestly and leave it where the evidence leaves it. What no one can miss is the mountain on the other side of the scale: the whole counsel of Israel's Scriptures, copied and kept by a desert sect, down to the last prophets and psalms.

The crown of the find was in the first cave, among the first seven scrolls: the Great Isaiah Scroll. It is a complete copy of the book of Isaiah — all sixty-six chapters, fifty-four columns of Hebrew written on seventeen sheets of leather sewn together, a scroll more than twenty-four feet long. Paleography and radiocarbon agree in dating it to roughly 125 years before Christ. To grasp what that means, hold it against what the world had before 1947: the oldest complete Hebrew Bibles in existence, the great Masoretic codices like the Aleppo Codex, date from around the year 930 after Christ — more than a thousand years later than the scroll in the cave. In one discovery, the documentary evidence for the text of Isaiah jumped backward across an entire millennium, to within a long lifetime of the Maccabees. Scholars opened the scroll and began, line by line, to compare it with the Isaiah in their printed Bibles. What they found is the subject of the next section, and it is the reason this history belongs on a shelf built for giving people hope.

## 4. What the Scrolls Proved: A Thousand Years, and the Text Stands

The comparison was made, and the verdict is now a matter of settled record: across a thousand years of hand copying — from the scroll written before Christ to the codices written a thousand years after Him — the book of Isaiah had come down essentially unchanged. The differences between the Great Isaiah Scroll and the medieval text number in the thousands if you count everything, but when you sort them, they crumble into almost nothing: the great majority are differences of spelling — Hebrew spelling changed its conventions across the centuries, as English did — along with small slips of a copyist's pen, a word order here, a dropped or doubled letter there. They alter no doctrine. They scarcely alter a sentence. The Isaiah preached from a thousand pulpits for a thousand years was the Isaiah in the cave. The scribes of Israel, counting letters and burying flawed copies, had done their work — or rather, the God who promised that His word would stand forever had done His, through their counting.

And there is one place in the scroll where the reading is not merely equal to ours but, if anything, sharper. The fifty-third chapter of Isaiah — the Suffering Servant, the chapter that reads so much like an eyewitness account of the crucifixion that unbelieving scholars have strained for centuries to explain it — stands in the Great Isaiah Scroll exactly as it stands in your Bible, written down more than a century before Bethlehem:

> "But he was pierced for our transgressions; he was crushed for our iniquities; upon him was the chastisement that brought us peace, and with his wounds we are healed." — Isaiah 53:5 (ESV)

Pierced. Crushed. Wounds that heal us. In a scroll older than the event, older than the Roman Empire's founding, copied by a sectarian scribe who did not know what he was looking at. No Christian hand touched that text — the men who copied it were waiting for a messiah of their own imagining, and would have rejected the crucified Jesus as theirs. That is what makes the witness so powerful: it is hostile custody. The church's opponents' own library proves that the church did not invent Isaiah 53 after the fact to fit Jesus; the prophecy sat in the cave, in black and white, a century and more before the cross, waiting. In one detail the scroll even recovers a word the medieval text had lost: where the later Hebrew copies of Isaiah 53:11 read that the Servant would "see" the fruit of his anguish, the scroll — agreeing with the ancient Greek translation — reads that he shall see *light*. The ESV carries the scroll's reading in its footnote to that verse. Think of it: after the darkness of the cross, the Servant sees light. A shepherd's cave gave that word back to the church.

Honesty about the variants belongs here too, as it does everywhere on this shelf. The scrolls do show that the biblical text circulated in the last centuries before Christ in more than one form — most copies stand very close to the text our Old Testament follows, some stand closer to the Hebrew behind the Greek Septuagint, a few resemble the Samaritan tradition — and scholars debate what that diversity means. What it does not mean is that the text was fluid or invented. Across every family of copies, in cave after cave, it is recognizably, stubbornly the same Bible — the same Isaiah, the same Psalms, the same Moses. The variants are the honest wear of hand copying, and the wonder is not that there are small differences. The wonder, after two thousand years of caves and empires and fires, is how few and how small they are.

## 5. The Other Writings: A Window into the World Jesus Entered

Only about a quarter of the library is Scripture. The rest — commentaries, rule books, hymns, calendars, prayers — is the community's own literature, and its value is different but real: it is the fullest window we have ever been given into the Judaism of the years just before Jesus, written not by its winners but by its exiles.

The **Community Rule** is the sect's constitution: how a novice was examined and admitted over two years of probation, how property was held in common, how the council sat, how sins were disciplined and meals were eaten in purity. The **War Scroll** lays out, in liturgical detail, the final forty-year war between the Sons of Light and the Sons of Darkness — the Kittim, as they called the Romans — with battle formations, trumpet signals, and inscriptions for the banners, down to the names of angels. The **Thanksgiving Hymns** — the Hodayot — are psalms of the community and perhaps of the Teacher himself, and they are startlingly evangelical in their anthropology, if in nothing else: man is dust and a worm, a creature of clay with nothing in himself, and all righteousness is God's gift. "I know that no man is righteous apart from You," one hymn confesses. The **Habakkuk Commentary** shows their method of reading Scripture: a *pesher*, a verse-by-verse decoding in which every prophecy of the ancient prophet is read as a coded account of the community's own times and enemies. The **Damascus Document** and the great **Temple Scroll** — the latter a sweeping re-presentation of the Law, written as God's own first-person speech — fill out a library of fierce devotion, meticulous purity, and burning expectation.

What does all this show us? It shows the spiritual weather of the world into which the Son of God was born. Here were men who pored over Isaiah and Habakkuk line by line, convinced the prophecies were coming due in their own generation — and they were right about the timing and wrong about nearly everything else. They expected messiahs — in some of their writings, two of them, a messiah of Aaron and a messiah of Israel — and a final war won by their own purified ranks. What they got, a generation after their settlement fell, was a Messiah from Nazareth who won His war by dying in it, and who gathered His community not by probation and purity rankings but by grace, around a table open to tax collectors and sinners. The scrolls let us feel the longing that filled the air Jesus breathed — the hunger for God to act, the certainty that the Scriptures held the key, the ache of a people who knew the promises by heart and could not see how they would be kept. John the Baptist, preaching in that same desert, in that same generation, dressed in that same prophetic roughness, did not appear in a vacuum. He appeared in a world the scrolls describe. And the difference between Qumran and the gospel is the difference between a community that withdrew from a defiled world to keep itself clean, and a Savior who plunged into the defilement to make the unclean clean. The scrolls show us the question. Jesus is the answer.

## 6. The Scrolls Today: Slow Scholars, Open Doors

The scrolls' modern history carries its own lessons, some of them humbling. Four of the first seven scrolls were bought in 1954 — through an advertisement in the Wall Street Journal, of all places — by Yigael Yadin, acting quietly for the young state of Israel, for a quarter of a million dollars raised by a philanthropist. In 1965 the Shrine of the Book opened at the Israel Museum in Jerusalem to house them: a white dome shaped like the lids of the jars in which the scrolls were found, set against a black basalt wall — light and darkness, the community's own great theme, built into the architecture. The Great Isaiah Scroll is displayed there, its columns wrapped around a great drum, though what visitors usually see today is a faithful facsimile, the original kept mostly in darkness — parchment that survived two thousand years in a cave can still be killed by a few decades of light.

Publication was the scandal. The official editorial team appointed in the 1950s was tiny — eight scholars at its largest — and for decades the great mass of Cave 4 fragments sat unpublished, accessible only to the team and its students, while the scholarly world fumed. It was, in fairness, an almost impossible task: tens of thousands of brittle fragments, no titles, no authors, no table of contents, and careers staked on getting it right. But forty years is forty years, and the delay fed every conspiracy theory the public could invent — that the scrolls contained secrets fatal to the faith, suppressed by frightened churches. The truth, when it finally came out, was almost comically ordinary: the unpublished material was more of what had already been published — more Bible, more rules, more hymns — and the delay was human slowness, not suppression. The logjam broke in 1991, when the Huntington Library in California announced it would open its complete set of scroll photographs to all scholars, and a facsimile edition of the photographs appeared the same year. Under new leadership the editorial team was expanded to around a hundred scholars, and the official publication series was completed in the years that followed. Then the doors opened all the way: the Israel Antiquities Authority, working with Google, launched the Leon Levy Dead Sea Scrolls Digital Library in 2012, and today anyone with a phone — anyone reading this page — can look at high-resolution images of the actual scrolls, column by column, for free. The most jealously guarded texts of the twentieth century became the most accessible ancient manuscripts on earth. There is a parable in that for anybody who has ever been tempted to hoard the things of God: the Word was never meant to sit in a locked room, and in the end, it never does.

## 7. The Word in the Cave

Step back and look at the whole of it. God preserved His word through the Masoretes' counting houses and the translators' martyrdoms, as the companion history of the Bible tells — and He also preserved it, through the same centuries, in clay jars in a desert cave, by the hands of a sect that got the Messiah wrong. He did not need their theology to be right to make their copying faithful. The men of Qumran thought they were storing up their library against a Roman siege. They were, in fact, storing up evidence for a church they would never see, against doubts that had not yet been invented — the doubt, fashionable in our own day, that the Bible was rewritten across the centuries, that Isaiah was edited into a different book, that the text in your hands is a rumor of a rumor. Against that doubt stands a leather scroll, twenty-four feet long, written before the birth of Christ, reading like your Bible because it is your Bible. God keeps His word in caves as surely as in cathedrals. He kept it in a cave for you.

So if you are the reader this ministry meets every week — the one who wants to believe the Book but has been told too often that it cannot be trusted, the one whose life has taught you that everything solid-looking crumbles — hear the testimony of the jars. Empires crumbled over that cave. The sect crumbled. The very temple the sect despised crumbled, and the armies of Rome and the armies of Islam and the armies of Britain passed over the ground, and the scrolls outlasted all of them, because the Word in them was never finally in the keeping of men. It was in the keeping of the God who breathed it, who has never lost a syllable He intended to keep, and who has a long record — caves, fires, scaffolds, locked rooms — of getting His word into the hands of the people He means to save by it. Your hands are not an exception. The same God who brought Isaiah out of a cave after two thousand years can bring a word out of that Book into your life tonight, exactly on time. Open it and see.

**A prayer.** Lord God, Keeper of Your own Word, we thank You for the jars in the cave — for shepherds who threw stones and scribes who counted letters, for a text that came through a thousand years of darkness reading the same as the day it went in. You have not left us to guess about the Book. You have buried Your evidence in the earth and dug it up in our lifetime. So give us the faith the evidence deserves: to trust the Scriptures wholly, to read them daily, and to build our lives on the word that does not wither. And for any soul still doubting whether You can be trusted at all — meet him in these pages, as You have met so many, with the wounds that heal us. In Jesus' name, amen.

Christ Proclaimed. Jesus is Lord.
