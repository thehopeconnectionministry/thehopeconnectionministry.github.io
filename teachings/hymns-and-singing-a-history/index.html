Hymns and Singing — A History

How God's People Have Sung Through the Centuries

  • Teachings
  • Church History
  • Hymns

The Hope Connection

1. The Church Has Always Sung

Before the church had buildings, budgets, or printing presses, it had a song. Singing is not decoration added to the Christian faith once everything else was in place. It is in the foundation. The apostle Paul commanded it as a mark of the Spirit-filled life:

"...be filled with the Spirit, addressing one another in psalms and hymns and spiritual songs, singing and making melody to the Lord with your heart." — Ephesians 5:18–19

And to the church at Colossae he gave the same charge, tying the song directly to the Word:

"Let the word of Christ dwell in you richly, teaching and admonishing one another in all wisdom, singing psalms and hymns and spiritual songs, with thankfulness in your hearts to God." — Colossians 3:16

Notice what singing does in those verses. It teaches. It admonishes. It is one of the ways the word of Christ takes up residence in a people and stays there. A congregation can forget a sermon by Thursday; it hums the hymn in the kitchen for sixty years. That is why the history of hymns is not a side story to the history of the church. In every age, what the church believed, it sang — and what it sang, it remembered, carried into prison and exile and war, and handed down to its children. This is the story of that song: where it came from, how it was taken from the people and given back to them, and the men and women God used to write the words the church still cannot stop singing.

2. Singing in Scripture: From the Red Sea to a Prison Cell

The first song in the Bible rises from the far shore of the Red Sea. Israel had come through the water on dry ground, and Pharaoh's army had not. And Moses — the same Moses who once protested that he was slow of speech — led the people in song:

"I will sing to the LORD, for he has triumphed gloriously; the horse and his rider he has thrown into the sea. The LORD is my strength and my song, and he has become my salvation." — Exodus 15:1–2

His sister Miriam took up a tambourine, and all the women went out after her with tambourines and dancing, answering the song. That pattern — deliverance, then singing — runs through the whole Bible like a heartbeat. Deborah sang after the battle. Hannah sang over her answered prayer. And David, the shepherd who became king, was the sweet psalmist of Israel.

David organized the singing of Israel like a king building an institution: he appointed Levites — Asaph and his brothers, the sons of Korah, thousands of musicians by the chronicler's count — to sing before the ark of the covenant day and night, and the Psalms preserve their work, psalm after psalm marked for the choir director, for the ascent to Jerusalem, for the festival and the fast. The Psalter became Israel's hymnbook: one hundred and fifty songs covering every country the human heart can travel — praise and lament, confession and thanksgiving, songs for going up to Jerusalem and songs for the night when God feels far away. The church did not invent its songbook. It inherited one, written by the Holy Spirit through a thousand years of God's people learning to sing in every circumstance, including the ones where singing made no earthly sense.

And the Psalter never left the lips of the Lord Jesus. He argued from it, prayed from it, and — this is the wonder — died with it. From the cross He cried the opening line of Psalm 22, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me," taking the whole psalm of suffering and vindication onto His own shoulders; and His last words were the words of Psalm 31: "into your hand I commit my spirit." The hymnbook of Israel was the prayer book of the dying Savior. When the church sings the Psalms, it sings the very songs its Lord sang in His agony — there is no deeper reason to keep them in the congregation's mouth forever.

The Psalms were still on Jesus' lips at the end. On the night He was betrayed, after instituting the Lord's Supper, Matthew records a small sentence that is easy to read past:

"And when they had sung a hymn, they went out to the Mount of Olives." — Matthew 26:30

The hymn would have been part of the Hallel, the psalms of praise sung at Passover — Psalms 113 through 118, ending with "Give thanks to the LORD, for he is good, for his steadfast love endures forever." Consider what that means: the Lord Jesus went out to Gethsemane, to betrayal, to the cross, singing. The Son of God sang on the worst night of His life, and the psalm He sang ended with steadfast love. If He could sing on the way to the cross, His people can sing on the way to anything.

The early church took the hint. In a Philippian jail, around midnight, two prisoners sat with their feet fastened in the stocks, their backs raw from a beating, in the inner prison where no light came:

"About midnight Paul and Silas were praying and singing hymns to God, and the prisoners were listening to them." — Acts 16:25

Singing hymns. At midnight, in chains, in the dark — and the other prisoners listening, because men do not sing in that place unless something is holding them that the place cannot touch. Then the earthquake came, the doors flew open, and the jailer and his household believed before the night was out. The church's song has been shaking prisons ever since. That is the biblical pattern this whole history follows: the song begins at the Red Sea, sounds in the upper room, echoes in the jail at Philippi — and it has never stopped since.

3. Chant and Choir: The Centuries When the People Listened

The early church sang as a congregation — the whole assembly lifting the Psalms and the first Christian hymns together, often answering a leader line by line. Some of the church's oldest songs are still with us: the Gloria, the Te Deum, the evening hymn "O Gladsome Light," sung in Greek churches when the lamps were lit, which may be the oldest Christian hymn still in regular use outside the Scriptures themselves.

As the centuries passed, though, the singing of the church slowly changed hands. One great exception shines out of the fourth century: Ambrose, bishop of Milan, who taught his people to sing hymns in their own defense. When the imperial court tried to seize his church for Arian worship in the year 386, Ambrose held the building with his congregation inside it, and to keep their courage up through the siege he taught them to sing hymns antiphonally, answering voice to voice, in the manner of the East. His young hearer Augustine — not yet converted — remembered those songs for the rest of his life, and wrote in his Confessions of how he wept at the singing in Milan, the voices flowing into his ears and the truth distilled into his heart. Ambrose wrote hymns of his own, strong and simple Latin verse, and is remembered as the father of hymnody in the Western church. For a moment, in Milan, the whole congregation sang like an army. It is a picture of what congregational singing was always meant to be.

But moments like Milan became the exception rather than the rule. In the monasteries, monks gathered seven and eight times a day to chant the whole Psalter through, week after week, in the plainsong that later took the name of Pope Gregory the Great — Gregorian chant. It was beautiful, disciplined, and soaked in Scripture, and the monks preserved the song of the church through centuries when much else was lost. The church owes them a real debt for it.

But something was lost too, and it must be named plainly. The chant was in Latin. The singers were increasingly professionals — monks, then trained choirs — while the ordinary believer in the pew became a listener. Elaborate music for many voices rose like incense from the choir loft, and the ploughboy and his wife, who could not read the Latin and had never been taught the chant, stood silent in their own church. The song that Paul had commanded the whole congregation to sing to one another had become a performance the congregation attended. The Psalms belonged to everyone again in principle. In practice, for the better part of a thousand years, the people of God mostly heard their faith sung by other people.

That is the bad news of this section, and it sets up the good news of the next: God was about to put the song back in the mouths of His people, and He would do it through a monk who could not stop singing himself.

4. The Reformation Gives the Song Back to the People

Martin Luther loved music the way he loved the gospel — entirely, and without embarrassment. "Next to the Word of God," he wrote, "the noble art of music is the greatest treasure in the world." He played the lute, sang in a strong tenor, gathered his family and students around the table to sing in parts, and set about giving the German church songs of its own. Luther did not merely permit congregational singing. He wrote the hymns himself — dozens of them — taking the great truths of the recovered gospel and setting them in sturdy German verse that a farmer could sing and remember.

The greatest of them came around the year 1529: "A Mighty Fortress Is Our God," built on Psalm 46 — "God is our refuge and strength, a very present help in trouble." It became the battle hymn of the Reformation, sung by princes and peasants, sung at diets and at gravesides, sung by Gustavus Adolphus's army going into battle and by exiles going into the wilderness. Heinrich Heine, no friend of the church, called it the Marseillaise of the Reformation. Luther's hymn taught a whole movement to be unafraid: the prince of darkness grim, we tremble not for him — his rage we can endure, for lo, his doom is sure; one little word shall fell him.

A century and a half after Luther, the Lutheran chorale tradition bore its greatest fruit in Johann Sebastian Bach, the cantor of Leipzig, who signed his works Soli Deo Gloria — to God alone be the glory — and built the hymns of the congregation into cantatas and passions of unmatched depth. Bach did not replace the people's song; he crowned it. In his Saint Matthew Passion, the congregation's own chorales rise out of the drama like the voice of the church itself answering the gospel, and the tune the English-speaking world knows as "O Sacred Head, Now Wounded" runs through that Passion like a thread of gold. When Bach played the organ or led the choir, he insisted he was doing nothing more than any faithful church musician: putting the congregation's confession into its most beautiful earthly form. The Reformation's gift — the people's song — had become, by God's kindness, one of the supreme achievements of human art, and it still belonged to the ploughboy.

In Geneva, John Calvin took a different road to the same destination. Calvin believed the congregation should sing only the words God Himself had given — the Psalms — but sing them they must, all of them, in their own language, with their own voices. The Genevan Psalter, completed in 1562, set all one hundred and fifty psalms in French verse for congregational singing, with tunes of lasting beauty — including the one English-speaking churches still sing as the Doxology, "Praise God, from Whom All Blessings Flow." Calvin called singing a gift that could lift the heart to God and warned against songs that were light or frivolous; the song of the church, he insisted, should have weight and majesty because of Whom it addressed. From Geneva the metrical psalms spread to France, the Netherlands, and Scotland, where the Scottish Psalter of 1650 gave generations of Covenanters — including those who sang on the scaffold and at the stake — their only hymnbook, and found it enough.

Between Wittenberg and Geneva, the pattern of Protestant singing was set: the Bible in the people's language, and the song in the people's mouths. What Rome's choirs had sung for the people, the Reformation's congregations now sang for themselves. It was not a small change. A faith that is sung by ploughboys and mothers and children is a faith that cannot easily be taken away, because it lives where no confiscation can reach — in the memory and in the heart.

The English-speaking world received the pattern in its own tongue. Sternhold and Hopkins put the Psalms into English meter in 1562, and their psalter was bound into the back of countless Bibles and Prayer Books for two centuries. The Scottish Psalter of 1650 did the same work north of the border, in renderings still sung in Scottish churches today. And when the Puritans crossed the Atlantic, the first book they printed in the New World was a hymnbook: the Bay Psalm Book of 1640, the first book printed in British North America — a plain, faithful, deliberately unadorned translation of the Psalms for congregational singing, because the colonists held that they should sing God's words as nearly as they could render them, and sing them all together. A people founding a civilization chose, as its first act of the printing press, to publish its songbook. That tells you what the song meant to them.

5. The Great Hymnwriters: Watts, Wesley, Newton, and the Song That Spread

If the Reformation gave the people back their song, the eighteenth century gave them a flood of new ones.

Isaac Watts, a frail, brilliant English Dissenter, grew up in churches that sang only metrical psalms — often slowly, line by line, in renderings so clumsy that the young Watts complained about them at the dinner table. His father challenged him: give us something better, then. He did — beginning that same week, and continuing until he had written some seven hundred and fifty hymns. Watts did something revolutionary and entirely biblical: he wrote hymns that spoke in the first person about Christ and the cross in New Testament light. "When I Survey the Wondrous Cross," "Joy to the World" — his rendering of Psalm 72 dressed for the coming of Christ — "O God, Our Help in Ages Past," "Alas! and Did My Savior Bleed": these taught English-speaking believers to sing not only the Psalms but their own faith, their own conversion, their own hope of heaven. He is rightly called the father of English hymnody.

Charles Wesley wrote more hymns than anyone could count — over six thousand, some say nine thousand — and his brother John published them and carried them across Britain and America on horseback. The Wesleys put their hymns into the hands of miners, factory workers, and field hands, the people the established church had largely forgotten, and taught them to sing theology until it became the furniture of their minds. "And Can It Be That I Should Gain" is Charles Wesley's own conversion set to music, and its most famous verse is pure gospel astonishment: "Amazing love! how can it be that Thou, my God, shouldst die for me?" "Hark! the Herald Angels Sing" gave the church Christmas in five verses of dense, joyful doctrine. Wherever Methodism went, the singing went first.

Then there is John Newton, whose story the church tells because it must. Newton went to sea as a boy, fell into the roughest company afloat, and became the captain of slave ships — buying men, women, and children on the African coast and carrying them across the Atlantic in floating prisons. A storm at sea in 1748, when he cried out to God in a sinking ship, began his slow turning; he left the trade, studied, and was ordained, becoming the beloved pastor of Olney. There, with his friend the poet William Cowper, he wrote hymns for their weekly prayer meetings, published in 1779 as the Olney Hymns. Among them was a hymn Newton first called "Faith's Review and Expectation," written to go with a New Year's sermon on 1 Chronicles 17:16–17 — David's astonished prayer, "Who am I, O LORD God?" The world knows it by its first two words: "Amazing Grace." A slave trader redeemed — told honestly, that is what he was, and he never let himself or anyone else forget it; he called himself "a wretch like me" in the hymn because he believed it, and late in life he said his memory was nearly gone but he remembered two things: that he was a great sinner, and that Christ was a great Savior. His hymn has since been sung at more bedsides, gravesides, prison services, and revival meetings than perhaps any song in the language. It is the sound of the gospel reaching the man who deserved it least — which is why everybody who sings it feels it was written for them.

Newton's friend Cowper deserves his own paragraph, because he shows what the hymns were for in the dark places of an ordinary life. William Cowper was one of the finest poets of his century and one of its most afflicted men, dogged all his life by depression and the settled terror that God had cast him off. Newton stood by him like a brother for decades, and together they wrote the Olney hymns partly as medicine for Cowper's own soul. Out of that afflicted mind came "There Is a Fountain Filled with Blood" and "God Moves in a Mysterious Way" — the hymn of a man who could not feel the providence he believed, singing "behind a frowning providence He hides a smiling face" because the doctrine was true even when the feelings were gone. The church has been borrowing Cowper's hymns in its dark nights ever since, which is exactly what they were written for.

Augustus Toplady belongs in this company too — a fierce defender of the doctrines of grace who, in 1763, published in a magazine a hymn he had first drafted after sheltering from a storm in a cleft of rock at Burrington Combe: "Rock of Ages, Cleft for Me." It has been sung at the funerals of prime ministers and in the smallest country chapels, and its plea — nothing in my hand I bring, simply to Thy cross I cling — is the whole gospel in a breath. Add Philip Doddridge's "O Happy Day, That Fixed My Choice," written to be sung at baptisms and conversions in a Northamptonshire congregation, and you have the shape of the century: pastor after pastor writing songs so that ordinary believers could carry the faith home in their mouths.

The nineteenth century added its own voices. Fanny Crosby, blinded in infancy by a botched treatment, refused bitterness and became the most prolific hymnwriter in American history — more than eight thousand hymns and gospel songs, including "Blessed Assurance," "To God Be the Glory," and "Pass Me Not, O Gentle Savior," many written under pen names so that hymnals would not look like monuments to one woman. When she was eight years old she wrote, in a child's verse, that she would not murmur or repine at her blindness, being resolved to be content — and she kept that resolution for ninety-four years. Ira Sankey, the singer who traveled with the evangelist Dwight L. Moody, carried the gospel song — simpler than the old hymns, built for revivals, made to be remembered after one hearing — across America and Britain, singing to crowds of thousands without amplification, his voice and a small reed organ filling halls and fields. The Sankey songbooks sold in the tens of millions. Between the stately hymns of Watts and Wesley and the gospel songs of Crosby and Sankey, the English-speaking church acquired the songbook it still sings from today.

6. Singing in the Dark: Revival, Prison, and Persecution

The true test of a church's song is not the festival but the prison. And the record of two thousand years is that the church sings where it suffers.

It sang in the Roman arena, where the martyrs' psalms were the last thing the crowds heard from them. It sang on the scaffolds of Scotland, where Margaret Wilson, tied to a stake in the Solway tide in 1685, sang from the twenty-fifth Psalm and read aloud from Romans 8 as the water rose around her. It sang in the holds of slave ships and then, in one of history's great turnings, in the fields of American slavery, where a people in chains took the songs of Exodus and the Psalms and made the spirituals — "Go Down, Moses," "Steal Away," "Nobody Knows the Trouble I've Seen" — singing of a deliverance their masters' Bibles had taught them to expect. The church of the enslaved sang its way toward freedom with its captors' own Book, and proved it had understood the Book better.

It sang on the American frontier, where the camp meetings of the Second Great Awakening rang with choruses made to be learned by people who owned no book — and where, in the singing schools of the South, teachers carried shape-note tunebooks from settlement to settlement so that farm families could sing in harmony from the printed page. The Sacred Harp tradition that grew out of those singing schools still gathers singers in hollow squares across the South to sing the old tunes at full voice, a sound as raw and sturdy as the faith that made it. It sang on the Atlantic, in a storm, in January of 1736, where a little company of Moravian believers on a ship bound for Georgia sang hymns calmly through a gale that had seasoned sailors preparing to die. A young Anglican missionary on that ship, John Wesley, watched them — watched the women and children sing without a tremor while the waves broke over the deck — and asked himself whether he, a clergyman, was ready to die as these simple people plainly were. He was not, and he knew it, and the honesty of that moment began the road that led, two years later, to his own heart being strangely warmed. The Moravians of Herrnhut, Count Zinzendorf's community of exiles, were perhaps the most singing church of the century: they held a prayer watch that ran unbroken for a hundred years, invented the lovefeast and the singstunde — whole services made of song — and sent singing missionaries to the slaves of the Caribbean and the Inuit of Greenland. Wesley borrowed their hymnbook habits and their small-group fire, and Methodism's song was lit, in part, from Moravian coals. And it sang in Wales through the ministry of William Williams of Pantycelyn, the sweet singer of the Welsh revival, whose "Guide Me, O Thou Great Jehovah" — pilgrim through this barren land — has been sung by Welsh congregations, soldiers, and rugby crowds alike until it became the sound of a nation's faith.

It sang in the revivals, where the song was often the sermon that stayed. In Wales in 1904 and 1905, the revival meetings were carried on waves of congregational singing that broke out spontaneously, and miners went down into the pits with hymns on their lips — it is said the pit ponies, used to curses, had to be retrained for men who no longer swore. It sang in the communist prisons of the twentieth century, where Richard Wurmbrand and his fellow prisoners composed hymns in solitary cells and tapped them through the walls in code, and where believers forbidden to sing aloud sang silently together, mouths moving, in perfect unison. It sang in China's house churches and in the underground gatherings of North Korea, in whispers, because a whispered hymn is still a hymn. In the Nazi camps, in the Soviet gulags, in the prisons of Iran in our own day, the testimony keeps arriving in the same form: they took everything, and they could not take the song.

It sang in Bedford jail, where John Bunyan spent twelve years for preaching without a license, separated from his wife and his blind daughter Mary, able to see his family through the prison door and unable to provide for them. He was told he could go free if he would promise to stop preaching; he answered that if he were released today, he would preach tomorrow. In that jail he wrote The Pilgrim's Progress, and in its second part he put a song in the mouth of Mr. Valiant-for-Truth that became one of the church's marching hymns: "He who would valiant be 'gainst all disaster, let him in constancy follow the Master." The tinker of Bedford, who had been a foul-mouthed soldier before grace found him, sang the pilgrim's defiance from a cell — and three and a half centuries of pilgrims have been singing it on the road ever since, most of them never knowing it was written by a prisoner.

And sometimes the song rises out of a single ruined life, and the whole church borrows it forever. In 1873, the Chicago lawyer Horatio Spafford sent his wife and four daughters ahead of him across the Atlantic; their ship, the Ville du Havre, collided with another vessel in the night and sank in twelve minutes. His wife survived. His four daughters did not. Crossing the ocean afterward to meet his grieving wife, Spafford was shown the place where the ship had gone down, and there — over the water that held his children — he wrote the words the church has sung at a million gravesides since: "When peace like a river attendeth my way, when sorrows like sea billows roll; whatever my lot, Thou hast taught me to say, it is well, it is well with my soul." He could write that because he believed the sea was not the end of his daughters' story, and that his own sins — all of them, as the hymn says, not in part but the whole — were nailed to the cross and he bore them no more. That is not optimism. That is doctrine, sung by a drowning-hearted father, and it has carried more believers through the worst nights of their lives than any sermon of the century.

Why not? Because the song was never stored in a building or a book. It was stored where Paul told the Colossians to store it — in hearts where the word of Christ dwells richly. Burn the hymnal and the hymns remain. Close the church and the singing moves to the cellar. Kill the singer and the song is taken up by the next believer in line, the way it has been taken up since Miriam answered Moses on the far shore of the sea. The song of the church is one of the surest proofs of the promise that the gates of hell will not prevail against it: empires have spent two thousand years trying to silence it, and it is louder now than it has ever been.

7. The Song of Heaven

Every hymn the church has ever sung is a rehearsal. The performance is still ahead.

In the book of Revelation, John hears the song that the redeemed will sing forever — a new song, sung to the Lamb who was slain:

"And they sang a new song, saying, 'Worthy are you to take the scroll and to open its seals, for you were slain, and by your blood you ransomed people for God from every tribe and language and people and nation.'" — Revelation 5:9

From every tribe and language and people and nation. The slave woman Blandina's confession, the ploughboy's psalm, Newton's astonished grace, the whispered hymn in a North Korean home, the spiritual sung in the cotton field, the doxology rising from a little country church on a Sunday morning — all of it is one song, warming up. In heaven it will not be whispered. No one will sing it in Latin so the people cannot follow, and no one will sing it in chains. The congregation that has been gathering since the Red Sea will finally be all in one place, and the song Paul commanded — psalms and hymns and spiritual songs, the word of Christ dwelling richly — will be sung by the whole company at once, to the Lamb, forever.

So sing now. Sing in the church and sing in the kitchen. Teach the hymns to your children the way you teach them their own names, because one day the hymns may be the strongest theology they can remember, in a hospital room or a hard year when everything else is hard to hold. Sing when it is easy, so that you will know how to sing when it is not. The church that sings is the church that remembers — and the church that remembers Whose it is cannot finally be silenced, in this age or in the age to come.

Lord Jesus, Singer of the Hallel on the way to the cross, put Your song in our mouths and Your word in our hearts. Thank You for the psalms of David, for the hymns of Luther and Watts and Wesley, for grace amazing enough to make a slave trader sing, and for the song that rises tonight from prisons we cannot see. Teach us to sing in the dark as Your people have always sung, until the night is over and we join the new song before Your throne — worthy are You, O Lamb of God. Amen.
