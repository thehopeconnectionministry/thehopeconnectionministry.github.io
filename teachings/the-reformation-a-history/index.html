The Reformation — A History

How God Recovered the Gospel for His People

  • Teachings
  • Church History

The Hope Connection — Lewis Fuller

1. What the Reformation Was, and Why It Still Matters

Five hundred years ago, the church in Europe had lost the gospel. Not lost it the way a man loses his keys — the Bible was still there, chained in the cathedrals, read in a language the people could not understand. The gospel was buried under centuries of human tradition, buried so deep that ordinary men and women no longer knew the simplest good news in the world: that a sinner is made right with God not by his own works, not by his money, not by his suffering, but by faith alone in Jesus Christ alone.

The Reformation was God's recovery of that gospel. It was not the invention of a new religion. It was the rediscovery of the old one — the faith of the apostles, preached by Paul, written down in Scripture, and hidden for generations under the inventions of men. When the Reformers stood up, they were not trying to start something new. They were trying to get back to something ancient: the Word of God, plainly read and plainly believed.

At the heart of it all stands one doctrine, the doctrine Paul preached and the medieval church had forgotten — justification by faith alone. Listen to the apostle:

"For we hold that one is justified by faith apart from works of the law." — Romans 3:28 (ESV)

That one sentence changed the world. It set terrified consciences free. It emptied the confessional booths and filled the churches with singing. It sent preachers into marketplaces and Bibles into farmhouses. And it cost many of its witnesses their lives.

Why does it still matter? Because the human heart has not changed. Men still try to climb to heaven on ladders of their own making. Churches still drift toward tradition, power, and money, and away from the plain Word of God. The Reformation matters because the gospel it recovered is the same gospel a sinner needs today — the same gospel a man in a jail cell or a hospital bed or a long night of regret needs to hear: you cannot save yourself, but Christ can, and He receives everyone who comes to Him by faith.

This is the story of how God did it — through monks and scholars, printers and kings, and a great company of martyrs whose names the world has mostly forgotten but heaven has not.

2. The World Before: A Church That Had Buried Its Own Bible

To understand the Reformation, you have to understand what the church had become by the year 1500.

The Roman Catholic Church of the late Middle Ages was the single great power of Western Europe. The pope in Rome claimed authority not only over the church but, in many matters, over kings and nations. Beneath that authority stood a vast system of teaching that had grown up over centuries — and much of it could not be found in Scripture at all.

The Mass as a sacrifice. The Lord's Supper was no longer taught as a memorial meal received by faith. It was taught as a sacrifice offered again by the priest on the altar, every day, in every cathedral and village church. The people did not eat and drink — they watched from a distance, separated by a screen, while the priest performed the service in Latin. The cup was withheld from them altogether.

Penance and purgatory. Forgiveness was not preached as God's free gift to the sinner who believes. It was administered through a system: confess to a priest, receive a penance, do works of satisfaction. And when a person died, almost no one expected to go straight to heaven. They expected purgatory — a place of cleansing fire where the remaining debt of sin would be burned away, perhaps for thousands of years, while their families paid for Masses to shorten the sentence.

Indulgences. Out of this system grew its ugliest fruit. An indulgence was a certificate, sold by the church, that claimed to reduce the time a soul spent in purgatory — your own time, or the time of a loved one already dead. By 1517, indulgence sellers were traveling through Germany with something close to a sales pitch. The most famous of them, a Dominican friar named Johann Tetzel, hawked indulgences to raise money for the building of Saint Peter's Basilica in Rome and to pay off the debts of a German archbishop. A jingle followed Tetzel from town to town: "As soon as the coin in the coffer rings, the soul from purgatory springs." The church was, quite literally, selling heaven.

Scripture kept from the people. The Bible existed only in Latin, in the translation called the Vulgate, and copies were rare and costly, hand-written by monks. Lay people were discouraged — and in some places forbidden — from reading it for themselves. It was not uncommon for a priest to know little more Scripture than the passages in his service book. The people heard the Word of God the way a man hears a conversation through a wall: muffled, in another tongue, and only in pieces chosen by someone else.

And yet, underneath all of this, there was hunger. Ordinary people longed for God. They bought indulgences because they feared judgment and loved their dead. They crowded to see relics and went on pilgrimages because they wanted to be near holiness. The spiritual hunger of the common people was real and deep. What they lacked was not sincerity. What they lacked was the gospel.

God was about to feed them.

3. The Forerunners: Morning Stars Before the Dawn

The Reformation did not begin in 1517. Long before Luther, God raised up men who saw the truth and paid for seeing it.

John Wycliffe (about 1328–1384) was an Oxford scholar in England, and he came to a conclusion that shook the church of his day: the Bible, not the pope, is the final authority for the Christian faith, and every believer has the right to read it. Wycliffe taught that the church had departed from Scripture, denounced the wealth and corruption of the clergy, and rejected teachings that had no foundation in the Word. Most importantly, he set scholars to work translating the Bible into English — the first complete English Bible, translated from the Latin Vulgate, so that the plowman and the weaver could read what the priest read. His followers, called Lollards, carried the message across England, preaching in the language of the people. Wycliffe died in his bed, but the church's hatred of him did not die: decades later his bones were dug up, burned, and thrown into a river. He is remembered as the Morning Star of the Reformation — the first light before sunrise.

Jan Hus (about 1369–1415) was a preacher in Prague, in Bohemia, who read Wycliffe's writings and preached the same truths to packed crowds in the Bethlehem Chapel — preaching in the Czech language, calling the church back to Scripture, and declaring that Christ, not the pope, is the true Head of the church. Summoned to the Council of Constance under a promise of safe conduct, Hus was arrested anyway, condemned as a heretic, and burned at the stake on July 6, 1415. He went to the fire singing. His followers, the Hussites, held off crusading armies sent to crush them and kept the flame of reform alive in Bohemia for a hundred years, until Luther's day. Tradition remembers Hus saying that though they might cook this goose — "Hus" means "goose" in Czech — in a hundred years a swan would sing that they could not silence. A century later, almost to the year, Luther stood before the emperor.

The printing press. Between Wycliffe and Luther stands a providence of God that changed everything: movable type. Around 1440, in Mainz, Germany, Johannes Gutenberg developed a printing press with movable metal type, and by about 1455 he had produced his famous printed Bible. Before Gutenberg, a Bible cost the price of a house and took years to copy by hand. After Gutenberg, books could be made by the hundreds, then the thousands, and their price fell within reach of ordinary men. When the Reformation came, it came with paper and ink. Luther's writings were the first bestsellers in history — within a few years his pamphlets were in every town in Germany, carried along trade roads faster than any army could march to stop them. The medieval church had kept the Word chained. God answered with a machine that multiplied it beyond counting.

4. Martin Luther: The Monk Who Found the Gospel

Martin Luther was born in 1483 in Eisleben, Germany, the son of a copper miner who wanted him to become a lawyer. In 1505, caught in a violent thunderstorm and terrified of death, young Luther cried out a vow to Saint Anne and promised to become a monk. He kept the vow. He entered an Augustinian monastery in Erfurt and threw himself into the system with everything he had — fasting, praying through the night, confessing his sins for hours at a time, wearing out his confessors. And the more he worked at it, the more miserable he became. He knew God's holiness. He knew his own sin. And the system of penance offered him no rest, because he could never know whether he had done enough. "I was a good monk," he said later, "and I kept the rule of my order so strictly that I may say that if ever a monk got to heaven by his monkery it was I."

In time Luther was sent to Wittenberg, where he became a professor and a doctor of theology, lecturing on the Bible itself — the Psalms, then Romans, then Galatians. And it was there, in the pages of Romans, that the buried gospel broke open in his soul. He wrestled for years with one phrase in Romans 1:17, "the righteousness of God," because he understood it only as the righteousness by which God punishes sinners. Then, by God's mercy, the rest of the verse opened to him:

"For in it the righteousness of God is revealed from faith for faith, as it is written, 'The righteous shall live by faith.'" — Romans 1:17 (ESV)

Luther saw it at last: the righteousness of God is not only the righteousness God demands — it is the righteousness God gives, a gift received by faith alone, the righteousness of Christ counted to the sinner who believes. "Here I felt that I was altogether born again," he wrote, "and had entered paradise itself through open gates." The monk who could never do enough discovered that Christ had done it all.

October 31, 1517. It was into this new joy that Johann Tetzel came selling indulgences near Wittenberg, and Luther's own people were buying them — believing they could purchase what Christ gives freely. On October 31, 1517, Luther posted his Ninety-five Theses on the door of the Castle Church in Wittenberg — ninety-five statements, in Latin, inviting debate about indulgences. He intended a scholars' argument. He got a revolution. The Theses were translated into German, printed, and spread across the country in weeks. Luther had struck the match; the printing press did the rest.

The road to Worms. In the years that followed, the argument sharpened. At the Leipzig Debate in 1519, Luther's opponent Johann Eck pressed him until Luther declared openly what he had come to believe: councils could err, the pope could err, and Scripture alone stands above them all. In 1520 Luther published the great treatises that laid out the Reformation program — including The Babylonian Captivity of the Church and The Freedom of a Christian — and when the pope issued a bull condemning his teaching and threatening him with excommunication, Luther burned the bull publicly in Wittenberg. In January 1521 he was excommunicated. That spring he was summoned to appear before the emperor himself.

The Diet of Worms, 1521. On April 18, 1521, in the city of Worms, Martin Luther — a thirty-seven-year-old monk — stood before the Holy Roman Emperor Charles V, the princes of Germany, and the assembled power of church and empire. His books were piled on a table. He was asked two questions: Are these yours? Will you recant? Luther asked for a night to think and pray. The next evening he gave his answer: he would recant only if convinced by Scripture or plain reason, because popes and councils had contradicted themselves and erred. Then came the words that have echoed for five hundred years: "Here I stand; I can do no other. God help me. Amen."

He left Worms a condemned man, an outlaw whom anyone could kill without penalty. But on the road home he was "kidnapped" by soldiers of his own prince, Frederick the Wise of Saxony, and hidden away in the Wartburg Castle, where he lived disguised as a knight, "Junker Jörg." There, in about eleven weeks, Luther translated the New Testament into German — the language of the people, rendered with such force and beauty that it shaped the German language itself. It was published in September 1522, and it sold out at once. The whole Bible in German followed in 1534.

Luther married a former nun, Katharina von Bora, in 1525, and their home in Wittenberg became a model of the Christian household and a haven for students and refugees. He preached, he taught, he wrote hymns — "A Mighty Fortress Is Our God" is his — and he stood, to the end of his life in 1546, on the ground he had taken at Worms: Scripture alone is the church's final authority, and a sinner stands righteous before God by faith alone in Christ alone.

5. Zwingli and the Swiss Reformation

The Reformation was never one man's work. At almost the same hour, God raised up reformers in other lands who reached the same truths from the same Book.

In Zurich, Switzerland, Ulrich Zwingli (1484–1531) became the people's priest at the Great Minster in 1519 and began doing something revolutionary: preaching straight through the Gospel of Matthew, verse by verse, in the language of the people, instead of following the church's appointed fragments. Zwingli had come to the truth of the gospel by his own road through Scripture, and under his preaching Zurich was transformed. In 1523 the city council held public disputations and sided with reform: images were removed from the churches, the Mass was replaced by the Lord's Supper as a memorial meal, and the Word of God took the center of the city's life.

Zwingli and Luther agreed on the great matters — Scripture alone, faith alone — though they famously disagreed, at the Marburg Colloquy in 1529, on the manner of Christ's presence in the Lord's Supper, and parted in sorrow over it. Zwingli died in 1531, killed in battle at Kappel while serving as a chaplain to Zurich's troops in a war against the Catholic cantons. His work was carried on in Zurich by Heinrich Bullinger, whose influence reached across Europe and deep into England. From Switzerland the Reformation spread city by city, each one won not by armies but by preaching — a minister opening a Bible and reading it aloud to people who had never heard it whole.

6. John Calvin and Geneva: The School of Christ

If Luther was the Reformation's thunder, John Calvin was its clear, steady light. Born in France in 1509, Calvin was a brilliant young scholar of law and classical letters who came to evangelical faith in the early 1530s and had to flee France for his life when persecution broke out against Protestants there.

In 1536, at the age of twenty-six, Calvin published in Basel the first edition of the Institutes of the Christian Religion — a short book then, which grew across his lifetime into the most complete and orderly statement of Reformed teaching ever written, and one of the great books of the Christian church. That same year, passing through Geneva, Switzerland, Calvin was famously confronted by the fiery reformer William Farel, who thundered God's judgment on him if he refused to stay and help establish the church there. Calvin stayed. Geneva expelled them both in 1538; Calvin spent happy, fruitful years pastoring in Strasbourg; but Geneva called him back in 1541, and he returned to spend the rest of his life there as its pastor and teacher.

What Calvin built in Geneva was not a throne but a school. He preached daily, lectured through book after book of the Bible, catechized children, trained pastors, and wrote commentaries on nearly the whole Scripture that are still read today. Geneva became a refuge city: persecuted believers fled there from France, England, Scotland, Italy, and the Netherlands — and Geneva sent them back as trained preachers, many of them to face prison and death at home. In 1559 Calvin founded the Geneva Academy to train ministers, and John Knox, who lived there during Bloody Mary's reign, called Geneva "the most perfect school of Christ that ever was in the earth since the days of the apostles."

Calvin died in 1564, worn out at fifty-four, and was buried in an unmarked grave at his own request. His legacy is not a tomb but a teaching: that the whole of life is lived before the face of God, that Scripture governs the church, and that salvation is, from first to last, the work of God's sovereign grace — so that all the glory goes to God alone.

7. The English Reformation: The Bible in the Language of the Plowman

The English Reformation is a story of two streams running side by side: the politics of a king, and the blood of the martyrs. Honesty requires telling both.

William Tyndale (about 1494–1536) belongs to the martyrs, and he is the hero of the English Bible. A scholar at Oxford and Cambridge, Tyndale was gripped by one conviction: the people of England must have the Scriptures in their own tongue, translated not from the Latin but from the original Greek and Hebrew. "If God spare my life," he told a learned clergyman who defended keeping the people ignorant, "ere many years I will cause a boy that driveth the plough shall know more of the Scripture than thou dost." Denied permission to translate in England, Tyndale fled to the continent and never came home again. His English New Testament, printed at Worms in 1526, was smuggled into England in bales of cloth and barrels of goods, bought eagerly, and burned publicly by the authorities — which only made men hungrier for it. Betrayed by a false friend, Tyndale was imprisoned near Brussels and on October 6, 1536, was strangled and burned at the stake. His last words were a prayer: "Lord, open the King of England's eyes." Within three years, that prayer was answered in a way no one expected: the King of England authorized a great English Bible — built largely on Tyndale's own translation — to be placed in every church in the land. The great majority of the King James New Testament, when it came generations later, is Tyndale's wording, carried across the years.

Henry VIII and the break with Rome. The political stream ran through the king. Henry VIII wanted a male heir and wanted his marriage to Catherine of Aragon annulled; the pope, for reasons both theological and political, refused. Henry's answer, through Parliament, was to break England away from Rome altogether: in 1534 the Act of Supremacy declared the king himself the supreme head of the Church of England. It must be said plainly: Henry's motives were not Luther's. He was no Protestant theologian — he had written a book against Luther and been named "Defender of the Faith" by the pope for it — and to the end of his life he held many Roman doctrines and persecuted men for denying them. Yet in God's providence the break with Rome opened a door that better men walked through. Under Henry the monasteries were dissolved, and under his son the gospel itself entered in.

Edward VI (reigned 1547–1553) was a boy king, devout and serious beyond his years, and during his short reign England's reformation became truly Protestant. Archbishop Thomas Cranmer gave the church the Book of Common Prayer (1549, revised 1552) — worship in English, beautiful and biblical, with the Scriptures read aloud to the people week by week. Reformers from the continent were welcomed to teach. It was a springtime, and it lasted six years.

Bloody Mary (reigned 1553–1558). Edward died at fifteen, and his half-sister Mary — a devoted Roman Catholic determined to restore England to Rome — took the throne. What followed earned her the name history still gives her. Across five years, roughly three hundred Protestants were burned at the stake: bishops and scholars, weavers and apprentices, old men and young mothers, blind men and lame men, whose only crime was the faith they would not deny. On October 16, 1555, at Oxford, the aged bishop Hugh Latimer and bishop Nicholas Ridley were burned together. As the fire was lit, Latimer called out the words every English schoolchild once knew: "Be of good comfort, Master Ridley, and play the man; we shall this day light such a candle, by God's grace, in England, as I trust shall never be put out." The following March, Archbishop Cranmer himself was brought to the stake. Cranmer had weakened under pressure and signed recantations; but at the end he renounced them publicly, and when he came to the fire he thrust into the flame first the right hand that had signed them, crying that it had offended, holding it steady in the fire until it was consumed. Mary died in 1558. Her fires had accomplished the opposite of her intention: the sight of ordinary believers dying with Scripture on their lips turned England Protestant in its heart.

Elizabeth and the settlement (1558–1603). Mary's half-sister Elizabeth restored a Protestant England by the settlement of 1559 — the Church of England under the crown, the Prayer Book restored, the Thirty-nine Articles giving the church its Reformed confession. Elizabeth's long reign was no paradise for the godly — the Puritans, who wanted a fuller reformation, chafed under her halfway house — but England was settled as a Protestant nation, and under her successor, James I, came the crowning gift. At the Hampton Court Conference in 1604 the king authorized a new translation, and in 1611 the work of forty-seven scholars was published: the King James Bible. Drawn heavily on Tyndale, written in language of unmatched power and beauty, it became the Bible of the English-speaking world for three and a half centuries — the plowman's book, at last, in every home that wanted it.

8. John Knox and Scotland

Scotland's Reformation ran red with blood and came suddenly, like a storm off the North Sea. Its forerunner, George Wishart, was burned in 1546. His bodyguard and disciple was a tough, fearless preacher named John Knox (about 1514–1572), who was captured by the French, spent nineteen months as a galley slave, and eventually found his way to Geneva, where he sat under Calvin and called that city the most perfect school of Christ on earth.

Knox returned to Scotland in 1559 to find the nation ripe: a corrupt church, a French regent ruling for the young Mary, Queen of Scots, and nobles and common people alike hungry for the Word. Knox preached, and Scotland caught fire. In 1560 the Scottish Parliament abolished the authority of the pope, forbade the Mass, and adopted a Reformed confession of faith that Knox and his colleagues had written in four days. The Church of Scotland was rebuilt on the Presbyterian pattern: congregations governed by elders, ministers trained and examined, schools envisioned for every parish so that every child could read the Bible. Knox's History of the Reformation in Scotland and his thundering preaching made him the voice of a nation's conscience; he feared, as his enemies admitted, the face of no man. The saying attributed to him could stand over the whole Reformation: "Give me Scotland, or I die." Scotland got the gospel — and sent preachers, teachers, and eventually missionaries across the world.

9. The Counter-Reformation and the Cost

Rome did not watch all this in silence. The Counter-Reformation was the Roman Church's answer: the Council of Trent (1545–1563) restated Roman doctrine, formally condemned the gospel of justification by faith alone, and placed Luther's writings and vernacular Bibles on the Index of Forbidden Books; the Jesuits, founded in 1540, became the pope's shock troops of education and missions; and the Inquisition, with the machinery of Catholic monarchs, hunted evangelicals across Spain, Italy, and the Netherlands. In France, thousands of Huguenots — French Protestants — were slaughtered in the Saint Bartholomew's Day Massacre of 1572 and the wars that followed. In the Netherlands, Spanish rule executed thousands for the faith. The Reformation map of Europe was drawn, in no small part, in blood.

That is why John Foxe's Acts and Monuments — Foxe's Book of Martyrs, first published in English in 1563 — mattered so much. Foxe, an English exile under Mary, gathered the records of the martyrs: their examinations, their letters from prison, their last words at the stake. His great book was chained in cathedrals beside the Bible and read in homes for centuries. It taught ordinary believers who they were: heirs of a company of witnesses who had counted the Word of God worth more than life. The cost of the Reformation was not an abstraction. It was Latimer's candle and Cranmer's hand, Tyndale's prayer and Hus's song in the fire — men and women who believed that "the righteous shall live by faith" and sealed that belief with their blood.

10. The Five Solas: The Settled Summary of the Reformation

Later generations summed up the whole Reformation in five Latin phrases — five "alones." They are not slogans. They are the load-bearing walls of the gospel, and each one stands on Scripture.

Scripture alone — sola Scriptura. The Bible, and the Bible alone, is the church's final authority. Tradition, councils, and popes may err; the Word of God does not. "All Scripture is breathed out by God and profitable for teaching, for reproof, for correction, and for training in righteousness, that the man of God may be complete, equipped for every good work." — 2 Timothy 3:16–17 (ESV)

Faith alone — sola fide. A sinner is justified — declared righteous before God — through faith alone, not by faith plus works, sacraments, or penance. "For we hold that one is justified by faith apart from works of the law." — Romans 3:28 (ESV)

Grace alone — sola gratia. Salvation is God's free gift from beginning to end; nothing in the sinner earns it or starts it. "For by grace you have been saved through faith. And this is not your own doing; it is the gift of God, not a result of works, so that no one may boast." — Ephesians 2:8–9 (ESV)

Christ alone — solus Christus. There is one Mediator, and He needs no helpers — no priestly caste, no saints, no mother Mary standing between the sinner and God. "For there is one God, and there is one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus." — 1 Timothy 2:5 (ESV)

Glory to God alone — soli Deo gloria. Because salvation is God's work from first to last, the glory is His from first to last — in the church, and in every corner of ordinary life. "For from him and through him and to him are all things. To him be glory forever. Amen." — Romans 11:36 (ESV)

11. What the Reformation Gave the Ordinary Believer

Strip away the dates and the debates, and ask a simpler question: what did all of this mean for the farmer, the weaver, the mother at her hearth — for people like us?

It gave them the Bible in their own language. This was the Reformation's beating heart. The Word of God, read at the kitchen table, heard in the mother tongue on Sunday, sung in the Psalms. Faith comes by hearing, and for the first time in a thousand years, the people could hear.

It gave them assurance. The medieval system could never tell a sinner he had done enough — that was its engine and its cruelty. The recovered gospel announced that Christ's work is finished, that the believer's standing rests on His righteousness and not on their performance, and that a trembling conscience can rest. "The righteous shall live by faith" is not a threat. It is a resting place.

It gave them the priesthood of all believers. No longer did a man need a priest to carry his prayers to God or a saint to plead his case. In Christ, every believer has direct access to the Father, and every believer's calling — plowing, parenting, building, serving — is holy work done before God. "But you are a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people for his own possession, that you may proclaim the excellencies of him who called you out of darkness into his marvelous light." — 1 Peter 2:9 (ESV)

It gave them song. In the old order, choirs sang in Latin and the people listened. The Reformers put hymnbooks in the people's hands — Luther's hymns in Germany, the Psalms in meter in Geneva and Scotland and England — and congregations found their voice. Much of the hymn singing we love today flows straight out of the Reformation.

It put preaching at the center. The pulpit replaced the altar as the heart of the church's worship, because the Reformers believed God saves and sanctifies His people through His Word proclaimed. A sermon was not decoration. It was the main event — the living voice of the living God to His people.

12. Closing: Always Reforming, Always Back to the Word

The Reformers had a saying that has come down to us: ecclesia reformata, semper reformanda — the church reformed, always being reformed. Not reformed according to the fashion of the age, and not according to the opinions of men, but always being reformed according to the Word of God. That work is never finished in this life, because the church's drift is never finished: we drift toward comfort, toward tradition, toward trusting in ourselves, and the Word must call us back, generation after generation, to Christ.

That is why this history is not really about the sixteenth century. It is about the God who keeps His church. Empires tried to burn this gospel out of the world, and the fires only lit Latimer's candle. Men chained the Bible, and God answered with the printing press. The church buried its own treasure, and a trembling monk with an open Romans dug it up again. The same Word that shook Europe is open on our tables today, and it has lost none of its power.

So here is the Reformation's word to a believer today, and it is the word this ministry exists to proclaim: come back to the Book. Come back to Christ. Whatever you are carrying — guilt you cannot undo, a past you cannot outrun, a night that will not lift — the gospel the Reformers died to recover is the gospel that meets you where you are: the righteous shall live by faith, and the One who receives sinners has never turned away a single soul that came to Him. Bad news first, if we are honest: we cannot save ourselves; we never could. Good news forever: Christ has done what we could not, and He is enough.

A closing prayer

Father, we thank You for Your Word — for preserving it through the centuries, for raising up men and women who loved it more than life, and for putting it into our hands in our own tongue. Thank You for the gospel recovered in those days and preached in ours: that we are justified by grace alone, through faith alone, in Christ alone. Keep Your church always reforming according to Your Word. Keep us from trusting in anything we have done, and teach us to rest in what Christ has done. And give us, if need be, the courage of those who went before us — to stand, and to be able to do no other. In the name of Jesus Christ, our only Mediator, Amen.

Christ Proclaimed. Jesus is Lord.
