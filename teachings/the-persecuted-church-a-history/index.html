The Persecuted Church — A History

The Suffering Church, from Stephen to Today

  • Teachings
  • Church History
  • Persecution

The Hope Connection

1. He Told Us It Would Come

Persecution is not an accident in the Christian life. It is not a sign that something has gone wrong. The Lord Jesus promised it plainly, on the night before He died, sitting with His disciples in the upper room:

"Remember the word that I said to you: 'A servant is not greater than his master.' If they persecuted me, they will also persecute you." — John 15:20

Read that again slowly. The Master was hunted, slandered, arrested, beaten, and nailed to a cross. The servant should not expect a softer road than the Master walked. From the very beginning, the church has known the prison cell and the execution ground — and from the very beginning, the church has grown there.

That is why the book of Hebrews gives the comfortable church a command it must never outgrow:

"Remember those who are in prison, as though in prison with them, and those who are mistreated, since you also are in the body." — Hebrews 13:3

Remember. Not glance at and forget. Remember — as though you were chained beside them, because in the body of Christ, you are. One body means one pain. When a believer in North Korea is worked to death in a camp, a member of our own body is suffering. When a church in Nigeria is burned on a Sunday morning, the wound is in our own flesh. This history is written so that we will remember: where the suffering church came from, what it has endured, and what it has to teach those of us who worship in safety.

The story begins, as every Christian story begins, with bad news answered by good news. The bad news is real, and this history will not soften it: men and women and children have been tortured and killed for the name of Jesus in every century since the first one. The good news is better, and it runs underneath every page: not one of them was ever abandoned, the blood of the martyrs has always been the seed of the church, and every single one of them is now safe. The Lamb who was slain has kept every promise He ever made to His suffering people.

2. The First Martyr: Stephen

The church was only weeks or months old when the first Christian died for his faith. His name was Stephen, one of the seven men chosen to serve tables and care for widows in the Jerusalem church — a man, Luke tells us, "full of faith and of the Holy Spirit," who did great wonders and signs among the people.

Stephen's crime was preaching. Dragged before the council, he told Israel its own story back to it — Abraham, Joseph, Moses, the prophets — and showed how the nation had resisted the Holy Spirit in every generation, and how that resistance had now reached its climax: they had betrayed and murdered the Righteous One, Jesus, whom God had raised from the dead. The council ground their teeth at him. And then Stephen, full of the Holy Spirit, gazed into heaven and saw what no one else in the room could see: the glory of God, and Jesus standing at the right hand of God. Standing — not sitting. As if the risen Lord had risen from His throne to welcome His servant home, and to stand as his advocate before the court that was about to kill him.

They rushed him, dragged him out of the city, and stoned him. And Stephen died the way his Master had taught him to die — praying for his murderers:

"And as they were stoning Stephen, he called out, 'Lord Jesus, receive my spirit.' And falling to his knees he cried out with a loud voice, 'Lord, do not hold this sin against them.' And when he had said this, he fell asleep." — Acts 7:59–60

There is a detail in that account that Luke does not let us miss. The witnesses laid their garments at the feet of a young man named Saul. Saul of Tarsus approved of Stephen's execution. Saul went on from that day to ravage the church, entering house after house, dragging off men and women to prison. Saul was the persecutor — zealous, violent, breathing threats and murder against the disciples of the Lord.

And what did God do with Saul? He knocked him flat on the Damascus road, blinded him with the glory of the risen Christ, and made him the apostle to the Gentiles. The man who held the coats at the first martyrdom became the man who wrote half the New Testament. He became, in time, a prisoner himself — beaten, stoned, shipwrecked, chained in Rome, and finally beheaded under Nero. The persecutor became the persecuted. The coats he guarded that day were, in God's strange mercy, the beginning of his own story.

That is the pattern of this whole history, set down in its first chapter: the church's enemies are never beyond the reach of grace, the martyrs' prayers are never wasted, and God has a habit of answering the blood of His saints by raising up preachers from the very ranks of those who shed it. Stephen prayed, "Do not hold this sin against them." Heaven heard him. Saul was the answer.

3. Rome: Fire, Lions, and a Church That Would Not Die

For three hundred years, the church lived under the shadow of the Roman Empire — sometimes tolerated, sometimes hunted, never safe for long. Historians often speak of ten great waves of persecution under ten emperors. The reality was messier than any list: persecution rose and fell by place and by ruler, a governor here, a mob there, an emperor's whim everywhere. But the waves were real, and some of them nearly drowned the church. Or so it seemed.

The first wave came from Nero. In the year 64, a great fire consumed much of Rome, and rumor blamed the emperor himself. Nero needed a scapegoat, and he found one in a small, already-suspected sect. The Roman historian Tacitus, no friend of Christians, tells us what Nero did: he fastened the guilt on the Christians and inflicted exquisite punishments on them. Some were covered with the skins of wild animals and torn apart by dogs. Some were crucified. Some were set on fire and burned alive, used as living torches to light Nero's gardens at night. The apostles Peter and Paul both died in Rome in those years — Peter, tradition holds, crucified upside down because he counted himself unworthy to die in the same manner as his Lord; Paul, a Roman citizen, beheaded by the sword.

Wave followed wave. Under Domitian, under Trajan, under Marcus Aurelius, under Septimius Severus, the machinery of empire turned against the church again and again. Under Decius, in the year 250, persecution became systematic for the first time: every citizen was required to sacrifice to the gods of Rome and obtain a certificate proving it. Refuse, and face prison, torture, and death. Under Valerian, bishops were hunted by name. The charges against Christians were always shifting — atheism, because they would not worship the gods; treason, because they would not burn incense to the emperor's image; cannibalism, because slanderers twisted the words of the Lord's Supper; hatred of the human race, because they would not join the empire's festivals and games. The one charge that was always true was the one that mattered: they would worship no lord but Jesus.

In the midst of these centuries stand two witnesses whose stories the church has never stopped telling.

The first is Ignatius, the aged bishop of Antioch, arrested within living memory of the apostles and sent under armed guard across Asia Minor to be thrown to the wild beasts in Rome, around the year 107. The churches along his road sent delegations to meet him, and he wrote them letters on the journey — letters we still possess. The Roman church he begged not to rescue him: "I am the wheat of God, and let me be ground by the teeth of the wild beasts, that I may be found the pure bread of Christ." He did not love death. He loved Christ more, and he was determined that no one would rob him of the honor of confessing his Lord at the end of a long life of service. He got his wish.

Polycarp was the aged bishop of Smyrna, a man who had known the apostle John. When the persecution broke out in his city, the mob demanded his death, and the old man was brought into the stadium before the proconsul. He was offered the standard bargain: curse Christ, swear by the emperor, and live. His answer has rung down nineteen centuries: "Eighty-six years I have served Him, and He has done me no wrong. How then can I blaspheme my King who saved me?" They threatened him with wild beasts, then with fire. He answered that the fire they threatened burned for an hour, but the fire of the judgment to come was eternal. They bound him to the stake, and he prayed aloud, thanking God for counting him worthy of that hour. He died in the flames, an old man who would not trade his King for his life.

A generation before Perpetua, in the year 177, persecution fell on the churches of Lyons and Vienne in Gaul, and the church there sent an account of it to the churches in Asia — a letter that still survives. At the center of that account stands Blandina, a slave woman, the lowest person in the empire's social order, tortured from morning until evening until her torturers were exhausted and amazed that she still lived. Through every round she repeated one confession: "I am a Christian, and there is no wickedness among us." Hung on a post in the arena as food for the beasts, she prayed so earnestly for the other prisoners that the eyewitnesses said she put on Christ before their eyes. She was the last of the martyrs of Lyons to die, and the letter calls her a noble athlete who put on the great and invincible athlete, Christ. Rome measured people by rank. The arena kept revealing that heaven measures them by faith — and that slaves and noblewomen, in Christ, die like royalty.

Perpetua was a young mother in Carthage, twenty-two years old, a noblewoman with a baby son still nursing, arrested in the year 203 for refusing to sacrifice. Her father begged her, holding her child in his arms, to think of the baby and save herself. She pointed to a water pot on the floor and asked him whether it could be called by any other name than what it was. Then she said: neither can I call myself anything other than what I am — a Christian. She kept a diary in prison, one of the earliest writings by a Christian woman that we still possess, and in it she recorded her dreams, her grief over her child, her care for the fellow prisoners around her, and her strange, settled peace. She and her servant girl Felicitas — who gave birth in prison days before the end — were thrown to wild beasts in the arena and then killed by the sword. Perpetua, the eyewitness account says, had to guide the gladiator's shaking hand to her own throat. She would not let even her death be taken out of her own steady hands.

The last and worst wave was the Great Persecution under Diocletian, beginning in the year 303. Churches were demolished. Scriptures were burned — the empire now understood that the book was the church's lifeblood. Clergy were arrested wholesale; then all Christians were ordered to sacrifice on pain of death. Prisons filled. In some provinces the executioners' arms grew tired. And within ten years, the empire reversed itself entirely: Constantine legalized the faith, and the persecuted church became, almost overnight, the favored church of Rome.

Here is the wonder that every honest telling of these centuries must record: persecution did not shrink the church. It purified her and multiplied her. The second-century writer Tertullian said it to the rulers of Rome directly: "The blood of Christians is seed." Every martyr's death was a sermon the empire could not silence. Neighbors watched ordinary people — old bishops, young mothers, slaves and noblewomen side by side — die singing, forgiving, unafraid, and they asked the only question that mattered: what do these people know that we do not? Rome threw everything it had at the church for three hundred years. Rome is gone. The church is still here.

4. When the Persecutors Wore Christian Names

The saddest chapters of this history are the ones where the sword against true believers was swung by men who also claimed the name of Christ. As the medieval church grew corrupt — as the gospel of grace was buried under penance, purgatory, indulgences, and the machinery of Rome — men and women who recovered the simple faith of the Scriptures found themselves hunted not by pagan emperors but by the church itself.

In the mountains of northern Italy and southern France, the Waldensians kept a simple, Bible-shaped faith for centuries before the Reformation: Scripture in the language of the people, preaching by ordinary men, a rejection of purgatory and the Mass. For this they were crusaded against, driven into the high Alpine valleys, and massacred in waves across the centuries — most infamously in 1655, when the Duke of Savoy's soldiers slaughtered whole villages in the Piedmont valleys, a horror that moved John Milton to write his famous sonnet and moved Protestant Europe to intervene. The Waldensians endured. Their descendants worship in Italy to this day.

Jan Hus, the Bohemian preacher who called the church back to Scripture and to Christ as its only Head, was promised safe conduct to the Council of Constance — and was burned at the stake anyway, on July 6, 1415, singing as the flames rose. Before him, John Wycliffe's bones were dug up and burned for his crime of giving England the Bible in English. The ashes were thrown into the River Swift. The men who did it thought they were ending something. They were spreading it.

Wycliffe's work was finished by William Tyndale, who gave his life to put the New Testament into the hands of the English ploughboy — his stated ambition was that the boy who drove the plough would know more of the Scripture than the learned men who opposed him. Hunted out of England, betrayed by a false friend in the Low Countries, Tyndale was strangled and burned at Vilvoorde in 1536, praying with his last breath: "Lord, open the King of England's eyes." Within a few years, by that same king's order, an English Bible — largely Tyndale's own work — stood chained open in every parish church in England. The prayer was answered in the persecutor's own lifetime, by the persecutor's own hand. That is how God writes this history.

Then came the Reformation, and with it a harvest of martyrs across Europe. In England, under Mary Tudor, nearly three hundred Protestants were burned in five years — bishops like Hugh Latimer and Nicholas Ridley, who died at Oxford in 1555 with Latimer's famous words ringing out: "Be of good comfort, Master Ridley, and play the man; we shall this day light such a candle, by God's grace, in England, as I trust shall never be put out." Archbishop Thomas Cranmer followed them in 1556, thrusting into the fire first the hand that had signed his recantation. In 1563, John Foxe published his great Book of Martyrs — the Acts and Monuments — gathering the testimonies, examinations, letters, and dying words of the martyrs of England and Europe. Chained in cathedrals beside the Bible, it became, after the Scriptures, the most-read book in the English-speaking world. Generations of ordinary believers learned their faith's cost from Foxe. The church has been learning it from him ever since.

In France, the Huguenots — the French Protestants — built churches, held synods, and endured wave after wave of violence through the French Wars of Religion. It broke over them in full fury on the night of August 24, 1572: St. Bartholomew's Day. With the royal court in Paris for a wedding, the killing of Huguenot leaders began in the night and spread into a general massacre. Thousands died in Paris in a matter of days; the slaughter spread to the provinces, and the total dead across France numbered in the tens of thousands. Admiral Coligny, the Huguenots' great leader, was murdered in his lodgings and thrown from his window. The Pope ordered a medal struck to celebrate the event. The Huguenots were later granted a measure of toleration, then stripped of it again in 1685, when hundreds of thousands fled France rather than surrender their faith — carrying their skills, their industry, and their Bibles to England, Holland, Prussia, South Africa, and America. France impoverished itself to persecute them. The nations that received them were enriched by them.

In Scotland, the Covenanters of the seventeenth century refused to surrender the crown rights of Christ over His church to the Stuart kings. For their covenants they were hunted across the moors in the years remembered as the Killing Time: shot at their own doors, hanged at market crosses, drowned tied to stakes in the rising tide — as Margaret MacLachlan and Margaret Wilson were at Wigtown in 1685, the younger Margaret singing psalms as the water rose. George Wishart and the young Patrick Hamilton had already burned in the generation before. Scotland's church was bought with blood, and Scotland remembered the price.

The lesson of this whole section is one the church must never stop learning: persecution does not only come from outside the walls. Whenever the institution becomes more precious to men than the truth, whenever power dresses itself in religious robes, the true church suffers — and, as always, endures.

5. The Modern Martyrs: Camps, Gulags, and Underground Churches

The twentieth century — the century that congratulated itself on outgrowing such things — became the bloodiest century in the church's long history. The persecutors changed their banners: no longer emperors demanding incense, but ideologies demanding the whole soul. The demand underneath was the same as Nero's. Bow, or suffer.

In Germany, Dietrich Bonhoeffer saw clearly, earlier than most, what the Nazi regime was and what it would demand of the church. A pastor and theologian, he helped lead the Confessing Church that refused to let the swastika stand over the altar, trained young pastors in an underground seminary at Finkenwalde, and wrote of the cost of discipleship — of cheap grace, which he named as the deadly enemy of the church. Drawn into the resistance against Hitler, he was arrested in 1943 and held in prison while he wrote letters of astonishing faith and cheer to his family and friends. In April 1945, weeks before the war in Europe ended, he was taken to Flossenbürg concentration camp and hanged. The camp doctor who witnessed it wrote that Bonhoeffer knelt and prayed before climbing the scaffold steps, "brave and composed," and that in his fifty years of medical work he had scarcely seen a man die so submissive to the will of God. He was thirty-nine years old.

In the Soviet Union, the persecution was longer and colder. Churches were closed by the tens of thousands, pastors shot or sent to the camps of Siberia, and the registered church kept on a short state leash — while underneath, an unregistered underground church baptized in frozen rivers, copied Scripture by hand, and met in forests and cellars. Richard Wurmbrand, a Romanian pastor, spent fourteen years in communist prisons — three of them in solitary confinement in an underground cell — tortured for his refusal to stop preaching. Released and ransomed to the West, he came to the free world with the scars on his body and one burning message: the church behind the Iron Curtain was alive, faithful, and forgotten by the comfortable church that should have remembered it. His book Tortured for Christ, published in 1967, woke millions to the suffering church, and the ministry he founded carried aid and Bibles to persecuted believers for decades after. His testimony before committees of the United States Senate, where he stripped to show the wounds on his torso, remains one of the most sobering scenes of the Cold War — a prisoner of Christendom's newest empire, standing in the halls of the free, asking to be remembered.

In China, Watchman Nee built a movement of simple, Scripture-shaped churches and taught a generation of Chinese believers to know Christ deeply. When the communists took power, he refused both flight and compromise. Arrested in 1952, he spent twenty years in prison and labor camps. He was offered release if he would recant. He would not. He died in a labor camp in 1972, and a note was found under his pillow, written in a shaking hand: "Christ is the Son of God who died for the redemption of sinners and resurrected after three days. This is the greatest truth in the universe. I die because of my belief in Christ." The authorities who imprisoned him expected the church in China to die with its leaders. Instead, under the worst persecution of the century, the Chinese church grew from under a million believers to tens of millions — perhaps the greatest church growth in Christian history, accomplished without buildings, without freedom, and without the West's help.

The century had opened with a warning of what was coming. In 1915, the Ottoman Empire turned on its Armenian subjects — the oldest Christian nation on earth, Christian since the year 301 — and in the deportations and massacres that followed, well over a million Armenians perished, along with great numbers of Assyrian and Greek Christians. Pastors were arrested and killed on the first night. Whole congregations were marched into the desert to die. Survivors carried the faith into exile across the world, and the Armenian church, which had already survived fourteen centuries of pressure, survived this too — wounded beyond words, and unbowed.

Armenia could be named, and Mexico under the Cristeros, and Spain, and a hundred other places. The pattern never changes: the ideology promises the church a quiet life at the price of a bowed knee, and the church — the true church, in every land — keeps discovering that it would rather have Christ in a prison than comfort without Him.

6. The Persecuted Church Today

This is not ancient history. The church is persecuted now, this year, in more places than at any time in living memory, and a sober telling of it matters — sober, factual, without exaggeration, because the truth is heavy enough on its own.

In North Korea, ranked year after year as the most dangerous nation on earth in which to be a Christian, faith in Christ is treated as treason against the ruling family. Believers discovered by the state face execution or the labor camps — whole families, including children, punished together for one member's faith, under a system that punishes three generations for a single "crime." The underground church there worships in whispers, hides single pages of Scripture, and teaches children the faith in secret, knowing exactly what discovery costs.

In Nigeria, more Christians have been killed for their faith in recent years than in any other nation on earth. In the north and the Middle Belt, believers face the terrorism of Boko Haram and its offshoots, and armed attacks on farming villages that have left thousands dead and whole congregations displaced. Churches have been bombed on Christmas Day. Schoolgirls have been kidnapped for refusing to convert. And still, on Sunday mornings, the churches of Nigeria fill.

In the Middle East — the very land where the church was born — ancient Christian communities have been shattered in a generation. In Iraq, the Christian population collapsed from well over a million before 2003 to a small remnant, driven out by war and by the Islamic State's campaign of marking Christian homes, forced conversion, and murder. In Syria, believers were caught between the regime, the rebels, and the jihadists. In Egypt, the Copts — whose church goes back to the apostle Mark — have endured church bombings on Palm Sunday and the filmed murder of twenty-one of their men on a Libyan beach in 2015, men who died with the name of Jesus on their lips. In Eritrea, believers have been held for years — some for decades — in metal shipping containers in the desert heat, imprisoned for no charge other than unauthorized worship, while in Sudan and now South Sudan, churches have been bombed from the air in wars that ground on for a generation. In Somalia, to be discovered as a Christian is to face death at the hands of al-Shabaab or of one's own family. Across the Muslim-majority world, men and women who come to Christ from Islam carry a particular cross: the convert may lose family, livelihood, freedom, and sometimes life itself, and must often worship in total secrecy, a congregation of one, sustained by a hidden Bible and the knowledge that they are part of a family far larger than the one that cast them out.

Add to these the believers imprisoned in Iran's house-church raids, the pastors jailed in Nicaragua, the churches demolished in China, the converts hunted in Afghanistan, and the mob violence against Christians in Pakistan, and the map of suffering is wide indeed.

It is commonly reported — by historians of the church and by those who count such things — that more Christians were martyred in the twentieth century than in all the previous centuries of the church combined. That is not a precise count; no one can produce the full ledger, and much of the killing happened in places where no records were kept and no reporters went. But the scale of it is not seriously in doubt. The age of martyrdom is not past. By many measures, we are living in it.

And here is what must be said alongside it, because the persecuted church insists on saying it about itself: it is not a church of victims begging for pity. Again and again, believers in prison camps and burned villages have sent the same message to the free church — do not pity us; pray for us, and be faithful where you are, because the same Lord who keeps us here will keep you there. The persecuted church is, in many places, the growing church, the praying church, the joyful church. It has something the comfortable church desperately needs to relearn.

7. What the Suffering Church Teaches the Comfortable Church

What, then, is our answer? Hebrews 13:3 gives the shape of it: remember them as though in prison with them. Remembrance is not a feeling. It is a set of practices.

First, prayer — real, regular, named prayer. The persecuted church asks for this before it asks for anything else, and it asks with a twist that should humble us: prisoners like Wurmbrand have testified that they knew, in the camps, when the church was praying. Pray for believers by country and by need; pray not first for their deliverance, but for their faithfulness — that is what they ask for themselves.

Second, remembrance in the congregation's own life. This is why observances like Red Week matter — a week in which the church deliberately turns its eyes to its imprisoned and mistreated members, marks the reality of their suffering, teaches the children their names and their stories, and refuses to let comfort become forgetfulness. A church that will not remember its prisoners has already begun to forget its Lord, who told us that visiting the prisoner was visiting Him.

Third, practical support. The body cares for its wounded members: aid for prisoners' families, Bibles and training for underground believers, legal advocacy for the accused, and refuge for those who must flee. The ministries that carry this work stand in a direct line from the Christians of Rome who ransomed captives and collected money for imprisoned confessors in the mines — the comfortable church has always been meant to spend its comfort on the suffering church.

Fourth, courage of our own. The cheapest response to the persecuted church is admiration from a safe distance. The honest response is to ask what faithfulness costs here, and to pay it: to speak the truth when it is unpopular, to refuse the small compromises that are offered to us at prices far lower than a prison cell. If men and women can confess Christ before firing squads, we can confess Him before neighbors, employers, and family. Their courage is not only an inspiration. It is a rebuke to our fear — and, by God's grace, a cure for it.

Underneath all of it stands the law and the gospel of this history. The law says it plainly: this world hates Christ, and it will hate His people; comfort is not the promise, and a church that expects the world's applause has forgotten its Master. The gospel says it better: Christ has already walked this road ahead of His people — arrested, condemned, executed — and He walked out of the grave at the end of it. No martyr has ever gone anywhere that Jesus has not already been, and come back from, in triumph.

8. The Martyrs Before the Throne

The last word in this history does not belong to the persecutors. It belongs to the book of Revelation, where John is shown the company that no executioner could thin:

"After this I looked, and behold, a great multitude that no one could number, from every nation, from all tribes and peoples and languages, standing before the throne and before the Lamb, clothed in white robes, with palm branches in their hands... 'These are the ones coming out of the great tribulation. They have washed their robes and made them white in the blood of the Lamb. Therefore they are before the throne of God, and serve him day and night in his temple; and he who sits on the throne will shelter them with his presence. They shall hunger no more, neither thirst anymore; the sun shall not strike them, nor any scorching heat. For the Lamb in the midst of the throne will be their shepherd, and he will guide them to springs of living water, and God will wipe away every tear from their eyes.'" — Revelation 7:9, 14–17

Every tear. The young mother in the arena, the old bishop in the flames, the pastor in the gulag, the believer in the camp whose name no one on earth ever recorded — God recorded it. They are not lost. They are not even, in the deepest sense, waiting in sorrow. They are home, sheltered, shepherded, and their tears are already being wiped away by the hand of God Himself.

So this is what we say to the suffering church, and what the suffering church has said to us across twenty centuries: hold fast. The night is not the end of the story. Remember those who are in prison, and let the remembering change you — until the day when there are no more prisons to remember, and the whole church, the comfortable and the persecuted made one company at last, stands before the throne with palm branches in its hands.

Lord Jesus, You who were persecuted unto death and rose in triumph, remember Your suffering church tonight — in the camps and the prisons, in the burned villages and the whispered rooms. Give them the faith of Stephen, the steadiness of Polycarp, the courage of Perpetua, and the joy that no torturer has ever been able to take. Teach us who worship in safety to remember them as though chained with them, to pray, to give, and to confess You without fear in our own place. And bring us all, at last, to stand before Your throne, where every tear is wiped away. Amen.

Christ Proclaimed. Jesus is Lord.
