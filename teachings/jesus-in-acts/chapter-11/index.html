Acts 11 — Repentance to Life: The Church Learns to Glorify Grace

Jesus in Acts — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Acts
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Jesus is the Lord whose grace will not be argued with. The church learns to stop arguing with grace — God grants repentance to life to the Gentiles, and at Antioch the risen Christ builds the church that will send the missionaries. Acts 11 is a chapter about God teaching His own people to celebrate what He has done, and then quietly building, in a mixed and lively city, the launching pad for the mission to the world.

Commentary

The criticism at Jerusalem (11:1–3). The apostles and brothers throughout Judea hear that the Gentiles have received the word of God. But when Peter comes up to Jerusalem, the circumcision party criticizes him, saying, "You went to uncircumcised men and ate with them." The bad news here is uncomfortable for church people: the believers closest to the truth can be the slowest to celebrate grace when it lands on the wrong kind of people. Their complaint is even about a table — who Peter ate with. Tables matter in Acts; fellowship is the gospel made visible, and that is exactly why it is fought over.

Peter's defense: telling the story (11:4–17). Peter does not pull rank. He "began and explained to them in order" — the rooftop, the sheet, the messengers, the house full of people, the sermon, and then the decisive fact: "As I began to speak, the Holy Spirit fell on them just as on us at the beginning." He remembers the Lord's word about being baptized with the Holy Spirit, and draws the conclusion that silences the argument:

"If then God gave the same gift to them as he gave to us when we believed in the Lord Jesus Christ, who was I that I could stand in God's way?" — Acts 11:17 (ESV)

Who was I that I could stand in God's way? That is the right question for every church argument about grace. Peter simply tells the story, step by step, and lets God defend what God did.

From criticism to worship (11:18). And the good news is what happens when the critics hear it: "When they heard these things they fell silent. And they glorified God, saying, 'Then to the Gentiles also God has granted repentance that leads to life.'" Criticism turned into worship. That is what an honest report of grace can do to a divided church — and it is worth noticing that the Jerusalem believers were not asked to approve the Gentile mission in advance. They were asked to glorify God for it afterward, and they did.

The church at Antioch (11:19–26). Now Luke picks up the scattered believers of chapter 8. Those scattered after Stephen preach in Phoenicia, Cyprus, and Antioch — at first to Jews only. But some of them, men of Cyprus and Cyrene, come to Antioch and speak to the Greeks also, preaching the Lord Jesus. "And the hand of the Lord was with them, and a great number who believed turned to the Lord." Anonymous believers — Luke does not even give their names — plant the most important missionary church in the New Testament. Jerusalem hears of it and sends Barnabas, the son of encouragement, who arrives, sees "the grace of God," and is glad, and exhorts them all to remain faithful to the Lord with steadfast purpose — "for he was a good man, full of the Holy Spirit and of faith." Barnabas's instinct for grace is the pattern: he sees it, rejoices in it, and strengthens it. Then he goes to Tarsus to look for Saul — the encourager fetching the former persecutor to help teach — and for a whole year they meet with the church and teach a great many people. "And in Antioch the disciples were first called Christians." The name was probably coined by outsiders. It stuck, because it was true: these people belonged to Christ, talked about Christ, and were known by Christ.

Prophecy and generosity (11:27–30). Prophets come down from Jerusalem to Antioch, and one of them, Agabus, foretells by the Spirit a great famine over all the world — which takes place in the days of Claudius. The disciples' response is immediate and practical: "everyone according to his ability" decides to send relief to the brothers living in Judea. The Gentile church's first recorded act as a church is generosity to the Jewish church that sent its teacher. Grace received becomes grace given, and the relief is sent by the hands of Barnabas and Saul — the two men chapter 13 will send to the nations.

Key verses

"Then to the Gentiles also God has granted repentance that leads to life." — Acts 11:18 (ESV)

"And the hand of the Lord was with them, and a great number who believed turned to the Lord." — Acts 11:21 (ESV)

"And in Antioch the disciples were first called Christians." — Acts 11:26 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Acts 11 is that God's own people can criticize grace when it lands on the wrong kind of people — and that impulse did not die in the first century. But the good news is what God does with it: He gives His church a story it cannot argue with, turns criticism into glorifying, and grants repentance that leads to life to the Gentiles. And in Antioch He builds the mixed, generous, sending church — proof that the people grace gathers are the people grace sends.

A note from the Reformers. Spurgeon pointed to Barnabas in this chapter as the model churchman: he saw the grace of God and was glad. Not suspicious, not controlling — glad. A heart that can rejoice at grace in unlikely places, Spurgeon said, is a heart that understands the gospel.

Reflection question: The Jerusalem believers moved from criticizing grace to glorifying God for it. Is there a work of God in someone else's life — someone unlikely, someone you did not choose — that you have been questioning instead of celebrating? What would celebrating it look like this week?
