Acts 14 — Through Many Tribulations: Iconium, Lystra, and Derbe

Jesus in Acts — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Acts
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Jesus is the living God whom the apostles preach instead of accepting worship for themselves — and the risen Lord who is worth a stoning to Paul. Acts 14 shows the gospel making disciples who are told the truth about the road ahead and strengthened by grace to walk it. The Jesus of this chapter does not promise His people an easy road. He promises them a kingdom at the end of a hard one, and His own presence all the way through it.

Commentary

Iconium: a great multitude, and a divided city (14:1–7). At Iconium Paul and Barnabas enter the synagogue together and speak in such a way that a great number of both Jews and Greeks believe. That phrase — spoke in such a way — is worth pausing on: the same gospel, preached with the Spirit's power, persuades multitudes. But the unbelieving Jews stir up the Gentiles and poison their minds against the brothers. So the apostles remain a long time, speaking boldly for the Lord, "who bore witness to the word of his grace, granting signs and wonders to be done by their hands." Note whose word it is: the word of His grace. The city divides — some side with the Jews, some with the apostles — and when a plot forms to mistreat and stone them, they learn of it and flee to Lystra and Derbe. The pattern of the whole journey repeats: the word preached, some believe, opposition hardens, and the missionaries keep going. Flight here is not cowardice; it is Jesus' own instruction, and there are other cities waiting.

Lystra: the lame man healed (14:8–10). At Lystra sits a man who cannot use his feet — crippled from birth, who never walked. He hears Paul speaking. Paul, looking intently at him and seeing that he has faith to be made well, says in a loud voice, "Stand upright on your feet." And he springs up and begins walking. It is Acts 3 again, in a pagan city this time: the name of Jesus lifts a man who has never taken a step, and Paul sees the faith before he speaks the word. God gives faith, and God honors it, in Lystra as surely as in Jerusalem.

Mistaken for gods (14:11–18). The crowds see what Paul has done and shout in their own language, "The gods have come down to us in the likeness of men!" They call Barnabas Zeus and Paul Hermes, because he is the chief speaker, and the priest of Zeus brings oxen and garlands to the gates and wants to offer sacrifice to them. Here is a temptation subtler than stoning: worship. And the apostles' response is immediate and violent in its humility — they tear their garments, rush out into the crowd, and cry out: "Men, why are you doing these things? We also are men, of like nature with you, and we bring you good news, that you should turn from these vain things to a living God, who made the heaven and the earth and the sea and all that is in them." Paul preaches to pagans from creation: God overlooked the times of ignorance, but He never left Himself without witness — He gave rains from heaven and fruitful seasons, satisfying their hearts with food and gladness. Even with these words, they scarcely restrain the people from offering sacrifice. Soli Deo gloria with torn clothes: the servants of Christ refuse the glory that belongs to God alone, and point a pagan city to the living God who made them and feeds them.

Stoned — and back inside (14:19–20). But Jews arrive from Antioch and Iconium — traveling a hundred miles to kill a sermon — persuade the crowds, and stone Paul and drag him out of the city, supposing him to be dead. Within days, the same city that tried to crown him tries to bury him. Crowds are weather; the word is climate. The disciples gather about him — the little church at Lystra, standing in a circle around their fallen apostle — and he rises up and enters the city. Whether Paul was dead or near death, Luke does not say; what he says is enough: the man they left for dead walks back into the city that stoned him, and the next day departs with Barnabas for Derbe. The risen Jesus is worth a stoning to Paul, because Paul has met Him, and men who have met the risen Christ do not measure their lives by safety.

Strengthening the churches on the way home (14:21–28). At Derbe they preach and make many disciples. Then — astonishingly — they retrace their steps: Lystra, Iconium, Pisidian Antioch, the very cities that expelled and stoned them, "strengthening the souls of the disciples, encouraging them to continue in the faith, and saying that through many tribulations we must enter the kingdom of God." The apostles never hide the cost from new believers; they tell the truth about the road ahead, because converts promised an easy road quit on the hard one, and converts told the truth endure. They appoint elders in every church, with prayer and fasting commend them to the Lord in whom they believed, sail from Attalia to Antioch — the sending church — and gather the church together and declare "all that God had done with them, and how he had opened a door of faith to the Gentiles." Not their hardships first. God's work. And they remain there a long time with the disciples. The first journey ends at home, with a report about God.

Key verses

"We also are men, of like nature with you, and we bring you good news, that you should turn from these vain things to a living God, who made the heaven and the earth and the sea and all that is in them." — Acts 14:15 (ESV)

"Strengthening the souls of the disciples, encouraging them to continue in the faith, and saying that through many tribulations we must enter the kingdom of God." — Acts 14:22 (ESV)

"And when they arrived and gathered the church together, they declared all that God had done with them, and how he had opened a door of faith to the Gentiles." — Acts 14:27 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Acts 14 is plain, and the apostles never hide it from new believers: following Jesus costs. Through many tribulations we must enter the kingdom of God — that is the honest word, spoken to brand-new disciples in the cities that did the stoning. But the good news is that the suffering is not the end of the story. The living God turns hearts from vain things to Himself, a door of faith stands open to the nations, and the apostle left for dead gets up and walks back in. Elders are appointed, disciples are commended to the Lord on whom they believed, and the kingdom is worth the road.

A note from the Reformers. Calvin admired the apostles' honesty in this chapter: new believers were told at the start that the road runs through tribulation. A gospel that hides the cross, he taught, makes fair-weather Christians; the truth about the cost, told in love, makes enduring ones.

Reflection question: Paul told new believers the truth about affliction instead of hiding it. How does knowing the road is hard — and that God walks it with you — change the way you face your current trouble? Name the trouble, and the next faithful step, this week.
