Acts 21 — The Lord's Will Be Done: Bound for Jerusalem

Jesus in Acts — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Acts
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Jesus is the Lord whose name Paul is ready to be bound and to die for — and whose road to Jerusalem Paul now deliberately retraces. Paul follows his Lord's own road: set face toward the city that will bind him, warned at every stop, and prayed over by a church that finally prays Jesus' own prayer — the Lord's will be done. In Acts 21 the name of Jesus is worth more to Paul than freedom, safety, and the tears of his dearest friends.

Commentary

Warnings at every port (21:1–9). The ship leaves Miletus and the weeping elders, and the voyage becomes a rhythm of farewells and warnings. At Tyre, the disciples there "through the Spirit" tell Paul not to go on to Jerusalem. When the days are over, the whole church — wives and children included — comes out of the city with them, and they kneel down on the beach and pray. At Ptolemais they greet the brothers. At Caesarea they enter the house of Philip the evangelist — Philip of the Ethiopian chariot, one of the seven — who now has four unmarried daughters who prophesy. The scattered servant of chapter 8 has a settled household of prophetic daughters; God wastes none of His servants' years.

Agabus and the belt (21:10–14). While they are staying for many days, a prophet named Agabus comes down from Judea. He takes Paul's belt, binds his own feet and hands with it, and says, "Thus says the Holy Spirit, 'This is how the Jews at Jerusalem will bind the man who owns this belt and deliver him into the hands of the Gentiles.'" The prophecy is vivid, visual, and unanimous with every other warning on the journey. When the believers hear it, they — and Luke includes himself: "we and the people there" — urge Paul not to go up to Jerusalem. Here is the chapter's subtle bad news, because it wears the face of love: everyone who cares about Paul begs him not to go, weeping, until his heart is nearly broken. Affection itself can become a temptation when it argues against the known will of God. Paul's answer echoes his Lord's road to the same city:

"What are you doing, weeping and breaking my heart? For I am ready not only to be imprisoned but even to die in Jerusalem for the name of the Lord Jesus." — Acts 21:13 (ESV)

And since he will not be persuaded, they fall silent and pray the prayer Gethsemane taught the church: "Let the will of the Lord be done." That sentence is not resignation. It is worship — friends surrendering a man they love to a God they trust.

Received gladly in Jerusalem (21:15–26). They go up to Jerusalem, and the brothers receive them gladly. The next day Paul goes in with the team to James, and all the elders are present. Paul relates one by one the things God has done among the Gentiles through his ministry, and they glorify God. Then James raises the local problem: thousands of Jewish believers, all zealous for the law, have been told that Paul teaches Jews among the Gentiles to forsake Moses. The report is distorted, but the tension is real. The proposed solution: Paul will join four men under a vow, pay their expenses, and go through purification in the temple, so all will know there is nothing in the reports — that Paul himself walks in observance of the law. As for the Gentile believers, the council's letter stands: grace settled in chapter 15 is not reopened. Paul agrees. The apostle of Gentile freedom submits to a Jewish rite he does not need, for the sake of peace with brothers he will not needlessly offend — liberty used for love, the same Paul who would not yield an inch on justification yielding gladly on ceremony.

The mob in the temple (21:27–36). When the seven days are almost completed, Jews from Asia — Paul's old mission field coming to Jerusalem to destroy him — see him in the temple, stir up the whole crowd, and seize him: "Men of Israel, help! This is the man who is teaching everyone everywhere against the people and the law and this place. Moreover, he even brought Greeks into the temple and has defiled this holy place." The charge is false — they had seen Trophimus the Ephesian with Paul in the city and suppose he brought him in. Supposition becomes accusation, accusation becomes riot. The whole city is stirred up; the people run together, seize Paul, drag him out of the temple, and the doors are shut. As they are seeking to kill him, word comes to the tribune of the cohort that all Jerusalem is in confusion. Soldiers and centurions run down; the beating stops when the crowd sees the tribune; Paul is arrested and bound with two chains — Agabus's prophecy fulfilled to the letter, in Roman iron. The crowd follows, crying, "Away with him!" As Paul is about to be brought into the barracks, he asks the tribune in Greek, "May I say something to you?" The tribune, surprised, mistook him for an Egyptian revolutionary. Paul identifies himself: a Jew from Tarsus, a citizen of no obscure city. "I beg you, permit me to speak to the people." Permission is granted. The bound apostle stands on the steps, motions with his hand — and a great silence falls. The chapter ends with the prisoner about to preach to the mob that tried to kill him. Bound, exactly as prophesied, and in God's hand entirely.

Key verses

"For I am ready not only to be imprisoned but even to die in Jerusalem for the name of the Lord Jesus." — Acts 21:13 (ESV)

"And since he would not be persuaded, we ceased and said, 'Let the will of the Lord be done.'" — Acts 21:14 (ESV)

"And when he himself had related in detail what God had done among the Gentiles through his ministry, they glorified God." — Acts 21:19–20 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Acts 21 is subtle, because it wears the face of love: the people who care most about Paul plead with him, weeping, to turn back from God's known will — and in Jerusalem a false charge, built on a supposition, nearly gets him killed by a mob in God's own temple courts. Love can argue against obedience, and crowds can be certain and wrong at the same time. But the good news is the church's surrender and the apostle's readiness: "Let the will of the Lord be done" — and a man ready not only to be bound but to die for the name of the Lord Jesus, carried out of the riot on Roman shoulders, exactly as prophesied, exactly in God's hand.

A note from the Reformers. Calvin noted that the church's hardest obedience in this chapter is not Paul's but his friends': ceasing to plead, and praying "the Lord's will be done" over someone they love. Surrendering our dearest people to God's will, he taught, is faith at its most costly — and its most peaceful.

Reflection question: Paul's friends loved him and still had to surrender him to God's will. Is there someone or something you love that you need to place, finally, in the Lord's hands — and will you pray this week, out loud, "Let the will of the Lord be done"?
