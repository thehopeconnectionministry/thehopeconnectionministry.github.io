Acts 22 — Paul Tells His Story: Testimony on the Temple Steps

Jesus in Acts — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Acts
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Jesus is the risen Lord who turns a persecutor's story into the church's testimony. Paul tells it on the very steps of the temple whose mob tried to kill him: what he was, how Jesus met him, what Jesus did, and where Jesus sent him. The Jesus of Acts 22 is the One who appears to His enemies, washes away sins of persecutors, and sends the forgiven man to the very people the crowd cannot bear to hear about.

Commentary

The defense begins: a Hebrew silence (22:1–5). Paul addresses the crowd: "Brothers and fathers, hear the defense that I now make before you." And when they hear that he is addressing them in the Hebrew language, they become even more quiet. The mob quiets for its mother tongue. Paul begins where every testimony begins — with what he was. He is a Jew, born in Tarsus, brought up in Jerusalem, educated at the feet of Gamaliel according to the strict manner of the law of the fathers, "being zealous for God, as all of you are this day." That line is pastoral genius and perfect honesty at once: he has stood exactly where they stand, zeal and all. "I persecuted this Way to the death, binding and delivering to prison both men and women," as the high priest and the whole council bear witness. He does not minimize it. Testimony that hides the bad news makes the good news sound small.

The Damascus road, retold (22:6–11). As he journeyed and drew near Damascus, about noon a great light from heaven suddenly shone around him. He fell to the ground and heard the voice: "Saul, Saul, why are you persecuting me?" "Who are you, Lord?" "I am Jesus of Nazareth, whom you are persecuting." His companions saw the light but did not understand the voice. "What shall I do, Lord?" — the persecutor's first prayer is a surrender and a job application in one sentence. The Lord directs him into Damascus, where he will be told all that is appointed for him to do. He cannot see because of the brightness of the glory of that light, and is led by the hand by those who are with him. The man who came to lead captives is led like a child. Every retelling of this story in Acts adds something; here Paul stresses that the glory itself blinded him — he met more light than his old eyes could hold.

Ananias and baptism (22:12–16). Ananias — in this telling, "a devout man according to the law, well spoken of by all the Jews who lived there" — comes to him, stands by him, and says, "Brother Saul, receive your sight." And in that very hour Paul receives his sight and sees him. Ananias delivers the commission: "The God of our fathers appointed you to know his will, to see the Righteous One and to hear a voice from his mouth; for you will be a witness for him to everyone of what you have seen and heard." Then the sentence Luke chose as this chapter's memory verse — Ananias's urgent, tender command to the church's former enemy:

"And now why do you wait? Rise and be baptized and wash away your sins, calling on his name." — Acts 22:16 (ESV)

Wash away your sins — said to a man with the saints' blood on his conscience. The washing is real, and it is received the only way washing ever is: by calling on His name.

The trance in the temple (22:17–21). Paul adds a piece of the story told nowhere else. Back in Jerusalem, praying in this very temple, he fell into a trance and saw the Lord, who warned him to leave quickly because Jerusalem would not accept his testimony. Paul argued — even in prayer, Paul argues — that his reputation as a persecutor would make his testimony credible here. The Lord's answer closes the argument and opens the Gentile mission: "Go, for I will send you far away to the Gentiles."

One word detonates the quiet (22:22–29). Up to that word — Gentiles — they listen. Then they raise their voices: "Away with such a fellow from the earth! For he should not be allowed to live!" The bad news of this chapter is that a heart can hear a whole testimony of grace and choke on grace for outsiders. They throw off their cloaks and fling dust into the air — Stephen's execution is being re-enacted in gestures. The tribune orders Paul brought into the barracks to be examined by flogging, to find out why they shout against him. Paul is stretched out for the whips when he asks the centurion standing by a quiet, devastating question: "Is it lawful for you to flog a man who is a Roman citizen and uncondemned?" The centurion warns the tribune; the tribune comes and asks if it is true. "Yes." The tribune notes he bought his citizenship for a large sum. Paul: "But I am a citizen by birth." Immediately those who were about to examine him withdraw, and the tribune is afraid — for he has already bound a Roman citizen. God keeps His witness for Rome by means nobody expected: a birth certificate, deployed at the last second before the whip.

Before the council (22:30). The next day, desiring to know the real reason Paul is accused by the Jews, the tribune unbinds him and commands the chief priests and all the council to meet, and brings Paul down and sets him before them. The testimony on the steps becomes a defense before the Sanhedrin. Chapter 23 is already standing in the doorway.

Key verses

"I am Jesus of Nazareth, whom you are persecuting." — Acts 22:8 (ESV)

"And now why do you wait? Rise and be baptized and wash away your sins, calling on his name." — Acts 22:16 (ESV)

"Go, for I will send you far away to the Gentiles." — Acts 22:21 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Acts 22 is that a heart can hear a whole testimony of grace — a persecutor blinded by glory, washed, and sent — and choke on one word: grace for outsiders. The crowd listened in silence through everything until the mission reached the Gentiles. But the good news is that the story stands anyway. The risen Jesus turns persecutors into witnesses, washes away the sins of the very man who held the coats, and sends him far away with the news. And when the whip is already stretched out, God keeps His witness for the work ahead by means nobody expected.

A note from the Reformers. Spurgeon urged ordinary Christians to learn Paul's three-part pattern here — what I was, what Jesus did, where He sent me — as the shape of every useful testimony. No embellishment is needed, he said; a true story of grace, told plainly, is the church's oldest sermon.

Reflection question: Paul's testimony was simply what he was, what Jesus did, and where Jesus sent him. If you had two quiet minutes on the steps, what is your story — in those three parts? Write it in your notebook this week, plainly, in a page or less.
