Acts 15 — Grace Settled: The Jerusalem Council

Jesus in Acts — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Acts
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Jesus is the Lord Jesus through whose grace alone — Peter's own words — both Jew and Gentile are saved. Acts 15 is the church gathering to defend one gospel and sending that freedom out in a letter written to set burdened consciences free. No chapter in Acts mattered more to the church's future: add one requirement to Christ, and you quietly replace Him. The council refuses, and the gospel stays good news.

Commentary

The dispute at Antioch (15:1–2). Some men come down from Judea to Antioch and teach the brothers a sentence that would undo the gospel: "Unless you are circumcised according to the custom of Moses, you cannot be saved." Paul and Barnabas have "no small dissension and debate" with them — Luke's understatement for a fight for the gospel itself. This is not a quarrel about customs or food. It is a question about whether Christ is enough. The church decides the question must be settled where it belongs: Paul and Barnabas and some of the others are appointed to go up to Jerusalem to the apostles and elders. On the way, passing through Phoenicia and Samaria, they describe in detail the conversion of the Gentiles — "and brought great joy to all the brothers." Even on the road to a doctrinal battle, the report of grace makes churches glad.

The council: Peter's testimony (15:3–11). In Jerusalem, some believers from the party of the Pharisees rise and state the case for the requirement: "It is necessary to circumcise them and to order them to keep the law of Moses." The apostles and elders gather to consider the matter, and after much debate Peter stands up. His testimony settles the heart of it. God chose that by his mouth the Gentiles should hear the word of the gospel and believe — Cornelius's house, chapter 10. "And God, who knows the heart, bore witness to them, by giving them the Holy Spirit just as he did to us, and he made no distinction between us and them, having cleansed their hearts by faith." Then Peter names the bad news honestly: the law is a yoke "that neither our fathers nor we have been able to bear." Why put God to the test by laying that yoke on the disciples? And then the conclusion, which is the gospel itself:

"But we believe that we will be saved through the grace of the Lord Jesus, just as they will." — Acts 15:11 (ESV)

The Greek word is charis — grace, God's free and unmerited favor. Peter's whole case at the council stands on this one word: saved through the charis of the Lord Jesus. Grace is not God helping those who help themselves. It is God saving those who cannot. And notice Peter's order: not "they will be saved like us," but "we will be saved... just as they will." The Jewish apostle puts himself on the Gentiles' ground: one way of salvation, for Jew and Gentile alike — grace, through faith, in Jesus.

Barnabas, Paul, and James (15:12–21). The assembly falls silent and listens to Barnabas and Paul as they relate what signs and wonders God has done through them among the Gentiles. Evidence before theory: God has already voted, with lives. Then James — the Lord's brother, leading the Jerusalem church — speaks, and anchors the decision in Scripture: the prophets agree with this; Amos promised that God would rebuild the tent of David "that the remnant of mankind may seek the Lord, and all the Gentiles who are called by my name." God's plan for the nations is not a late adjustment; it is in the prophets. James's judgment: "we should not trouble those of the Gentiles who turn to God." Not trouble them. The council asks the Gentile believers to abstain from a short list — things polluted by idols, sexual immorality, what has been strangled, and blood — not as conditions of salvation, but as the terms of table fellowship in mixed churches, so that Jewish and Gentile believers can eat together without offense. Grace for salvation; consideration for fellowship. The distinction is the wisdom of the whole chapter.

The letter (15:22–35). The whole church chooses men to go with Paul and Barnabas to Antioch — Judas called Barsabbas and Silas, leading men among the brothers — carrying a letter. The letter disowns the troublemakers ("we gave them no instructions"), commends "our beloved Barnabas and Paul, men who have risked their lives for the name of our Lord Jesus Christ," and delivers the decision as the mind of a united church: "For it has seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us to lay on you no greater burden than these requirements." No greater burden. When the letter is read in Antioch, the congregation rejoices "because of its encouragement." Doctrine that sets consciences free produces joy — that is the test of the council's work. Judas and Silas, themselves prophets, encourage and strengthen the brothers with many words. Silas stays on — and chapter 15's closing verses will show why.

The disagreement (15:36–41). The chapter ends with a human crack. Paul proposes revisiting the brothers in every city where they proclaimed the word. Barnabas wants to take John Mark; Paul does not think it wise, since Mark withdrew from them in Pamphylia and did not go with them to the work. A sharp disagreement arises, and they separate: Barnabas takes Mark and sails to Cyprus; Paul chooses Silas and departs, commended by the brothers to the grace of the Lord. Luke does not hide it, and we should not either: two godly men, both right about some things, divide over a judgment call. But watch what God does with it — now there are two missionary teams instead of one, and Mark, the man at the center of the split, will one day be "very useful" to Paul and the penman of a Gospel. The gospel of grace settles the church's doctrine in this chapter; grace is still at work on the church's people.

Key verses

"And he made no distinction between us and them, having cleansed their hearts by faith." — Acts 15:9 (ESV)

"But we believe that we will be saved through the grace of the Lord Jesus, just as they will." — Acts 15:11 (ESV)

"For it has seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us to lay on you no greater burden than these requirements." — Acts 15:28 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Acts 15 is Peter's honest sentence: the law is a yoke that neither our fathers nor we have been able to bear — and every requirement we add to Christ for salvation quietly replaces Him. The teachers at Antioch were not villains; they were sincere men adding one small condition, and that one condition would have unmade the gospel. But the good news is Peter's conclusion, and it is the gospel itself: we are saved through the grace of the Lord Jesus — Jew and Gentile, the same way, the only way. One Savior, one grace, no greater burden. The church fought for that freedom once, in council, so that burdened consciences could be free forever.

A note from the Reformers. No chapter armed the Reformation like this one. Luther appealed to Acts 15 constantly: when men lay new burdens on consciences as necessary for salvation, the council's answer stands — hearts are cleansed by faith, and we are saved through the grace of the Lord Jesus. Nothing added. Nothing needed.

Reflection question: The council refused to lay extra burdens on new believers. Is there a requirement you have quietly added to God's grace — for yourself, or for someone else? Name it, and lay it down this week at the foot of the one gospel.
