Acts 23 — Cheer Up, Paul: The Lord Stands By at Midnight

Jesus in Acts — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Acts
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Jesus is the Lord who stands by His prisoner at midnight and promises Rome. No oath, plot, or council can cancel what the risen Christ has spoken. Acts 23 is the lowest night of Paul's ministry — struck at a high priest's order, rescued from a riot, with forty men sworn to kill him — and it contains one of the tenderest sentences in the book: the Lord stood by him.

Commentary

Before the Sanhedrin (23:1–10). Paul looks intently at the council and begins: "Brothers, I have lived my life before God in all good conscience up to this day." The high priest Ananias commands those standing by to strike him on the mouth. Paul flashes back — "God is going to strike you, you whitewashed wall!" — and when told he has reviled God's high priest, he steps back with Scripture: "I did not know, brothers, that he was the high priest, for it is written, 'You shall not speak evil of a ruler of your people.'" Whether Paul's eyesight, the informality of the session, or irony explains his not knowing, his submission to the written word is immediate: even an unjust ruler is answered within the law's bounds. Then Paul, perceiving the council is part Sadducees and part Pharisees, cries out: "Brothers, I am a Pharisee, a son of Pharisees. It is with respect to the hope and the resurrection of the dead that I am on trial." It is strategy, and it is also the literal truth — the resurrection is the case; without it there is no gospel and no charge. A dissension arises: the Sadducees say there is no resurrection, nor angel, nor spirit, but the Pharisees acknowledge them all. The scribes of the Pharisees' party stand up and contend sharply: "We find nothing wrong in this man. What if a spirit or an angel spoke to him?" The dissension becomes violent, and the tribune, afraid Paul will be torn to pieces, commands the soldiers to go down, take him away by force, and bring him into the barracks. Paul's defense collapses into an uproar, and soldiers drag him back to his cell. By every human measure, the day is a failure.

The Lord stood by him (23:11). Then Luke writes the sentence the whole chapter leans on:

"The following night the Lord stood by him and said, 'Take courage, for as you have testified to the facts about me in Jerusalem, so you must testify also in Rome.'" — Acts 23:11 (ESV)

The Greek word is tharsei — "cheer up, take courage." It is the word Jesus spoke to a paralyzed man on a mat, to disciples in a storm at sea, and on the last night of His earthly teaching. Now the risen Lord speaks it Himself to a prisoner in a cell, and it has lost none of its power on the way. Notice what the Lord does not do: He does not apologize for the day, explain the day, or promise an easy road. He commends the testimony already given — failed-looking as it was — and renews the destination: Rome. The good news is not that the plot evaporates. It is that the Lord Himself comes into the cell at midnight with a promise.

The plot, and a brave nephew (23:12–22). When it is day, more than forty Jews form a conspiracy and bind themselves by oath neither to eat nor drink until they have killed Paul. They recruit the chief priests and elders into the scheme: the council will ask the tribune to bring Paul down for a fuller hearing, and the ambush will do the rest. It is a careful, murderous, religiously sanctioned plot. But the son of Paul's sister hears of the ambush — Luke's only mention of Paul's family in Acts, and it saves his life. The young man comes to the barracks and tells Paul. Paul calls a centurion: "Take this young man to the tribune, for he has something to tell him." The tribune takes the boy by the hand — a kind Roman officer with a frightened teenager — listens, and charges him to tell no one. God works through a brave nephew, a careful commander, and a hand held at the right moment.

Four hundred seventy soldiers (23:23–35). The tribune calls two centurions and orders two hundred soldiers, seventy horsemen, and two hundred spearmen to leave by the third hour of the night for Caesarea, with mounts for Paul. Four hundred seventy armed men to move one prisoner past forty plotters — Rome's full machinery, mobilized by a midnight promise it knows nothing about. The tribune writes a letter to Governor Felix that presents his own conduct favorably — Luke lets us smile at the self-protection in it — and reports the facts: the man was seized, about to be killed, rescued, and charged over questions of Jewish law, "nothing deserving death or imprisonment." The soldiers bring Paul by night to Antipatris, the horsemen carry him on to Caesarea, and the letter and the prisoner are delivered to the governor. Felix reads it, asks what province Paul is from, and says, "I will give you a hearing when your accusers arrive." Paul is kept in Herod's praetorium. What the Lord stands by, no oath can cancel — and the road to Rome is now paved with Roman spears.

Key verses

"Take courage, for as you have testified to the facts about me in Jerusalem, so you must testify also in Rome." — Acts 23:11 (ESV)

"It is with respect to the hope and the resurrection of the dead that I am on trial." — Acts 23:6 (ESV)

"And he said, 'I will give you a hearing when your accusers arrive.' And he commanded him to be guarded in Herod's praetorium." — Acts 23:35 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Acts 23 is as low as Paul's ministry gets: struck on the mouth at the high priest's order, rescued from a riot of his own people's council, and more than forty men sworn by oath to kill him before they eat. Human religion and human politics are both arrayed against one prisoner. But the good news is the night that follows the worst day: the Lord stands by His servant in the cell and speaks courage and Rome into the dark — and then works through a brave nephew, a careful commander, and two hundred soldiers to carry His prisoner safely out. What the Lord stands by, no oath can cancel.

A note from the Reformers. Calvin drew comfort for afflicted believers from the timing here: the Lord's visit came not before the hard day but the night after it, in the cell. God's consolations, he taught, are timed for the dark — and a promise spoken by Christ at midnight is worth more than a crowd's applause at noon.

Reflection question: The Lord stood by Paul the night after his worst day. Where do you need to hear "take courage" from Him tonight — and what promise of His can you hold in the dark, written in your notebook before you sleep?
