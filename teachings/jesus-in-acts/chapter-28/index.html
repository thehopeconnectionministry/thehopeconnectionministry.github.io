Acts 28 — Without Hindrance: The Gospel in Rome

Jesus in Acts — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Acts
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Jesus is the risen Lord whose witness stands at last where He promised in chapter 1 — in Rome itself, chained in body, preaching the kingdom of God without hindrance. The book ends where the risen Christ said it would. The last picture of Acts is not a chain; it is an open door, and a prisoner welcoming all who come to it. That is Jesus in Acts, from the first verse to the last: keeping His promise, pouring out His Spirit, and carrying His gospel to the ends of the earth.

Commentary

Malta: kindness, a viper, and healing (28:1–10). After they are brought safely through, they learn the island is called Malta. The native people show them unusual kindness — they kindle a fire and welcome them all, because it has begun to rain and is cold. Shipwrecked prisoners, welcomed at a fire by strangers: grace keeps arriving in unlikely uniforms. Paul gathers a bundle of sticks and lays them on the fire when a viper, driven out by the heat, fastens on his hand. The islanders see the creature hanging from his hand and say to one another, "This man must be a murderer; though he has escaped from the sea, Justice has not allowed him to live." Human religion reads providence quickly and wrongly in both directions — for when Paul shakes the creature off into the fire and suffers no harm, they wait for him to swell up or fall dead, and after waiting long and seeing nothing, they change their minds and say he is a god. Paul, who tore his garments at Lystra rather than be worshiped, will have none of it here either; Luke simply moves on to the work. The father of Publius, the chief man of the island, lies ill with fever and dysentery; Paul visits him, prays, lays his hands on him, and heals him. Then the rest of the people on the island who have diseases also come and are cured. They honor the party with many honors, and when they are ready to sail, the islanders put on board whatever they need. Malta receives the gospel's kindness for three months, and sends its witnesses off supplied.

At last, Rome (28:11–16). After three months they set sail in a ship that has wintered in the island — an Alexandrian ship with the twin gods as a figurehead, Luke notes without anxiety; God's providence sails on pagan shipping without contamination. They touch Syracuse, Rhegium, and Puteoli, where they find brothers and are invited to stay seven days. "And so we came to Rome." And the brothers there, when they hear about them, come as far as the Forum of Appius and Three Taverns to meet them — forty miles, some of them, to walk a prisoner home. "On seeing them, Paul thanked God and took courage." Even apostles are strengthened by the sight of believers coming down the road. And when they come into Rome, Paul is allowed to stay by himself, with the soldier who guards him. The capital of the world, a rented room, a rotating guard, a chain — and the promise of Acts 1:8 standing fulfilled in a doorway.

A whole day with the leaders of Rome's Jews (28:17–22). After three days Paul calls together the local leaders of the Jews. His opening is a model of peacemaking: "Brothers, though I had done nothing against our people or the customs of our fathers, yet I was delivered as a prisoner from Jerusalem into the hands of the Romans." Examined, they wished to set him at liberty, since there was no charge deserving death; the Jews objected; he was compelled to appeal to Caesar — "not that I had any charge to bring against my nation." Then the sentence that explains his whole life and his chain: "For this reason, therefore, I have asked to see you and speak with you, since it is because of the hope of Israel that I am wearing this chain." The hope of Israel — the resurrection, the Messiah — is why the chain is on his wrist. Their answer is fair and curious: they have received no letters about him, no one has reported or spoken evil of him, "but we desire to hear from you what your views are, for with regard to this sect we know that everywhere it is spoken against." A sect spoken against everywhere — and its prisoner is given a hearing in the capital. They appoint a day.

From morning till evening (28:23–28). They come to his lodging in greater numbers, and from morning till evening he expounds to them, testifying to the kingdom of God and trying to convince them about Jesus both from the Law of Moses and from the Prophets. A whole day in the Scriptures with one chained teacher. Some are convinced by what he says, but others disbelieve. And disagreeing among themselves, they depart after Paul makes one statement — Isaiah's word, spoken by the Holy Spirit to their fathers: "Go to this people, and say, 'You will indeed hear but never understand, and you will indeed see but never perceive.' For this people's heart has grown dull... lest they should see with their eyes and hear with their ears and understand with their heart and turn, and I would heal them." The bad news follows this gospel to the capital itself: hearing without understanding, the oldest tragedy in Israel's story, repeated in a Roman lodging. But the good news has the final word, as it had the first: "Therefore let it be known to you that this salvation of God has been sent to the Gentiles; they will listen." They will listen. The sentence is not bitterness; it is Isaiah's promise and Jesus' map arriving at their destination together.

Two whole years, unhindered (28:30–31). Luke's last paragraph is the book's last picture, and he chooses every word of it:

"He lived there two whole years at his own expense, and welcomed all who came to him, proclaiming the kingdom of God and teaching about the Lord Jesus Christ with all boldness and without hindrance." — Acts 28:30–31 (ESV)

The Greek word for "without hindrance" is akōlytōs — unprevented, unhindered. It is the very last word of the book of Acts in Greek. The chained apostle's gospel is unchained. Paul does not get his verdict in Luke's account; the church does not get its ending; the book simply stops with the word still going out from a rented room to all who come. That is deliberate. The story in Acts does not really end — it stops with the gospel still moving, because the next chapter is the one being written in the lives of believers right now.

Key verses

"Since it is because of the hope of Israel that I am wearing this chain." — Acts 28:20 (ESV)

"Therefore let it be known to you that this salvation of God has been sent to the Gentiles; they will listen." — Acts 28:28 (ESV)

"Proclaiming the kingdom of God and teaching about the Lord Jesus Christ with all boldness and without hindrance." — Acts 28:31 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Acts 28 is Isaiah's old word standing over the parting crowd: people can hear all day, from morning till evening, out of Moses and the Prophets, and still not understand — hearts grown dull in the very presence of the truth. That tragedy follows the gospel even to Rome. But the good news has the final word, as it had the first: this salvation of God has been sent to the nations, and they will listen. For two whole years the chained apostle welcomes all who come, proclaiming the kingdom with all boldness and without hindrance. The book that began in Jerusalem ends in Rome, and its last picture is not a chain. It is an open door.

A note from the Reformers. Spurgeon preached the last word of Acts as the church's standing orders: the gospel is akōlytōs — unhindered — whatever the chains on its messengers. Empires have tried to hinder it for two thousand years, he said, and have only underlined the word. Our part is Paul's part: welcome all who come, and teach Christ with all boldness.

Reflection question: Acts ends with the gospel unhindered in the heart of the empire, welcomed in through one man's rented room. Where is your Rome — the place you most want to see the word of Christ reach — and what is your part in carrying it there: a door opened, a welcome given, a word spoken this week?
