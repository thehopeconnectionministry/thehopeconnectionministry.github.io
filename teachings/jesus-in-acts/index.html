The Hope Connection

"But you will receive power when the Holy Spirit has come upon you, and you will be my witnesses in Jerusalem and in all Judea and Samaria, and to the end of the earth." — Acts 1:8 (ESV)

Introduction — Acts at a Glance

Acts is the second half of one great work. Luke's Gospel tells what Jesus began to do and teach; Acts tells what the risen, ascended Jesus went on doing — not from a hillside in Galilee this time, but from the right hand of the Father, by His Holy Spirit, through ordinary people. That is why this book never really feels finished. It begins with a promise and ends with an open door. In between, fishermen preach like prophets, a persecutor becomes an apostle, jail doors open at midnight, and the good news about Jesus crosses every fence men build — Jew and Gentile, clean and unclean, Jerusalem and Rome. If you have ever felt too small, too ordinary, or too late to be used by God, Acts is your book.

Who wrote it. Luke wrote Acts. He was a physician (Colossians 4:14), the author of the Gospel that bears his name, and a faithful companion of the apostle Paul. Luke traveled with Paul on parts of the journeys this book records — you can watch for the places where his "they" quietly becomes "we" (see Acts 16:10). He was not an apostle, and he does not pretend to be. He was a careful historian and a beloved friend who stayed when others left: near the end of his life Paul writes, "Luke alone is with me" (2 Timothy 4:11). Luke researched his material carefully, interviewed eyewitnesses, and set it down in order, so that ordinary believers could have certainty about the things they had been taught (Luke 1:3–4).

Who it was written for. Like the Gospel, Acts is addressed to Theophilus — most likely a real man, a believer (or a seeker close to believing) who needed a solid, ordered account of how the gospel ran from Jerusalem to the capital of the world. Beyond Theophilus, Luke wrote for the church: for Jewish believers learning that God's promise was always bigger than their fences, for Gentile believers learning that they truly belonged, and for every believer since who has wondered whether the same Spirit still works today. Acts answers: He does.

When and where. Luke wrote around AD 62–65. The events run from about AD 30 to about AD 62 — roughly the church's first thirty years. The setting is the whole map of Acts 1:8: it opens in Jerusalem, moves through Judea and Samaria, crosses into Asia Minor and Greece, and ends in Rome, with Paul under house arrest, preaching the kingdom of God without hindrance. The book ends where it does not because the story ran out, but because Luke's account had reached the heart of the empire — and the gospel was still going.

The shape of the book. Acts 1:8 is not only the key verse; it is the table of contents. "You will be my witnesses in Jerusalem" — chapters 1–7: the Spirit falls, the church is born, and the witness is borne in the city where Jesus was crucified. "And in all Judea and Samaria" — chapters 8–12: persecution scatters the believers, Philip preaches in Samaria, Saul is converted, Peter is sent to a Roman household, and the church learns that grace is wider than it thought. "And to the end of the earth" — chapters 13–28: Paul and Barnabas are sent from Antioch, the council at Jerusalem guards the one gospel of grace, and Paul carries the witness through Asia and Greece, through arrest and trial and storm, all the way to Rome. Over it all stands one Person at work: the ascended Christ, pouring out His Spirit, building His church, and keeping every promise He made.

The key verse. Everything in Acts flows out of one sentence, spoken by the risen Jesus just before He ascended:

"But you will receive power when the Holy Spirit has come upon you, and you will be my witnesses in Jerusalem and in all Judea and Samaria, and to the end of the earth." — Acts 1:8 (ESV)

Notice whose work this is. The disciples do not manufacture power; they receive it. They do not invent the mission; Jesus assigns it. And the theme running through every chapter is this: Jesus in Acts is the ascended Lord who keeps His promise, pours out His Spirit, and carries His gospel to the ends of the earth.

Read in the Reformation way. This commentary reads Acts the way the Reformers taught the church to read every book of the Bible, and the five watchwords are not imported into Acts — they are found in it. Sola scriptura — Scripture alone is the rule: at Berea, ordinary believers were commended for testing even an apostle's preaching against the Scriptures daily (Acts 17:11), and every sermon in this book argues from the written Word. Sola gratia — grace alone saves: when a jailer cries, "What must I do to be saved?" the answer is not a list of duties but a gift to receive, and the Jerusalem council settles it — "we believe that we will be saved through the grace of the Lord Jesus" (Acts 15:11). Sola fide — faith alone receives that grace: hearts are cleansed by faith (Acts 15:9), and everyone who believes is justified from everything the law of Moses could never justify (Acts 13:39). Solus Christus — Christ alone is the Savior: "there is no other name under heaven given among men by which we must be saved" (Acts 4:12). And soli Deo gloria — glory to God alone: when crowds try to worship Paul and Barnabas as gods, the apostles tear their garments and point away from themselves to the living God (Acts 14:14–15). Along the way you will meet brief nods to Luther, Calvin, and Spurgeon where they genuinely shed light on a passage — a line or two, never a lecture. And in every chapter the law and the gospel are kept distinct: the law shows us our sin and our need (the bad news), and the gospel shows us Christ and His finished work (the good news). Peter's Pentecost sermon does exactly this — "you crucified" first, then forgiveness for the very people who did it.

How to use this commentary. Each chapter section begins with "Jesus in this chapter" — a short look at how Christ is revealed there. Then the commentary walks the chapter's movements in order, explaining the text plainly and fully. Key verses are quoted in full from the English Standard Version. Each chapter closes with "The bad news and the good news" — the law-then-gospel pattern, the weight of the chapter and the hope in Christ — and one reflection question to carry into prayer. Read it slowly. Read it with your Bible open. And remember, as you read, that the Lord of this book is not finished. The same ascended Christ who worked in these chapters is at work still — meeting people where they are, in the name of Jesus.

How to Use This Commentary

This commentary was built to be used, not just read. Here is how to get the most out of it.

The order of every session. Work through each chapter the same way, in this order:

  1. Read the chapter in your own Bible first. Open your Bible to the chapter of Acts and read it all the way through before you read a word of commentary. The commentary serves the Scripture; it never replaces it. Read it out loud if you can — Acts was written to be heard, full of speeches and sermons, and you will catch things with your ears that your eyes slide past.
  2. Then read the commentary section for that chapter. Keep your Bible open beside it and look up the verses as they come. Start with "Jesus in this chapter" — before anything else, fix your eyes on how Christ is revealed there. Charles Spurgeon said a passage is not finished until Christ has been seen in it, and that is the aim of every section here.
  3. Then sit with the reflection question. Each chapter ends with one reflection question and a "bad news and good news" paragraph. Do not rush past them. Read the bad news honestly — let the law do its searching work — and then read the good news slowly, out loud if you can. Answer the reflection question in prayer, out loud to God, not just in your head.
  4. Write in a notebook. Keep a simple notebook with this study. Write down your answer to the reflection question, your answer to the study guide question for that chapter (see the Acts Study Guide below), and the memory verse for the chapter. A truth is not really yours until you have written it, prayed it, and lived it. You do not need anything fancy — a dollar notebook and a pen will do the whole book.

The rhythm: three sessions a week. This fits the study rhythm used across The Hope Connection: three sessions a week, one book at a time, until the book is done. At one chapter per session, three sessions a week, you will walk through all twenty-eight chapters of Acts in about nine and a half weeks. If you want to go slower — and slower is often better — spread one chapter across the whole week: session one, read the chapter in your Bible and the "Jesus in this chapter" opening; session two, read the commentary; session three, return to the chapter, work through the questions, and pray it back to God. Either pace is fine. What matters is the steadiness, not the speed.

No catch-up piles. This rule is not negotiable, because it protects the whole habit: if you miss a day, you do not owe anybody two chapters. You simply pick up at the next chapter at the next session. Life happens — sickness, family, work, hard weeks. A study rhythm that piles up guilt every time life interrupts will not survive a month. This one bends and keeps going. Missed a week? Start again at the next chapter. The Lord is not keeping a tally, and neither are we.

Using it alone. Everything above is written for one reader, one Bible, one notebook, and the Lord. Pray before you open the book — a short, real prayer: "Lord, open my eyes, and let this Word search me." Pray again when you finish, turning the chapter's good news into thanksgiving and its bad news into confession.

Using it with a spouse or a friend. Read the chapter out loud together at the start of the session — take turns reading. Then read the commentary, or take turns reading it aloud too. Talk through the reflection question and the study guide question together, honestly. Pray for each other out of what the chapter showed you. Acts is a book about the church together — praying together, eating together, sending together — and two people studying it together will see things one reader alone will miss.

Using it in a small group. One chapter per meeting. Have someone read the chapter aloud from the Bible to open. Walk through the commentary section together, stopping wherever the text raises a question. Save real time at the end for the reflection question and the study guide question — that is where study becomes life. Close in prayer, and let the chapter's memory verse be the group's verse for the week: read it together at the start of the next meeting and see who can say it from memory. Keep the group small enough that everybody talks, and keep the door open — Acts is good news for people who have never opened a Bible, and every chapter here is written plain enough for a brand-new believer to follow.

The memory verses. The study guide below gives one memory verse for each chapter — twenty-eight verses, the spine of Acts. If you take one verse a week alongside the chapter rhythm, by the time you finish the book you will carry its whole story in your heart: the promise of the Spirit, repentance and forgiveness, no other name, grace alone settled, a jailer saved at midnight, and a gospel preached without hindrance. That is treasure no one can take from you.

Study Questions — One per Chapter

Work each question in your notebook after the chapter's reflection question. Many of them follow the Bible's own pattern of putting off the old and putting on the new (Ephesians 4:22–24; Colossians 3:8–10).

  1. Acts 1 — Wait for the power. Jesus told His witnesses to wait before they worked. Put off running ahead in your own strength; put on prayerful waiting. Where is He asking you to wait for His power — and what will waiting look like this week: a set time, a closed door accepted, a prayer kept?
  2. Acts 2 — The promise is for the far off. Peter's promise reaches "all who are far off." Put off writing someone off as too far gone; put on praying for them by name. Who in your life seems far off, and what would it mean to believe this promise is for them too?
  3. Acts 3 — Ask for more than coins. The lame man asked for money and received healing. Put off small, safe asking; put on asking God for the deeper thing. What small thing have you been asking for, when He may want to give you something far deeper?
  4. Acts 4 — Be known as one who was with Jesus. The council could tell the apostles had been with Jesus. Put off keeping quiet about His name where it costs you; put on plain, kind boldness. Where are you keeping quiet — and what is one sentence you could say there this week?
  5. Acts 5 — Give the whole gift. Ananias wanted the appearance of devotion without the whole cost. Put off holding back part of the price while presenting the whole gift; put on whole-hearted honesty before God. Is there a place where you are doing this? Name it to God today.
  6. Acts 6 — Serve the small need well. The apostles treated a food complaint as a spiritual matter worth solving well. Put off despising small service; put on seeing tables as ministry. Is there a small, unnoticed need around you that God may be calling you to meet this week?
  7. Acts 7 — Forgive like Stephen. Stephen forgave his killers while the stones were falling. Put off nursing the record of wrongs; put on praying for the person who wronged you. Who do you need to forgive — and will you pray Stephen's prayer for them by name this week?
  8. Acts 8 — Start where they are. Philip started where the Ethiopian was reading and preached Jesus from there. Put off waiting for the perfect opening; put on sitting down beside someone's questions. Whose questions could you simply sit down beside this week, starting where they are?
  9. Acts 9 — Stopped in order to be sent. Jesus stopped Saul mid-road and mid-sin. Put off running hard in a wrong direction; put on stopping when He speaks. Where might He be stopping you — not to shame you, but to turn you toward the work He has for you?
  10. Acts 10 — Cross the line grace crossed. Peter's fence was respectable and religious, and God tore it down anyway. Put off drawing lines around the table that God has erased; put on going to the outsider. What line have you drawn that the gospel may be calling you to cross?
  11. Acts 11 — Glorify God for grace to others. The Jerusalem believers moved from criticizing grace to glorifying God for it. Put off questioning a work of God in someone else's life; put on celebrating it. Is there grace in another person's life you have been questioning instead of celebrating?
  12. Acts 12 — Trust God with both answers. James died and Peter was delivered, and the church trusted God in both. Put off treating prayer as a formula you control; put on trusting prayer that leaves the outcome with God. Can you trust Him with the prayers He answers differently than you asked?
  13. Acts 13 — Stop trying to justify yourself. Paul says the law of Moses could not justify you from all things, but Jesus can. Put off every homemade way of making yourself right with God; put on receiving justification as a gift. What are you still trying to use to make yourself right with God?
  14. Acts 14 — Tell the truth about the hard road. Paul told new believers the truth about affliction instead of hiding it. Put off expecting an easy road; put on continuing in the faith through trouble. How does knowing the road is hard — and that God walks it with you — change the way you face your current trouble?
  15. Acts 15 — Add nothing to grace. The council refused to lay extra burdens on new believers. Put off every requirement you have quietly added to God's grace — for yourself or for someone else; put on resting in grace alone. Name one added requirement you will lay down this week.
  16. Acts 16 — Believe for your household. Lydia's heart was opened by the Lord; the jailer's whole house was baptized by morning. Put off despairing over your family; put on praying and speaking the simple word: "Believe in the Lord Jesus." Who in your household will you pray for daily this week?
  17. Acts 17 — Search the Scriptures daily. The Bereans searched the Scriptures daily to test even an apostle's preaching. Put off taking every teaching on trust; put on testing what you hear by the Word. What would daily searching look like in your life — a time, a place, a plan?
  18. Acts 18 — Stay where He has people. God told Paul He had "many people in this city" before Paul could see any of them. Put off quitting a hard place too soon; put on trusting that God is already at work there. Who might God already be preparing in your town that you have not met yet?
  19. Acts 19 — Burn what ties you to the old life. The Ephesian believers burned what tied them to their old life, at real cost. Put off keeping a private reserve of the old life; put on a clean, costly break. What would your public, costly break with the old life look like?
  20. Acts 20 — Finish your course. Paul counted finishing his race above holding his life dear. Put off guarding your comfort above your calling; put on commending yourself and those you love to the word of God's grace. What is the race God has given you — and what would finishing it with joy require of you now?
  21. Acts 21 — Let the Lord's will be done. Paul's friends loved him and still had to surrender him to God's will. Put off pleading against a known will of God, even out of love; put on praying, "The Lord's will be done." Is there someone or something you love that you need to place, finally, in the Lord's hands?
  22. Acts 22 — Tell your story plainly. Paul's testimony was simply what he was, what Jesus did, and where Jesus sent him. Put off waiting until your story sounds impressive; put on telling it plain. If you had two quiet minutes, what is your story — in those three parts?
  23. Acts 23 — Hear Him in the night. The Lord stood by Paul the night after his worst day. Put off believing a bad day is the end of the story; put on holding His promise in the dark. Where do you need to hear "take courage" from Him tonight — and what promise of His can you hold?
  24. Acts 24 — Do not wait for a convenient time. Felix trembled and then waited for a more convenient time. Put off scheduling obedience for later; put on obeying now. Is there an obedience you keep scheduling for later — and what is "later" costing you?
  25. Acts 25 — Trust the King behind the courts. God carried His promise through courts that kept failing Paul. Put off despair when human means fail; put on trusting the God who moves through setbacks. Where have you seen God move your life forward through means that looked like setbacks?
  26. Acts 26 — Wish them what you have. Paul wished every hearer would become what he was — except the chains. Put off keeping your walk with Christ private; put on wishing it, out loud, for someone you love. If you could wish your own walk with Christ on someone, what would you wish for them first?
  27. Acts 27 — Believe God in the storm. Paul believed God's word while the ship was still breaking apart. Put off waiting for calm weather before you trust; put on believing before the outcome arrives. What has God spoken that you need to go on believing before the outcome arrives?
  28. Acts 28 — Find your Rome. Acts ends with the gospel unhindered in the heart of the empire. Put off thinking the story is finished or that your part is too small; put on welcoming all who come to your door with the word. Where is your Rome — the place you most want to see the word of Christ reach — and what is your part in carrying it there?
