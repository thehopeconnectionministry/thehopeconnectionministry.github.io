Acts 8 — Scattered Seed: Samaria, Simon, and an Ethiopian Rejoicing

Jesus in Acts — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Acts
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Jesus is the seed that persecution scatters — and it grows everywhere it lands. The risen Christ receives Samaritans, rebukes a heart that would buy Him, and sends an Ethiopian official home rejoicing with Isaiah fulfilled in Jesus. Acts 8 is the second panel of the Acts 1:8 map opening: the gospel crosses into Samaria, and then, on a desert road, it quietly starts toward the ends of the earth.

Commentary

Scattered, and preaching as they went (8:1–4). Saul approves of Stephen's execution, and a great persecution arises against the church in Jerusalem. The believers are all scattered throughout Judea and Samaria — except the apostles, who stay in the city. Devout men bury Stephen and make great lamentation over him, and Saul ravages the church, entering house after house, dragging off men and women to prison. It looks like defeat. Luke shows us it is distribution. "Now those who were scattered went about preaching the word." The fire meant to stamp out the church spreads it. What men meant as scattering, God used as sowing.

Philip in Samaria (8:5–13). Philip — another of the seven table-servants — goes down to Samaria and proclaims Christ there. Samaria: the despised half-way people, the old enemy cousins. And the crowds with one accord pay attention to what Philip says, unclean spirits come out, many paralyzed and lame are healed, "so there was much joy in that city." Joy in Samaria, because of Jesus. But the city has an old attraction: Simon the magician, who has amazed the people for years and accepted their verdict — "This man is the power of God." Simon himself believes Philip's preaching and is baptized, and follows him, amazed. The scene is hopeful on the surface. Luke is about to show us what is underneath.

The Spirit given, and Simon rebuked (8:14–25). The apostles in Jerusalem hear that Samaria has received the word of God and send Peter and John, who pray for the Samaritans to receive the Holy Spirit, lay hands on them, and they receive Him. The Jerusalem laying-on of hands matters: Samaria is not a rival church; it is the same church, one body, sealed by the apostles' own hands. But Simon sees the Spirit given and offers money: "Give me this power also." Peter's rebuke is among the severest in the New Testament: "May your silver perish with you, because you thought you could obtain the gift of God with money!" A gift of God cannot be purchased — that is the bad news for every heart that wants God's power on its own terms, for its own name. "Your heart is not right before God. Repent, therefore, of this wickedness of yours, and pray to the Lord that, if possible, the intent of your heart may be forgiven you." Even the rebuke carries a door: repent, pray. Simon's answer — "Pray for me to the Lord" — is fear, at least, and Luke leaves him there, in the hands of a merciful God and an honest warning. Peter and John preach in many Samaritan villages on their way home: the mission to Samaria is not a raid but a homecoming.

Philip and the Ethiopian (8:26–40). An angel sends Philip south to the desert road from Jerusalem to Gaza — away from the crowds, for the sake of one man. A eunuch from Ethiopia, a court official of Candace the queen, in charge of her whole treasury, has come to Jerusalem to worship and is riding home, reading Isaiah aloud in his chariot. The Spirit says, "Go over and join this chariot." Philip runs — a grown man running beside a chariot because one stranger is reading Scripture — and hears him reading Isaiah 53: "Like a sheep he was led to the slaughter... in his humiliation justice was denied him." "Do you understand what you are reading?" "How can I, unless someone guides me?" And beginning from this Scripture, Philip tells him the good news about Jesus. The passage the man cannot understand is the passage about the suffering Servant — and the whole sermon is Jesus. They come to water, and the eunuch's question is pure joy: "See, here is water! What prevents me from being baptized?" He is baptized, and goes on his way rejoicing, and the gospel is on its way to Africa. Philip is carried away and found at Azotus, preaching through every town on his way to Caesarea. One man, one chariot, one chapter of Isaiah — and a continent begins to turn.

Key verses

"Now those who were scattered went about preaching the word." — Acts 8:4 (ESV)

"Then Philip opened his mouth, and beginning with this Scripture he told him the good news about Jesus." — Acts 8:35 (ESV)

"And when they came up out of the water, the Spirit of the Lord carried Philip away, and the eunuch saw him no more, and went on his way rejoicing." — Acts 8:39 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Acts 8 is Simon's heart: religion that wants God's power as a possession, a technique, an addition to its own fame. That heart is not right before God, and money, talent, and reputation cannot make it right. But the good news is the rest of the chapter: persecution scatters the seed and it grows; Samaritans receive the word with joy; and on a desert road a foreign official reading a hard passage hears that it is all about Jesus — and goes home rejoicing. Nobody is too far out, too far off, or too alone on a desert road for the risen Christ to send someone running beside their chariot.

A note from the Reformers. Spurgeon loved Philip here: the preacher sent away from a revival to one soul. He reminded his students that God counts congregations differently than we do — one Ethiopian in a chariot was worth an evangelist's whole journey. No one you are sent to is a small assignment.

Reflection question: Philip started where the Ethiopian was reading and preached Jesus from there. Whose questions could you simply sit down beside this week — starting where they are, with the passage or the pain already in front of them?
