Acts 2 — Pentecost: The Spirit Poured Out and Three Thousand Saved

Jesus in Acts — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Acts
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Jesus is not on the stage in Acts 2, and He is the whole sermon. The exalted Christ pours out the Spirit He promised, and the crucified and risen Lord is preached as a gift — forgiveness for the very people who crucified Him. Peter's message is all Christ: God raised Him, God exalted Him, God made Him both Lord and Christ. Pentecost is not the birthday of a human movement. It is the coronation gift of a reigning King.

Commentary

The sound, the fire, the languages (2:1–13). When the day of Pentecost arrives, they are all together in one place. Pentēkostē means "the fiftieth day" — the harvest feast fifty days after Passover, when Israel brought God the firstfruits of the wheat. It is a fitting day for the first harvest of the church. Suddenly there comes from heaven a sound like a mighty rushing wind, and it fills the entire house. Divided tongues as of fire rest on each one of them, and they are all filled with the Holy Spirit and begin to speak in other tongues as the Spirit gives them utterance. Jerusalem is full of devout Jews from every nation under heaven, and each hears the mighty works of God declared in his own language. Some are amazed and perplexed. Some mock: "They are filled with new wine." The Spirit's arrival is never tame, and it is never universally applauded. But notice what the languages are for: not display, but proclamation. The first thing the Spirit does is make Christ understood across every barrier of tongue and nation.

Peter's sermon: the law (2:14–23). Peter stands with the eleven and preaches — Peter, who weeks earlier could not confess Christ to a servant girl. His sermon is law before it is gospel. These men are not drunk; it is only nine in the morning. This is what Joel prophesied: God pouring out His Spirit in the last days, and "everyone who calls upon the name of the Lord shall be saved." Then Peter preaches Jesus of Nazareth, a man attested by God with mighty works, "delivered up according to the definite plan and foreknowledge of God" — and then the sentence that cuts:

"This Jesus, delivered up according to the definite plan and foreknowledge of God, you crucified and killed by the hands of lawless men." — Acts 2:23 (ESV)

Both truths stand together and neither is softened: God's definite plan, and their guilty hands. Peter does not flatter the crowd, and he does not excuse them. That is honest preaching, and it is kindness — a wound that is never named is never healed.

Peter's sermon: the gospel (2:24–36). But the sermon does not end at the cross. "God raised him up, loosing the pangs of death, because it was not possible for him to be held by it." Peter proves it from Scripture — David foresaw it in Psalm 16, and David's tomb is still occupied while Christ's is empty. Then Psalm 110: the Lord said to my Lord, sit at My right hand. This Jesus God raised up, and of that we all are witnesses. Being exalted, He has poured out this Spirit that you yourselves are seeing and hearing. Then the conclusion, preached to the very people who killed Him:

"Let all the house of Israel therefore know for certain that God has made him both Lord and Christ, this Jesus whom you crucified." — Acts 2:36 (ESV)

Cut to the heart (2:37–41). Now when they hear this, they are cut to the heart, and say, "Brothers, what shall we do?" That question is the law finishing its work. And Peter's answer is the gospel opening its arms:

"And Peter said to them, 'Repent and be baptized every one of you in the name of Jesus Christ for the forgiveness of your sins, and you will receive the gift of the Holy Spirit. For the promise is for you and for your children and for all who are far off, everyone whom the Lord our God calls to himself.'" — Acts 2:38–39 (ESV)

The very people who crucified Him are offered forgiveness in His name. That is grace with no small print. About three thousand souls receive the word and are baptized that day — the firstfruits of a harvest still being gathered.

The life of the first church (2:42–47). And they devote themselves to the apostles' teaching and the fellowship, to the breaking of bread and the prayers. Awe comes upon every soul. They share their goods, sell possessions for anyone in need, attend the temple together, break bread in their homes with glad and generous hearts, praise God, and have favor with all the people. "And the Lord added to their number day by day those who were being saved." Note who adds: the Lord. The church's part is devotion; the increase is His.

Key verses

"But this is what was uttered through the prophet Joel: 'And in the last days it shall be, God declares, that I will pour out my Spirit on all flesh.'" — Acts 2:16–17 (ESV)

"God raised him up, loosing the pangs of death, because it was not possible for him to be held by it." — Acts 2:24 (ESV)

"For the promise is for you and for your children and for all who are far off, everyone whom the Lord our God calls to himself." — Acts 2:39 (ESV)

"And they devoted themselves to the apostles' teaching and the fellowship, to the breaking of bread and the prayers." — Acts 2:42 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Acts 2 is Peter's charge, and it lands on all of us: our sin put Jesus on the cross. We cannot hear Pentecost honestly and walk away innocent. But the good news thunders back in the same sermon: this same Jesus God raised up, exalted, and made both Lord and Christ — and the promise of forgiveness and the Spirit is "for you and for your children and for all who are far off." The far-off ones are still coming home, and the Lord is still adding to His number day by day.

A note from the Reformers. Luther pointed to this sermon as the pattern of all true preaching: the law that cuts to the heart, and then the gospel that binds it up — preached to the worst sinners in the room as a promise, not a bargain. Cut first, then comforted: that order is mercy.

Reflection question: Peter's promise reaches "all who are far off." Who in your life seems far off from God right now — and will you start praying for them this week as someone the promise was spoken for?
