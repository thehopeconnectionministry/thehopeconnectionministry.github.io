Acts 20 — The Whole Counsel: Farewell to the Ephesian Elders

Jesus in Acts — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Acts
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Jesus is the Lord Jesus who gave Paul his ministry — testifying to the gospel of the grace of God — and whose flock was purchased with His own blood. In Acts 20 Paul leaves the flock to the word of God's grace: the risen Christ keeps building His church through the whole counsel, faithfully preached, tearfully guarded, and fully sufficient. The chapter is a shepherd's charge, and its center is not Paul. It is the grace of God and the blood of Christ.

Commentary

Through Macedonia and Greece, and a boy raised (20:1–12). After the Ephesus uproar ceases, Paul sends for the disciples, encourages them, and departs for Macedonia, traveling through the regions where he planted churches, "giving them much encouragement." Ministry on the move in Acts is largely this: going back, strengthening, encouraging. He spends three months in Greece, sails with a team of coworkers from across the churches — Luke lists them like a roll call of the nations — and comes to Troas. There, on the first day of the week, the church gathers to break bread, and Paul talks with them, prolonging his speech until midnight, intending to depart in the morning. A young man named Eutychus, sitting at the window, sinks into a deep sleep as Paul talks on and on, falls from the third story, and is taken up dead. Paul goes down, bends over him, takes him in his arms, and says, "Do not be alarmed, for his life is in him." They break bread, eat, and Paul converses until daybreak — and they bring the youth away alive, "and they were not a little comforted." Even the long sermon in Acts ends in mercy: the boy restored, the church comforted, the meal shared at dawn.

Hurrying past Ephesus (20:13–16). Paul travels by land to Assos while the others sail — Luke leaves us a glimpse of an apostle walking alone, likely praying and thinking through what is ahead. He rejoins the ship, sails past Ephesus deliberately, "so that he might not have to spend time in Asia, for he was hastening to be at Jerusalem, if possible, on the day of Pentecost." The man who spent years in Ephesus now sails past it, because Jerusalem — and the suffering the Spirit has been testifying awaits him there — is pulling him forward. But he cannot pass the Ephesian elders entirely. From Miletus he sends to Ephesus and calls the elders of the church to come to him.

The Miletus charge: the pattern of ministry (20:17–24). What follows is the only speech in Acts addressed to Christians, and it is soaked in tears. Paul first recalls his manner among them: serving the Lord with all humility and with tears, holding nothing back that was profitable, teaching publicly and from house to house, testifying both to Jews and to Greeks of repentance toward God and of faith in our Lord Jesus Christ. That is his whole message in one line — repentance toward God, faith in Christ — the law and the gospel, to every audience, without adjustment. Now he is going to Jerusalem, constrained by the Spirit, not knowing what will happen except that the Holy Spirit testifies in every city that imprisonment and afflictions await him. Then the verse that defines his life:

"But I do not account my life of any value nor as precious to myself, if only I may finish my course and the ministry that I received from the Lord Jesus, to testify to the gospel of the grace of God." — Acts 20:24 (ESV)

The charge: guard the flock, preach the whole counsel (20:25–31). Paul knows he will see their faces no more, and he testifies that he is innocent of the blood of all — because he did not shrink from declaring the whole counsel of God. The Greek word is boulē — counsel, purpose, the settled plan of God. Paul declared "the whole counsel of God" — not the parts that went down easy. God's purpose is a whole, and His people need all of it. Then the charge to the elders: "Pay careful attention to yourselves and to all the flock, in which the Holy Spirit has made you overseers, to care for the church of God, which he obtained with his own blood." Overseers made by the Holy Spirit, guarding a flock priced at the blood of God — shepherding is not a hobby in this chapter; it is a holy trust. And the warning is the bad news every church must hear: "I know that after my departure fierce wolves will come in among you, not sparing the flock; and from among your own selves will arise men speaking twisted things, to draw away the disciples after them. Therefore be alert." Wolves from outside, and twisted teaching from inside — shepherds must watch their own hearts first.

Commended to God and the word of His grace (20:32–38). But Paul does not leave them with the warning. He leaves them with the word:

"And now I commend you to God and to the word of his grace, which is able to build you up and to give you the inheritance among all those who are sanctified." — Acts 20:32 (ESV)

He has coveted no one's silver or gold; his own hands ministered to his necessities; "in all things I have shown you that by working hard in this way we must help the weak and remember the words of the Lord Jesus, how he himself said, 'It is more blessed to give than to receive.'" Then he kneels down and prays with them all. And there is much weeping on the part of all; they embrace Paul and kiss him, grieved most of all because he said they would not see his face again. And they accompany him to the ship. The whole counsel, faithfully preached, is enough to guard the flock when the preacher is gone — and the tears on the beach say what the doctrine means in flesh and blood.

Key verses

"But I do not account my life of any value nor as precious to myself, if only I may finish my course and the ministry that I received from the Lord Jesus, to testify to the gospel of the grace of God." — Acts 20:24 (ESV)

"For I did not shrink from declaring to you the whole counsel of God." — Acts 20:27 (ESV)

"Pay careful attention to yourselves and to all the flock, in which the Holy Spirit has made you overseers, to care for the church of God, which he obtained with his own blood." — Acts 20:28 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Acts 20 is Paul's warning, and every church must hear it: fierce wolves will come in among the flock, and even from among the elders themselves men will arise speaking twisted things. No congregation is exempt, and shepherds must watch their own hearts first. But the good news is Paul's commendation: he leaves the flock not to their own cleverness but "to God and to the word of his grace, which is able to build you up." The flock was obtained with Christ's own blood, and the whole counsel, faithfully preached, remains fully sufficient to guard it when the preacher is gone.

A note from the Reformers. Calvin held Acts 20:32 before ministers as their commissioning and their comfort: God and the word of His grace — not our gifts, not our presence — build up the church. A ministry that commends people to the Word can afford to leave; a ministry that commends people to itself never can.

Reflection question: Paul counted finishing his race above holding his life dear. What is the race God has given you — and what would finishing it with joy require of you now: a duty resumed, a whole counsel spoken, a person commended to the word of grace?
