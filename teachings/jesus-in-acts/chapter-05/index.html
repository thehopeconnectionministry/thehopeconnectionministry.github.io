Acts 5 — The Holy Church: A Lie Exposed and Witnesses Freed

Jesus in Acts — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Acts
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Jesus is the Lord whose Spirit guards the holiness of His church and frees His witnesses. No prison, council, or lie can stop the word of the risen Lord. The chapter holds two things together that we keep trying to separate: God takes sin in His church seriously — and no power on earth can silence the gospel He has sent. Tenderness and holiness, boldness and purity, belong to the same Lord.

Commentary

Ananias and Sapphira (5:1–11). The generosity of Acts 4 had a shadow. A man named Ananias, with his wife Sapphira, sells a piece of property, keeps back part of the proceeds, and brings only a part and lays it at the apostles' feet — presenting it as the whole. Peter's word is surgical: "Ananias, why has Satan filled your heart to lie to the Holy Spirit and to keep back for yourself part of the proceeds of the land?" The sin was not keeping money; the land was his, and the price was his, as Peter himself says. The sin was the lie — wanting the reputation of generosity without the cost, performing devotion before the church while deceiving the Spirit. Ananias falls down and breathes his last. About three hours later Sapphira comes in, not knowing what has happened, and is given her own chance to tell the truth. She repeats the lie, and falls at Peter's feet and breathes her last. And great fear comes upon the whole church. This is a sobering chapter, and it is meant to be. The God who is gracious is also holy, and the young church learns at the start that He will not be used as a stage prop for anyone's image.

Signs, and a jailbreak (5:12–21a). Many signs and wonders are regularly done among the people by the hands of the apostles. Believers are added to the Lord in greater numbers, crowds gather from towns around Jerusalem bringing the sick, and Peter's shadow is hoped for as a shelter of healing. But the high priest and the Sadducees, filled with jealousy, arrest the apostles and put them in the public prison. During the night an angel of the Lord opens the prison doors, brings them out, and gives them their orders: "Go and stand in the temple and speak to the people all the words of this Life." At daybreak, they are found doing exactly that. God frees His witnesses not for their comfort but for their commission — out of the cell and straight back to the pulpit.

We must obey God rather than men (5:21b–32). The council sends for prisoners who are not there, hears the baffling report — the prison found locked, the guards standing, the cell empty — and then hears the apostles are in the temple teaching. Brought before the council, they are charged: "We strictly charged you not to teach in this name, yet here you have filled Jerusalem with your teaching, and you intend to bring this man's blood upon us." Then Peter and the apostles give the answer that becomes the church's marching orders in every conflict between God's command and man's:

"We must obey God rather than men. The God of our fathers raised Jesus, whom you killed by hanging him on a tree. God exalted him at his right hand as Leader and Savior, to give repentance to Israel and forgiveness of sins. And we are witnesses to these things, and so is the Holy Spirit, whom God has given to those who obey him." — Acts 5:29–32 (ESV)

Notice the gospel inside the defiance: the exalted Jesus is Leader and Savior precisely to give repentance and forgiveness. Even the men holding the whip are being preached a Savior who gives repentance to Israel.

Gamaliel's counsel (5:33–42). The council wants to kill them. Gamaliel, a respected teacher of the law and Paul's own former teacher, counsels caution: movements of men have risen before and come to nothing; "if this plan or this undertaking is of man, it will fail; but if it is of God, you will not be able to overthrow them. You might even be found opposing God!" The council takes his advice — and still beats the apostles and charges them not to speak in the name of Jesus. Then comes one of the most astonishing sentences in Acts: they leave the presence of the council "rejoicing that they were counted worthy to suffer dishonor for the name." And every day, in the temple and from house to house, they do not cease teaching and preaching that the Christ is Jesus. Beaten, threatened — and rejoicing, and back at it by morning. That is what the Spirit's boldness looks like with bruises on it.

Key verses

"We must obey God rather than men." — Acts 5:29 (ESV)

"God exalted him at his right hand as Leader and Savior, to give repentance to Israel and forgiveness of sins." — Acts 5:31 (ESV)

"Then they left the presence of the council, rejoicing that they were counted worthy to suffer dishonor for the name." — Acts 5:41 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Acts 5 is that God takes holiness in His church seriously: you cannot lie to the Holy Spirit and call it worship, and half-hearted devotion dressed up as the whole gift is a lie He sees through. But the good news runs on through the whole chapter: signs multiply, prisoners are freed at dawn, and the exalted Christ stands as Leader and Savior, giving repentance and forgiveness of sins even to the people persecuting His church. If this work is of God — and it is — no one can overthrow it.

A note from the Reformers. Luther's stand before emperors and councils drew its courage from this chapter's sentence — obey God rather than men. The Reformers did not treat Acts 5:29 as rebellion; they treated it as the church's quiet duty whenever man's command and God's Word collide.

Reflection question: Ananias wanted the appearance of devotion without the whole cost. Is there a place where you are holding back part of the price while presenting the whole gift — and will you bring that place into the light before God this week?
