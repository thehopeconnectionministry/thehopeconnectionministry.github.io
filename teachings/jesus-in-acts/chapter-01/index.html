Acts 1 — The Ascended Lord: The Promise, the Prayer, and the Replacement

Jesus in Acts — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Acts
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Jesus is risen, teaching, promising, and ascending — and He is in charge of everything that follows. The risen and ascended King pours out His Spirit and sends His witnesses from Jerusalem to the ends of the earth. The mission is His before it is ever ours. Luke's first readers needed to know it, and so do we: Acts is not mainly the story of what the apostles did for Jesus. It is the story of what Jesus kept doing, from heaven, through them.

Commentary

The former account and the risen Teacher (1:1–3). Luke opens by tying this book to his Gospel: "In the first book, O Theophilus, I have dealt with all that Jesus began to do and teach." Began is the load-bearing word. The Gospel was only the beginning. For forty days after His suffering, Jesus presented Himself alive by many proofs, appearing to the apostles and speaking about the kingdom of God. Forty days of teaching from a risen Lord — the church was not built on excitement or rumor. It was built on evidence, instruction, and a King who had conquered death and took time to prepare His people before He left them.

The promise of the Spirit (1:4–8). While staying with them, Jesus orders them not to depart from Jerusalem, but to wait for "the promise of the Father" — the Holy Spirit, with whom they will be baptized "not many days from now." Then comes the disciples' question, and it shows how much they still misunderstand: "Lord, will you at this time restore the kingdom to Israel?" They are still thinking of a small, national kingdom. Jesus does not scold them; He enlarges them. The times and seasons belong to the Father. Their business is not a calendar but a commission:

"But you will receive power when the Holy Spirit has come upon you, and you will be my witnesses in Jerusalem and in all Judea and Samaria, and to the end of the earth." — Acts 1:8 (ESV)

The Greek word for witness is martys — one who tells what he has seen and heard. It is the word that later gives us "martyr," because in Acts telling the truth about Jesus will cost some of His witnesses their lives. A witness is not a salesman. He does not invent his message or improve it. He just says what he saw. And notice the order of the promise: power first, witness second. Jesus does not send them out empty and tell them to try harder. He tells them to wait until they are clothed with power from on high.

The ascension (1:9–11). As they watch, Jesus is lifted up, and a cloud takes Him out of their sight. The ascension is not Jesus leaving the work; it is Jesus taking His throne. He has finished the work of redemption and now reigns, interceding for His people and directing His church. Two men in white robes stand by the gazing disciples with a gentle rebuke and a great promise: "This Jesus, who was taken up from you into heaven, will come in the same way as you saw him go into heaven." Do not stand staring at the sky. He is coming back the same way He left — visibly, bodily, gloriously. Until then, there is work to do on the ground.

Waiting in prayer (1:12–14). So they return to Jerusalem — about a Sabbath day's journey — and go up to the upper room. Luke names the eleven, and then gives us the church's first activity after the ascension: "All these with one accord were devoting themselves to prayer, together with the women and Mary the mother of Jesus, and his brothers." One accord. Devoting themselves. The promise was certain, and still they prayed — because prayer is not how we talk God into His promises; it is how He prepares us to receive them. And note who is in the room: Mary, His mother, praying like everyone else; and His brothers, who once thought Him out of His mind (Mark 3:21), now believers. The risen Christ gathers even His own family's doubters.

The replacement of Judas (1:15–26). Peter stands up among about a hundred and twenty believers and does something the church must learn to do: he interprets a tragedy by Scripture. Judas's betrayal and death were a horror, but not a surprise to God — the Psalms had spoken of it, and another must take his office. The qualifications matter: the replacement must be a man who accompanied them all the time the Lord Jesus went in and out among them, from John's baptism to the ascension — a witness to the resurrection. Two men are put forward, Joseph called Barsabbas and Matthias. They pray — "You, Lord, who know the hearts of all, show which one of these two you have chosen" — and cast lots, and the lot falls on Matthias. Some wonder at the method; the point is the posture. The church does not grab; it asks the Lord who knows hearts to choose, and receives His choice. The twelve are restored, the foundation is complete, and everything is ready. All that is missing is the wind.

Key verses

"But you will receive power when the Holy Spirit has come upon you, and you will be my witnesses in Jerusalem and in all Judea and Samaria, and to the end of the earth." — Acts 1:8 (ESV)

"And while they were gazing into heaven as he went, behold, two men stood by them in white robes, and said, 'Men of Galilee, why do you stand looking into heaven? This Jesus, who was taken up from you into heaven, will come in the same way as you saw him go into heaven.'" — Acts 1:10–11 (ESV)

"All these with one accord were devoting themselves to prayer, together with the women and Mary the mother of Jesus, and his brothers." — Acts 1:14 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Acts 1 is that the job Jesus gives His people is bigger than they are — witnesses to the end of the earth, and they have no power of their own to do any of it. Left to ourselves we would run, hide, or shrink the mission down to our own little kingdom, which is exactly what the disciples are still asking about. The good news is that Jesus does not send His people out empty. The Holy Spirit is the Father's promise, and the ascended Christ pours Him out on His waiting people. Even Judas's empty place is filled, because God restores what sin has emptied. The mission of God begins not with our strength but with His gift.

A note from the Reformers. Calvin loved this chapter's order: the promise first, then prayer, then power. The church's strength, he taught, is never in its numbers or its plans but in Christ's promise received on its knees — a word for every small congregation that has ever felt outmatched.

Reflection question: Jesus told His witnesses to wait before they worked. Where is He asking you to wait for His power instead of running ahead in your own — and what will you do this week to wait well: pray, stay, and watch?
