Acts 7 — Stephen: The Son of Man Standing and a Martyr Forgiving

Jesus in Acts — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Acts
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Jesus appears in Acts 7 at the end, standing. Stephen sees the risen Son of Man standing at the right hand of God — standing to receive His witness, standing as Stephen's advocate — and the church's first martyr dies forgiving his killers with his Lord's own prayer. And at the edge of the scene, holding the coats, stands the young man who will carry this gospel to the nations. Christ receives the dying, and Christ is already preparing the next witness.

Commentary

The defense: God was never housed (7:1–50). Stephen, the table-servant turned preacher, stands accused of speaking against the temple and the law, and his defense is a walk through Israel's whole story. The God of glory appeared to Abraham in Mesopotamia — before there was a land, a temple, or a nation. Joseph was sold into Egypt, and God was with him there. Moses met God at a burning bush in Midian, on holy ground far from any sanctuary, and the God who heard Israel's groaning in Egypt delivered them by a man they first rejected: "Who made you a ruler and a judge over us?" That rejected Moses became the pattern — the prophet God raises up, His people refuse, and God saves them through the refused one anyway. The tabernacle traveled through the wilderness. David found favor and asked to build a dwelling. Solomon built a house — and Stephen lets the prophet Isaiah have the last word on the whole charge:

"Yet the Most High does not dwell in houses made by hands, as the prophet says, 'Heaven is my throne, and the earth is my footstool. What kind of house will you build for me, says the Lord, or what is the place of my rest? Did not my hand make all these things?'" — Acts 7:48–50 (ESV)

Stephen's point builds slowly and lands hard: God was never contained in one place or one building. He met His people in Mesopotamia, in Egypt, in the wilderness, on the move. The temple was God's gift, never God's cage.

The turn (7:51–53). Then the sermon turns like a blade. "You stiff-necked people, uncircumcised in heart and ears, you always resist the Holy Spirit. As your fathers did, so do you." Stephen names the family pattern: the prophets persecuted, the Righteous One announced — and betrayed and murdered by the very men now trying his servant. "You who received the law as delivered by angels and did not keep it." That is the bad news, and it is our family history too: God's gifts received, God's messengers refused. The council's answer is not repentance but rage — they grind their teeth at him.

The vision and the stones (7:54–60). But Stephen, full of the Holy Spirit, gazes into heaven and sees the glory of God, and Jesus standing at the right hand of God. Everywhere else in Scripture the risen Christ is seated — His work finished, His reign established. Here He stands: rising, as it were, to receive His first martyr, and to stand as his advocate before the council that heaven overrules. "Behold, I see the heavens opened, and the Son of Man standing at the right hand of God." They cry out with a loud voice, stop their ears, and rush together upon him. They cast him out of the city and stone him. And the witnesses lay down their garments at the feet of a young man named Saul. And as they stone Stephen, he calls out with his Lord's own dying words — "Lord Jesus, receive my spirit" — and falling to his knees he cries out with a loud voice:

"Lord, do not hold this sin against them." — Acts 7:60 (ESV)

And when he had said this, he fell asleep. Luke's word is gentle and deliberate: not a defeat, a sleep — because the One standing at God's right hand will wake him.

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Acts 7 is Stephen's charge, and it searches every hearer: God's people have a long history of receiving His law and resisting His Spirit, of honoring His house and refusing His messengers. Left to ourselves, we repeat the pattern. But the good news is what heaven does while the stones fall: the Son of Man stands to receive His witness, the dying man is given his Master's own forgiving prayer, and death itself is renamed a sleep. And the young man holding the coats has heard a prayer that God is about to answer in the most unlikely way imaginable.

A note from the Reformers. The Reformers pointed suffering believers to Stephen again and again: here is how a Christian dies — eyes on the standing Christ, forgiveness on his lips, and not one word of despair. Calvin called Stephen's death a sermon the whole church must keep preaching to itself.

Reflection question: Stephen forgave his killers while the stones were falling. Who do you need to forgive — and what would it take for you to pray for them this week the way Stephen prayed: by name, out loud, meaning it?
