Acts 25 — I Appeal to Caesar: The Promise Moves Through a Courtroom

Jesus in Acts — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Acts
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Jesus is the Lord whose promise moves through a courtroom. Paul's appeal to Caesar becomes God's own transport, carrying His witness toward Rome exactly as the Lord said at midnight in the cell. In Acts 25 no sermon is preached and no miracle occurs — and the risen Christ is advancing His purpose with every procedural step, through governors who think they are deciding and a prisoner who knows Who is.

Commentary

Festus arrives, and the old hatred with him (25:1–5). Two years of Paul's life have passed in a Caesarean cell when Porcius Festus succeeds Felix. Within three days of arriving in the province, Festus goes up to Jerusalem — and the chief priests and principal men of the Jews lay out their case against Paul at once, urging as a favor that he summon Paul to Jerusalem. Luke tells us plainly what the favor means: "planning an ambush to kill him on the way." The hatred has not cooled in two years; it has simply waited for a new governor. Festus answers that Paul is kept at Caesarea and that he himself intends to go there shortly: let the accusers come down and accuse him there, "if there is anything wrong about the man." He stays among them not more than eight or ten days, goes down to Caesarea, and the next day takes his seat on the tribunal and orders Paul brought in. A new governor, a prompt hearing — and the same trap underneath.

The trial that cannot convict (25:6–8). The Jews from Jerusalem stand around Paul, bringing many and serious charges against him that they cannot prove. Paul's defense is three sentences long and covers every jurisdiction at once: "Neither against the law of the Jews, nor against the temple, nor against Caesar have I committed any offense." Two years in custody, and the prosecution still has no evidence — because there is none. Paul has broken no law: not Moses', not the temple's, not Rome's. His crime, all along, is a confession: that Jesus is risen.

The favor, and the appeal (25:9–12). Festus, wishing to do the Jews a favor — the same phrase Luke used of Felix; governors keep trading this prisoner for goodwill — asks Paul: "Do you wish to go up to Jerusalem and there be tried on these charges before me?" Paul knows what a Jerusalem trial means; there is an ambush folded inside the offer. He answers with the clarity of a man who has counted every cost: he stands at Caesar's tribunal, where he ought to be tried; he has done no wrong to the Jews, as Festus himself knows very well. "If then I am a wrongdoer and have committed anything for which I deserve to die, I do not seek to escape death. But if there is nothing to their charges against me, no one can give me up to them." Then the sentence that moves the book:

"I appeal to Caesar." — Acts 25:11 (ESV)

Festus confers with his council and answers: "To Caesar you have appealed; to Caesar you shall go." It sounds like a legal maneuver; Luke means us to see a promise moving. Back in the Jerusalem cell the Lord had said, "You must testify also in Rome." Now Festus himself speaks the sentence without knowing what he is doing. Human courts keep failing this innocent man — one governor delayed for bribes, another is ready to grant favors — and justice among men is a frail reed for God's people to lean on. But the King is driving, and the road to Rome runs through a courtroom.

Agrippa and Bernice arrive (25:13–22). After some days King Agrippa and Bernice arrive at Caesarea and pay their respects to Festus. Agrippa is the great-grandson of Herod the Great — the fourth Herod of the gospel story — and, unlike the Roman governors, he knows the Jewish prophets and customs. As they stay many days, Festus lays Paul's case before the king, with a governor's honesty breaking through: the accusers did not charge crimes, but points of dispute about their own religion "and about a certain Jesus, who was dead, but whom Paul asserted to be alive." There is the whole controversy of Acts, summarized by a pagan official in one clause: a certain Jesus, dead — asserted to be alive. Festus admits he was at a loss how to investigate, offered the Jerusalem transfer, and reports the appeal. Agrippa's interest is aroused: "I also would like to hear the man myself." "Tomorrow," says Festus, "you will hear him."

The problem of the charge sheet (25:23–27). So on the next day Agrippa and Bernice come with great pomp, enter the audience hall with the military tribunes and the prominent men of the city, and Paul is brought in at Festus's command. Festus opens with an embarrassment: here is the man the whole Jewish community petitioned about, crying out that he ought not to live any longer — "but I found that he had done nothing deserving death. And as he himself appealed to the Emperor, I decided to send him. But I have nothing definite to write to my lord about him." An innocent prisoner must be shipped to the Emperor, and the governor cannot compose the charge sheet, because there is no charge. "For it seems to me unreasonable, in sending a prisoner, not to indicate the charges against him." Festus's problem becomes Paul's platform: the hearing before Agrippa exists, humanly speaking, to draft a letter — and it will produce the fullest testimony Paul ever gives. God is setting a stage with Rome's own pomp.

Key verses

"I appeal to Caesar." — Acts 25:11 (ESV)

"They did not charge him with such crimes as I expected, but had certain points of dispute with him about their own religion and about a certain Jesus, who was dead, but whom Paul asserted to be alive." — Acts 25:18–19 (ESV)

"But I found that he had done nothing deserving death. And as he himself appealed to the Emperor, I decided to send him." — Acts 25:25 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Acts 25 is that justice among men is a frail reed: one governor has delayed for bribes, another offers a deadly transfer as a favor, and an innocent man spends years in custody on charges no one can prove. God's people cannot finally lean on human courts. But the good news is that the promise moves through the courtroom anyway. The Lord said Rome, and Rome it will be — carried there by the prisoner's own appeal and a governor's unwitting sentence: "To Caesar you shall go." God moves His people's lives forward through means that look like setbacks, and He has never once lost a case He intended to win.

A note from the Reformers. Calvin marveled at God's providence in this chapter: the appeal that looks like Paul's last legal resort is in fact the Lord's chosen vehicle, prepared two years in advance. Providence, he taught, does not merely rescue us from circumstances; it works through them, including the frustrating ones.

Reflection question: God carried His promise through courts that kept failing Paul. Where have you seen God move your life forward through means that looked like setbacks — and where do you need to trust Him to do it again, this week, instead of despairing at the delay?
