Acts 24 — A Hope Toward God: Paul Before Felix

Jesus in Acts — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Acts
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Jesus is the resurrection hope Paul confesses before a governor who trembles and delays. The risen Christ is the hope of the just and the coming Judge of the unjust — and in Acts 24 He stands, unmoved and unhurried, in a corrupt court where justice is for sale and decisions are postponed for two years. Paul's confession outlasts the governor who hears it.

Commentary

The prosecution: flattery and charges (24:1–9). After five days the high priest Ananias comes down to Caesarea with elders and a spokesman — Tertullus, a hired lawyer. Tertullus opens with greasy flattery of Felix, praising his reforms and the peace the nation enjoys through his providence — from a governor whose cruelty and corruption were notorious. Then the charges: Paul is a plague, an agitator among all the Jews throughout the world, a ringleader of the sect of the Nazarenes, who even tried to profane the temple. The accusers join in, affirming that all these things are so. Flattery for the judge, labels for the prisoner: it is the oldest courtroom combination in the world, and Luke lets us watch it without comment.

Paul's defense: a confession, not a defense (24:10–21). The governor motions to Paul to speak, and Paul answers with courtesy and facts. It is not more than twelve days since he went up to worship in Jerusalem. They did not find him disputing with anyone or stirring up a crowd, in the temple, the synagogues, or the city. The charges cannot be proved. Then Paul turns the defense into a confession — the heart of the chapter:

"But this I confess to you, that according to the Way, which they call a sect, I worship the God of our fathers, believing everything laid down by the Law and written in the Prophets, having a hope in God, which these men themselves accept, that there will be a resurrection of both the just and the unjust." — Acts 24:14–15 (ESV)

Paul is not a renegade from Israel's faith; he is its heir. The Way is not a departure from the Law and the Prophets but their fulfillment, and its center is a hope: the resurrection of the dead, just and unjust alike. "So I always take pains to have a clear conscience toward both God and man." He explains his actual business in Jerusalem: he came to bring alms to his nation and to present offerings — the collection for the poor, years of Gentile generosity in his hands — and was found purified in the temple, without crowd or tumult. Some Jews from Asia were the real agitators, and they are not here to accuse him. Let these men state what wrongdoing they found. Or let it rest on the one cry he stood and made before the council: "It is with respect to the resurrection of the dead that I am on trial before you this day." One sentence, and it is the whole case: the resurrection is the charge, because the resurrection is the gospel.

Felix: trembling, and delaying (24:22–27). Felix, "having a rather accurate knowledge of the Way," puts them off and adjourns: he will decide when Lysias the tribune comes down. He orders Paul kept in custody but with liberty, and none of his friends prevented from attending to his needs. After some days Felix comes with his wife Drusilla, who is Jewish, and sends for Paul and hears him speak about faith in Christ Jesus. And Paul reasons about righteousness and self-control and the coming judgment — and Felix is alarmed. The governor who knows the Way trembles at the sermon. And then he does the most dangerous thing a convicted conscience can do: he schedules his surrender. "Go away for the present. When I get an opportunity I will summon you." The convenient time never comes. Meanwhile Felix hopes Paul will offer him money, so he sends for him often and converses with him — the gospel as a negotiation, a prisoner as a possible bribe. Two years pass. Then Felix is succeeded by Porcius Festus, and, desiring to do the Jews a favor, Felix leaves Paul in prison. Delayed obedience and purchased justice are both old sins, and both wear a governor's robe in this chapter. But the good news is the confession itself, standing unmoved in a corrupt court: the resurrection is coming, consciences can be kept clear before God and men, and no governor's calendar governs God's timetable.

Key verses

"And as he reasoned about righteousness and self-control and the coming judgment, Felix was alarmed and said, 'Go away for the present. When I get an opportunity I will summon you.'" — Acts 24:25 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Acts 24 wears a governor's robe: Felix knows the Way, trembles when Paul reasons about righteousness, self-control, and the coming judgment — and still sends him away to wait for a more convenient time, while hoping for a bribe. Two years of a good man's life pass in a cell because a convicted conscience kept postponing. But the good news is the confession standing unmoved in that corrupt court: there will be a resurrection of both the just and the unjust, the risen Christ is the hope of His people and the Judge of all, and no governor's calendar governs God's timetable.

A note from the Reformers. Spurgeon warned his hearers with Felix more than with almost any figure in Acts: almost persuaded is altogether lost. The trembling that postpones, he said, is not a step toward faith but the commonest road away from it — the convenient time is the devil's favorite appointment, and he always cancels it.

Reflection question: Felix trembled and then waited for a more convenient time. Is there an obedience you keep scheduling for later — and what is "later" costing you? Name it, and take the first step this week instead of the next convenient time.
