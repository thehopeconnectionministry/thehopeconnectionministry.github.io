Acts 27 — I Believe God: The Storm and the Shipwreck

Jesus in Acts — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Acts
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Jesus is present in Acts 27 by His word — the promise spoken by His angel in the night, which holds while everything else breaks. In a storm that takes cargo, tackle, ship, and finally hope itself, the word of the risen Christ holds: Paul believes God, and every soul on board comes safe to land. This chapter is the gospel acted out in weather: man cannot save himself, God has spoken, and believing His word in the dark is the way through.

Commentary

The voyage begins, and a warning ignored (27:1–12). It is decided that they should sail for Italy, and Paul and some other prisoners are delivered to a centurion named Julius, of the Augustan Cohort — a courteous officer who treats Paul kindly at Sidon, letting him go to his friends to be cared for. Luke is on board; the "we" returns. They sail under the lee of Cyprus and Cilicia, come with difficulty to Fair Havens in Crete, having lost much time — the voyage is already past the Fast, late in the dangerous season. Paul advises them: "Sirs, I perceive that the voyage will be with injury and much loss, not only of the cargo and the ship, but also of our lives." The prisoner is the only man aboard who sees clearly. But the centurion pays more attention to the pilot and the owner of the ship than to Paul, and the majority decides to sail on, hoping to reach Phoenix and winter there. Professional judgment outvotes the apostle. It will not be the last time expertise sails a ship — or a life — into a storm the word of God warned against.

The northeaster (27:13–20). When a moderate south wind blows, they think they have their opportunity — the storm arrives wearing fair weather's face. But soon a tempestuous wind, called the northeaster, strikes down from the land, and the ship is caught and cannot face the wind. They run under the lee of Cauda, barely secure the ship's boat, undergird the ship with ropes, lower the gear, and are driven along. The cargo goes overboard; on the third day they throw the ship's tackle overboard with their own hands. And Luke's bleak summary verse arrives: "When neither sun nor stars appeared for many days, and no small tempest lay on us, all hope that we would be saved was at last abandoned." That is the bad news, and many lives reach exactly that verse — no sun, no stars, no landmarks, hope given up. The ship is coming apart piece by piece, and there is nothing left to throw overboard except despair.

The angel's word, and Paul's faith (27:21–26). Since they have been without food for a long time, Paul stands up among them. He does not say "I told you so" as a taunt — he says it as a foundation: you should have listened to me. Yet now I urge you to take heart, "for there will be no loss of life among you, but only of the ship. For this very night there stood before me an angel of the God to whom I belong and whom I worship, and he said, 'Do not be afraid, Paul; you must stand before Caesar. And behold, God has granted you all those who sail with you.'" Note the ground of the promise: Paul must stand before Caesar — the midnight promise of chapter 23, still steering in chapter 27 — and the lives of 276 people are granted to Paul as a gift attached to that promise. Then Paul's faith, in the plainest sentence of his life:

"So take heart, men, for I have faith in God that it will be exactly as I have been told." — Acts 27:25 (ESV)

I believe God. Not "I believe in God" — though he does — but I believe God: I take His spoken word as fact while the ship is still breaking apart. They must run aground on some island. The promise includes the wreck; it simply rules out the loss.

Fourteen nights, and bread at dawn (27:27–38). On the fourteenth night in the Sea of Adria, the sailors sense land approaching; soundings show twenty fathoms, then fifteen. Fearing the rocks, they let down four anchors from the stern and pray for day to come. Some sailors try to escape in the ship's boat under pretense of laying out anchors; Paul says to the centurion and the soldiers, "Unless these men stay in the ship, you cannot be saved." The soldiers cut away the ropes of the boat and let it go. God's promise does not cancel man's responsibility; it runs through it — stay in the ship is the means, you will be saved is the end, and both are true at once. As day is about to dawn, Paul urges them all to take some food: fourteen days in continual suspense, without sustenance. "Therefore I urge you to take some food. For it will give you strength, for not a hair is to perish from the head of any of you." And having said this, he takes bread, gives thanks to God in the presence of all, breaks it, and begins to eat. The prisoner leads the ship's company in grace before meals, in a storm, at dawn — and they are all encouraged and eat themselves. Two hundred seventy-six souls, fed and steadied by one believer's thanksgiving.

The shipwreck, and not a life lost (27:39–44). At daylight they see a bay with a beach and decide to run the ship ashore. They cast off the anchors, loose the rudder ropes, hoist the foresail, and head for the beach. But the ship strikes a reef, runs aground: the bow sticks fast and does not move, and the stern is broken up by the surf. The soldiers' plan is to kill the prisoners, lest any swim away and escape — the old logic of empire. But the centurion, wishing to save Paul, keeps them from carrying out their plan. He orders those who can swim to jump overboard first and make for land, and the rest on planks or on pieces of the ship. "And so it was that all were brought safely to land." All. Exactly as spoken. God keeps His word through broken planks, and not a hair perishes.

Key verses

"Do not be afraid, Paul; you must stand before Caesar. And behold, God has granted you all those who sail with you." — Acts 27:24 (ESV)

"So take heart, men, for I have faith in God that it will be exactly as I have been told." — Acts 27:25 (ESV)

"And so it was that all were brought safely to land." — Acts 27:44 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Acts 27 is Luke's own summary: neither sun nor stars for many days, and all hope that they would be saved at last abandoned. Many lives reach exactly that verse, and pretending otherwise helps no one. But the good news stands in the middle of the storm in the person of a prisoner with an angel's word fresh in his ears: "I believe God, that it will be exactly as I have been told." The ship breaks up on the shore — and every one of the 276 souls comes safe to land, exactly as promised. God keeps His word through broken planks, and He keeps it still.

A note from the Reformers. Calvin held up Acts 27:25 as the very definition of faith: not a general optimism, but taking God at His particular word in the particular storm. Paul did not believe the weather would improve; he believed God had spoken — and that, Calvin said, is the only anchor that holds in a northeaster.

Reflection question: Paul believed God's word while the ship was still breaking apart. What has God spoken — in His Word, plainly — that you need to go on believing before the outcome arrives? Write it down, and one act of staying in the ship that it calls for this week.
