Acts 16 — The Lord Opened Her Heart: Philippi, Lydia, and a Jailer Saved

Jesus in Acts — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Acts
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Jesus is the Lord who opens hearts, opens prison doors, and opens whole households. The risen Christ saves whole homes in Philippi, starting at a riverside prayer meeting and finishing at midnight in a jailer's house. Nobody in this chapter finds Jesus by accident: a vision redirects the missionaries, the Lord opens a businesswoman's heart, and an earthquake opens a prison — and behind every opening stands the same Lord.

Commentary

Timothy, and the closed doors (16:1–8). Paul comes to Lystra and finds a young disciple named Timothy, the son of a Jewish mother who is a believer and a Greek father, well spoken of by the brothers. Paul takes him along — the aging apostle investing in the next generation, as Barnabas once invested in him. Then the journey takes a strange shape: they are forbidden by the Holy Spirit to speak the word in Asia; they attempt Bithynia, and the Spirit of Jesus does not allow them. Closed doors, twice, from the Spirit Himself. God's guidance in Acts includes His "no" — and His "no" is never wasted. It is steering.

The Macedonian call (16:9–10). A vision appears to Paul in the night: a man of Macedonia standing there, urging him, "Come over to Macedonia and help us." And when Paul has seen the vision, immediately "we" sought to go on into Macedonia — Luke joins the party here; watch the pronoun — "concluding that God had called us to preach the gospel to them." The gospel crosses from Asia into Europe not on a strategy but on a cry for help. Whole continents turn on obedience to a night vision and a concluded calling.

Lydia: the Lord opened her heart (16:11–15). They sail to Philippi, a Roman colony and the leading city of the district. On the Sabbath they go outside the gate to the riverside, where they suppose there is a place of prayer — there is no synagogue in Philippi — and sit down and speak to the women who have come together. One who hears them is Lydia, from Thyatira, a seller of purple goods, a worshiper of God. Luke writes the most important sentence in the chapter: "The Lord opened her heart to pay attention to what was said by Paul." Salvation is God's work at the root: Paul speaks, the Lord opens. She is baptized, and her household as well, and she urges them — "If you have judged me to be faithful to the Lord, come to my house and stay." And she prevails. Europe's first convert is a businesswoman, and her house becomes the church's base in Philippi. The letter Paul will later write to this church breathes the warmth that begins at this riverside.

The slave girl, and prison (16:16–24). Going to the place of prayer, they meet a slave girl with a spirit of divination, who brings her owners much gain by fortune-telling. She follows them for many days, crying, "These men are servants of the Most High God, who proclaim to you the way of salvation." Even true words from a demonic source are a trap, and Paul, greatly annoyed, turns and says to the spirit, "I command you in the name of Jesus Christ to come out of her." And it comes out that very hour. A girl is freed — and her owners' income is gone. They seize Paul and Silas, drag them into the marketplace before the rulers, and charge them with disturbing the city and advocating customs unlawful for Romans. The crowd joins the attack; the magistrates tear their garments, order them beaten with rods, inflict many blows, throw them into prison, and order the jailer to keep them safely. He puts them in the inner prison and fastens their feet in the stocks. Beaten for setting a slave free: that is the cost of the name in Philippi, and the night is not over.

Midnight songs and an open prison (16:25–34). About midnight Paul and Silas are praying and singing hymns to God — backs beaten, feet in stocks, in the dark — "and the prisoners were listening to them." Suddenly there is a great earthquake, the foundations shake, all the doors are opened, and everyone's bonds are unfastened. The jailer wakes, sees the doors open, draws his sword to kill himself — a Roman jailer who loses prisoners pays with his life — and Paul cries out, "Do not harm yourself, for we are all here." The jailer calls for lights, rushes in, trembling with fear falls down before Paul and Silas, brings them out, and asks the greatest question a man can ask: "Sirs, what must I do to be saved?" And the answer is the gospel in one sentence:

"And they said, 'Believe in the Lord Jesus, and you will be saved, you and your household.'" — Acts 16:31 (ESV)

They speak the word of the Lord to him and to all who are in his house. And the jailer takes them that same hour of the night and washes their wounds — the prisoners' wounds, washed by the jailer — and he is baptized at once, he and all his family. He brings them into his house, sets food before them, and rejoices along with his entire household that he has believed in God. By morning, a whole house is baptized and rejoicing. The Lord who opened Lydia's heart opens prison doors and a jailer's household, all in one city, all in one chapter.

Paul's citizenship (16:35–40). In the morning the magistrates send word to release the prisoners quietly. Paul declines the quiet exit: "They have beaten us publicly, uncondemned, men who are Roman citizens, and have thrown us into prison; and do they now throw us out secretly? No! Let them come themselves and take us out." The magistrates are afraid when they hear the word citizens — they have broken Roman law — and they come, apologize, and ask them to leave the city. Paul is not vindictive; he is protecting the young church. A public apology means the Philippian believers will not be treated as criminals' accomplices. So they go out of the prison, visit Lydia, see the brothers, encourage them, and depart. The church in Philippi is left standing in a house by a river, encouraged — and it will stand for centuries.

Key verses

"One who heard us was a woman named Lydia, from the city of Thyatira, a seller of purple goods, who was a worshiper of God. The Lord opened her heart to pay attention to what was said by Paul." — Acts 16:14 (ESV)

"About midnight Paul and Silas were praying and singing hymns to God, and the prisoners were listening to them." — Acts 16:25 (ESV)

"Believe in the Lord Jesus, and you will be saved, you and your household." — Acts 16:31 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Acts 16 arrives fast after the first joy: a slave girl is exploited by owners who profit from her bondage, and the men who free her are beaten and locked in the inner prison with their feet in stocks. The gospel disturbs profitable sins, and it pays for the disturbance. But the good news fills the chapter's night: the Lord opens hearts at riversides and households at midnight, prisoners sing, chains fall, and a jailer with his sword drawn hears the best news of his life — believe in the Lord Jesus, and you will be saved, you and your household. No heart, no prison, and no home is beyond the opening hand of the Lord.

A note from the Reformers. Spurgeon preached the jailer's question as the sinner's question in every age, and Paul's answer as the whole gospel in a sentence — no penance, no probation, no delay: believe, now, tonight, in the dark. Faith alone, receiving Christ alone, by grace alone.

Reflection question: Lydia's heart was opened by the Lord, and the jailer's by an earthquake at midnight. How has God gotten your attention — and are you listening now the way they did? And who in your household will you pray for by name this week with Acts 16:31 in your mouth?
