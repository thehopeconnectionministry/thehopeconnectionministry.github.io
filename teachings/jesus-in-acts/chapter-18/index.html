Acts 18 — Do Not Be Afraid: A Year and a Half in Corinth

Jesus in Acts — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Acts
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Jesus is the Lord who has many people in a city where His preacher cannot yet see one of them — and who keeps His trembling servant there a year and a half by a night promise. The risen Christ builds His church through tentmakers and teachers alike: a discouraged apostle, a displaced couple, a synagogue ruler, and a gifted Alexandrian who still needs to be taught more accurately. Nobody in this chapter is impressive. The Lord is.

Commentary

Tentmakers in Corinth (18:1–4). Paul leaves Athens and comes to Corinth alone — a port city famous for commerce and corruption. He finds a Jewish couple, Aquila and Priscilla, lately come from Italy because Claudius has commanded all the Jews to leave Rome. They are tentmakers, and Paul is a tentmaker by trade, so he stays with them and works. God provides for His servant through a shared trade and a shared roof — and through a refugee couple whose home will become one of the great teaching households of the early church. Every Sabbath Paul reasons in the synagogue, trying to persuade Jews and Greeks. Work with his hands on weekdays, the word on the Sabbath: that is the rhythm of Corinth, and there is no shame in it.

Opposition, and the turn to the Gentiles (18:5–8). When Silas and Timothy come down from Macedonia — bringing, among other things, support that frees Paul for the word — Paul is occupied with preaching, testifying to the Jews that the Christ was Jesus. And when they oppose and revile him, he shakes out his garments and says, "Your blood be on your own heads! I am innocent. From now on I will go to the Gentiles." He leaves the synagogue — and goes next door, to the house of Titius Justus, a worshiper of God, whose house adjoins the synagogue. The gospel moves one door over. And then the chapter's quiet thunderclap: Crispus, the ruler of the synagogue, believes in the Lord, together with his entire household. The synagogue's own ruler crosses the street, spiritually speaking, with his whole house. And many of the Corinthians, hearing Paul, believe and are baptized.

The night vision: I am with you (18:9–11). Paul would later admit he came to this hard city "in weakness and in fear and in much trembling" (1 Corinthians 2:3). Discouragement in God's servants is not a scandal in this chapter; it is the setting for one of the kindest promises in Acts:

"And the Lord said to Paul one night in a vision, 'Do not be afraid, but go on speaking and do not be silent, for I am with you, and no one will attack you to harm you, for I have many in this city who are my people.'" — Acts 18:9–10 (ESV)

I have many in this city who are my people — said of Corinth, before Paul can see them, while most of them are still in their sins. God counts His people before He gathers them. So Paul stays a year and six months, teaching the word of God among them. The promise does not remove the difficulty of Corinth; it gives the preacher a reason to remain in it.

Gallio's judgment seat (18:12–17). When Gallio becomes proconsul of Achaia, the opponents make a united attack and bring Paul before the tribunal: "This man is persuading people to worship God contrary to the law." Paul is about to open his mouth when Gallio cuts the case off: if it were a matter of wrongdoing he would listen, "but since it is a matter of questions about words and names and your own law, see to it yourselves. I refuse to be a judge of these things." And he drives them from the tribunal. The crowd seizes Sosthenes, the ruler of the synagogue, and beats him before the tribunal — and Gallio pays no attention to any of it. Rome's indifference, this time, serves the promise: no one attacks Paul to harm him in Corinth, exactly as the Lord said. The church learns that God's protection can arrive through a shrugging governor as surely as through an angel.

The road home, and Apollos taught (18:18–28). Paul stays many days longer, takes leave, and sails for Syria with Priscilla and Aquila, stopping at Ephesus — where he reasons in the synagogue, is asked to stay longer, declines, and leaves a promise: "I will return to you if God wills." He touches Caesarea, greets the church in Jerusalem, and comes down to Antioch: the second journey ends where the first began. Then the chapter closes with one of its loveliest scenes. Apollos, a native of Alexandria, comes to Ephesus — an eloquent man, competent in the Scriptures, instructed in the way of the Lord, fervent in spirit, teaching accurately the things concerning Jesus, though he knows only the baptism of John. He begins to speak boldly in the synagogue. Priscilla and Aquila hear him — and take him aside and explain to him the way of God more accurately. A gifted, fervent preacher, taught more accurately by a tentmaker couple, in private, without humiliation — and he receives it. He goes on to Achaia and greatly helps the believers, powerfully refuting opponents in public, "showing by the Scriptures that the Christ was Jesus." God builds His church with trembling apostles and teachable preachers, and with couples who open their home and their Bibles.

Key verses

"Do not be afraid, but go on speaking and do not be silent, for I am with you." — Acts 18:9–10 (ESV)

"And he stayed a year and six months, teaching the word of God among them." — Acts 18:11 (ESV)

"He began to speak boldly in the synagogue, but when Priscilla and Aquila heard him, they took him aside and explained to him the way of God more accurately." — Acts 18:26 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Acts 18 is that God's servants get discouraged: Paul arrives in Corinth alone, opposed, and by his own confession trembling, and the opposition that drives him out of the synagogue follows him to a tribunal. But the good news is the Lord's night word to His servant: "I am with you... for I have many in this city who are my people." God has people in hard cities before we can see a single one of them, and He keeps His workers standing in those cities by His presence, not by their strength. Corinth got a year and a half of the word because a trembling man believed a promise in the night.

A note from the Reformers. Spurgeon preached "I have many people in this city" to discouraged ministers and evangelists: the elect are not yet gathered, he said, but they are already God's — so preach on, because the Lord of the harvest knows His own addresses even when we do not.

Reflection question: God told Paul He had "many people in this city" before Paul could see any of them. Who might God already be preparing in your town, your workplace, or your family that you have not met — or not recognized — yet? Pray for them by place this week, and stay at your post.
