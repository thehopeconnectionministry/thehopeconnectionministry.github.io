Acts 12 — Chains and Kings: Peter Freed and the Word Growing

Jesus in Acts — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Acts
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Jesus is the risen Lord whom Herod cannot chain. He delivers His witness by an angel and receives His apostle by the sword, and He cannot be stopped either way. Acts 12 holds the church's hardest lesson and its greatest comfort in one chapter: God's deliverances are not formulas we control — and kings die, but the word of God grows and multiplies.

Commentary

James killed, Peter arrested (12:1–4). About that time Herod the king — Herod Agrippa I, grandson of Herod the Great — lays violent hands on some who belong to the church. He kills James the brother of John with the sword. James: one of the inner three, the first apostle to die. No angel comes for James. Luke records it in one flat sentence and moves on, and the honesty matters — God's deliverances are not formulas we control, and faithfulness is not measured by escape. When Herod sees that it pleases the crowds, he proceeds to arrest Peter also, during the Feast of Unleavened Bread, intending to bring him out after Passover for a public execution. Peter is delivered to four squads of soldiers to guard him — sixteen men, rotating watches, an apostle chained between two soldiers. Herod is taking no chances with a man whose church keeps walking out of locked prisons.

Earnest prayer (12:5). Luke's contrast is the chapter's engine:

"So Peter was kept in prison, but earnest prayer for him was made to God by the church." — Acts 12:5 (ESV)

The church has no army, no lobby, no leverage — so it prays, earnestly, through the night, in the house of Mary the mother of John Mark. But prayer was made. That little sentence is the church's whole military history in Acts. Kings have squads; the church has prayer, and prayer reaches the throne that rules kings.

The night of deliverance (12:6–11). On the very night Herod is about to bring him out, Peter is sleeping between two soldiers, bound with two chains, sentries before the door guarding the prison. Sleeping — on the night before his execution. That sleep is a sermon on trust all by itself. And behold, an angel of the Lord stands next to him, and a light shines in the cell. The angel strikes Peter on the side and wakes him: "Get up quickly." And the chains fall off his hands. Dress yourself, put on your sandals, wrap your cloak around you and follow me. Peter obeys, thinking he is seeing a vision — the deliverance is so quiet and complete it feels like a dream. They pass the first guard and the second, come to the iron gate leading into the city, and it opens for them of its own accord. They go out, walk one street, and the angel leaves him. Then Peter comes to himself: "Now I am sure that the Lord has sent his angel and rescued me from the hand of Herod and from all that the Jewish people were expecting."

Knocking at the prayer meeting's door (12:12–17). Peter goes to the house of Mary, where many are gathered and praying — the very people praying for his release. He knocks at the door of the gateway, and a servant girl named Rhoda comes to answer. Recognizing Peter's voice, in her joy she does not open the gate but runs in and reports that Peter is standing at the gate. The praying church's first response to its answered prayer is disbelief: "You are out of your mind." She keeps insisting. "It is his angel!" Peter keeps knocking — answered prayer can be persistent at the door while we argue theology inside. When they open and see him, they are amazed. He motions with his hand for silence, describes how the Lord brought him out of the prison, gives one instruction — "Tell these things to James and to the brothers" (James the Lord's brother, now leading in Jerusalem) — and departs to another place. At daybreak there is no small disturbance among the soldiers over what became of Peter, and Herod, failing to find him, examines the sentries and orders them executed. The machinery of empire grinds its own servants; the prisoner is free.

Herod's end, and the word growing (12:20–25). Herod goes down to Caesarea, and on an appointed day, arrayed in royal robes and seated on his throne, he delivers an oration to a delegation from Tyre and Sidon. The people shout, "The voice of a god, and not of a man!" Immediately an angel of the Lord strikes him down, because he did not give God the glory, and he is eaten by worms and breathes his last. The king who killed an apostle and accepted worship as a god is struck down in his robes. Then Luke sets his summary over the whole chapter like a banner:

"But the word of God increased and multiplied." — Acts 12:24 (ESV)

Kings die. The word doesn't. Barnabas and Saul return from Jerusalem with the relief, bringing John Mark with them — and chapter 13 is already loading.

Key verses

"So Peter was kept in prison, but earnest prayer for him was made to God by the church." — Acts 12:5 (ESV)

"Now I am sure that the Lord has sent his angel and rescued me from the hand of Herod and from all that the Jewish people were expecting." — Acts 12:11 (ESV)

"But the word of God increased and multiplied." — Acts 12:24 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Acts 12 is real and must not be skipped: James dies, and Peter walks free. God's people are not promised identical outcomes, and prayer is not a formula we control. Herods still rage, and some faithful people are not delivered in this life. But the good news is just as real: chains fall, gates open of their own accord, the praying church is God's chosen weapon against kings, and the chapter's last word belongs neither to Herod nor to death — "the word of God increased and multiplied." The risen Christ cannot be chained, and neither can His gospel.

A note from the Reformers. Calvin taught from this chapter that prayer is not the church's last resort but its appointed warfare — God ordains the prayers of His people as the very means by which He carries out His purposes. The church in Mary's house was not watching history; it was, on its knees, part of it.

Reflection question: James died and Peter was delivered, and the church trusted God in both. Can you trust Him with the prayers He answers differently than you asked — and will you keep praying earnestly anyway, this week, for someone in chains of one kind or another?
