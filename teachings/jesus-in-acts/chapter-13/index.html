Acts 13 — Sent from Antioch: Justified by Faith in Pisidian Antioch

Jesus in Acts — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Acts
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Jesus is the risen Son of David whom the Spirit sends preachers to proclaim — and in Paul's first recorded sermon, He is announced as the One through whom everyone who believes is justified from everything the law of Moses could never justify. Acts 13 is where the third panel of the map opens wide: the ends of the earth. And the message that travels is the message the Reformation would one day shake the world with.

Commentary

The sending church (13:1–3). In the church at Antioch there are prophets and teachers: Barnabas, Simeon called Niger, Lucius of Cyrene, Manaen, and Saul. While they are worshiping the Lord and fasting, the Holy Spirit says, "Set apart for me Barnabas and Saul for the work to which I have called them." The first missionary journey begins with worship and prayer, not a strategic plan — and it costs the sending church its two best men. After fasting and praying, they lay their hands on them and send them off. Missions in Acts is the church giving away its strength on its knees, at the Spirit's word.

Cyprus and the proconsul (13:4–12). They sail to Cyprus and preach in the synagogues of Salamis, with John Mark assisting. Crossing the island to Paphos, they meet a Jewish false prophet named Bar-Jesus — Elymas the magician — attached to the proconsul Sergius Paulus, "a man of intelligence," who summons Barnabas and Saul because he wants to hear the word of God. Elymas opposes them, seeking to turn the proconsul away from the faith. Then Saul — "who was also called Paul," Luke notes, using his Roman name as the Gentile mission opens — filled with the Holy Spirit, looks intently at Elymas and rebukes him: son of the devil, enemy of all righteousness, full of deceit — "will you not stop making crooked the straight paths of the Lord?" Mist and darkness fall on Elymas, and he goes about seeking people to lead him by the hand. The proconsul believes, astonished at the teaching of the Lord. The first named convert of the journey is a Roman governor, and what astonishes him is not the blindness but the teaching.

Paul's sermon at Pisidian Antioch (13:13–41). Paul and his companions sail to Perga — where John Mark leaves them and returns to Jerusalem; Luke records it without comment, and chapter 15 will show its cost — and come to Pisidian Antioch. On the Sabbath they sit in the synagogue, and after the reading of the Law and the Prophets, the rulers send the invitation every preacher hopes for: "Brothers, if you have any word of encouragement for the people, say it." Paul stands and preaches the first full sermon of his that Luke records. It walks Israel's story from the Exodus to David, and then straight to Jesus: of David's offspring God has brought to Israel a Savior, Jesus, as He promised. John baptized and pointed to Him. The rulers in Jerusalem did not recognize Him, fulfilled the prophets' voices by condemning Him, and asked Pilate to execute Him. They took Him down from the tree and laid Him in a tomb. "But God raised him from the dead." Then Paul lands the sentence:

"Let it be known to you therefore, brothers, that through this man forgiveness of sins is proclaimed to you, and by him everyone who believes is freed from everything from which you could not be freed by the law of Moses." — Acts 13:38–39 (ESV)

The Greek word is dikaioō — to justify, to declare righteous. It is a courtroom word. God the Judge pronounces the believer righteous through Jesus Christ — not because the believer has become righteous enough, but because Christ is. The verdict is received by faith, never earned as a wage. The bad news stands inside the sentence: Moses could never justify us; the law diagnoses, it cannot cure. The good news is that Jesus can, and does, freely, for everyone who believes. Paul closes with Habakkuk's warning — beware, lest the scoffers' word come true — because a gift this great refused becomes a judgment this heavy.

The word turns to the Gentiles (13:42–52). As they leave, the people beg that these things be told them the next Sabbath, and many Jews and devout converts follow Paul and Barnabas, who urge them to continue in the grace of God. The next Sabbath almost the whole city gathers to hear the word of the Lord. But the religious leaders, seeing the crowds, are filled with jealousy and begin to contradict and revile. Paul and Barnabas speak out boldly: it was necessary that the word be spoken first to you; since you thrust it aside, "we are turning to the Gentiles" — as the Lord commanded, in Isaiah's word: "I have made you a light for the Gentiles, that you may bring salvation to the ends of the earth." When the Gentiles hear this, they begin rejoicing and glorifying the word of the Lord, "and as many as were appointed to eternal life believed." The word spreads through the whole region. Opposition stirs up persecution, and Paul and Barnabas are driven out — and they shake off the dust from their feet, as Jesus taught, and come to Iconium. And the disciples they leave behind are "filled with joy and with the Holy Spirit." Driven out, and the church they planted is full of joy. That is the pattern of the whole journey.

Key verses

"And by him everyone who believes is freed from everything from which you could not be freed by the law of Moses." — Acts 13:39 (ESV)

"For so the Lord has commanded us, saying, 'I have made you a light for the Gentiles, that you may bring salvation to the ends of the earth.'" — Acts 13:47 (ESV)

"And when the Gentiles heard this, they began rejoicing and glorifying the word of the Lord, and as many as were appointed to eternal life believed." — Acts 13:48 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Acts 13 is Paul's own sentence: the law of Moses could not justify you from all things. Every homemade system of being right with God — law-keeping, reputation, religious effort — diagnoses the disease and cannot cure it. But the good news is the same sentence finished: through Jesus, forgiveness of sins is proclaimed, and by Him everyone who believes is justified — freely, fully, and forever. When some thrust the word aside, it turns to the outsiders, and they rejoice. It is still turning, and people are still rejoicing.

A note from the Reformers. This was Luther's verse before it was ever printed in his Bible studies: justification by faith, declared in a synagogue sermon by the apostle who would write it to the Romans. Luther said this truth — that God declares sinners righteous through Christ alone, received by faith alone — is the article by which the church stands or falls. Acts 13:39 is that article in seed form.

Reflection question: Paul says the law of Moses could not justify you from all things, but Jesus can. What are you still trying to use to make yourself right with God — and will you lay it down this week and receive justification as the gift it is?
