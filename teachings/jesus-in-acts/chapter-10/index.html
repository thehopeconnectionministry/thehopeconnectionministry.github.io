Acts 10 — The Table Crossed: Cornelius and the Gentile Pentecost

Jesus in Acts — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Acts
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Jesus is Lord of all — that is Peter's sermon and this chapter's heartbeat. God tears down the wall Peter would have kept: the risen Jesus pours His Spirit on a Roman household, and the gospel proves in a soldier's living room that it is for every nation. The crossing in Acts 10 is not Peter's idea and not Cornelius's achievement. God does the crossing Himself, from both directions at once.

Commentary

Cornelius, the God-fearing centurion (10:1–8). In Caesarea — Rome's headquarters city in Judea — there is a centurion of the Italian Cohort named Cornelius: devout, God-fearing, generous to the poor, constant in prayer. He is a good man by every human measure, and he still needs the gospel; Acts is honest about that. About the ninth hour of the day he sees an angel in a vision: "Your prayers and your alms have come up as a memorial before God." Send men to Joppa and bring Simon who is called Peter — who is lodging, the angel notes, with Simon a tanner, whose house is by the sea. Cornelius obeys at once and sends two servants and a devout soldier. Heaven is arranging a meeting between a Roman officer and a Jewish fisherman who would never, under ordinary rules, sit in the same room.

The sheet on the rooftop (10:9–16). The next day, as they are traveling and nearing the city, Peter goes up on the housetop about the sixth hour to pray. He is hungry, and while the meal is being prepared he falls into a trance and sees the heavens opened and something like a great sheet descending, containing all kinds of animals — clean and unclean together. A voice says, "Rise, Peter; kill and eat." Peter's answer is the bad news in all of us: "By no means, Lord; for I have never eaten anything that is common or unclean." The Greek word is koinos — common, ordinary, and so treated as defiled. It is Peter's protest on the rooftop: he has never eaten anything common or unclean. God's answer overturns the word itself: "What God has made clean, do not call common." This happens three times. Peter is perplexed — and the messengers are at the gate. God is rarely finished explaining before He asks us to move.

The messengers and the journey (10:17–33). The Spirit tells Peter to go with them without hesitation — literally, making no distinction — "for I have sent them." Peter invites these Gentiles in and gives them lodging, itself a small earthquake. The next day he goes to Caesarea, taking some brothers from Joppa as witnesses. Cornelius is waiting with his relatives and close friends gathered — a house full of Gentiles ready to hear. Peter enters — a Jew entering a Gentile home, crossing a line that custom treated as law — and explains himself plainly: "You yourselves know how unlawful it is for a Jew to associate with or to visit anyone of another nation, but God has shown me that I should not call any person common or unclean." Cornelius tells his vision, and ends with the sentence every preacher longs to hear: "Now therefore we are all here in the presence of God to hear all that you have been commanded by the Lord."

Peter's sermon: Lord of all (10:34–43). So Peter opens his mouth and preaches the sermon that changes the world:

"Truly I understand that God shows no partiality, but in every nation anyone who fears him and does what is right is acceptable to him." — Acts 10:34–35 (ESV)

He preaches the word God sent to Israel — good news of peace through Jesus Christ, "he is Lord of all." He tells the whole story: John's baptism, Jesus anointed with the Holy Spirit and with power, going about doing good and healing all who were oppressed by the devil, "for God was with him." The cross — "they put him to death by hanging him on a tree" — and the resurrection: God raised Him on the third day and made Him manifest to chosen witnesses, who ate and drank with Him after He rose. And the commission: preach to the people and testify that He is the One appointed by God to be Judge of the living and the dead. Then the sentence on which the chapter turns:

"To him all the prophets bear witness that everyone who believes in him receives forgiveness of sins through his name." — Acts 10:43 (ESV)

Everyone. In a Roman soldier's living room.

The Spirit falls on the Gentiles (10:44–48). While Peter is still saying these things, the Holy Spirit falls on all who hear the word. Heaven does not wait for our permission, or for the sermon to finish. The believers from among the circumcised who came with Peter are amazed, because the gift of the Holy Spirit is poured out even on the Gentiles — they hear them speaking in tongues and extolling God. Peter draws the only possible conclusion: "Can anyone withhold water for baptizing these people, who have received the Holy Spirit just as we have?" And he commands them to be baptized in the name of Jesus Christ. Then they ask him to remain for some days. The fisherman stays in the centurion's house, at the centurion's table. The wall is down, and nobody is going to rebuild it.

Key verses

"What God has made clean, do not call common." — Acts 10:15 (ESV)

"So Peter opened his mouth and said: 'Truly I understand that God shows no partiality.'" — Acts 10:34 (ESV)

"Everyone who believes in him receives forgiveness of sins through his name." — Acts 10:43 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Acts 10 is Peter's rooftop protest, and it lives in all of us: "By no means, Lord." We draw lines around the table that God has erased, and even apostles argue with grace when it heads toward the outsider. But the good news is that God does the crossing Himself. The risen Jesus is Lord of all, He died and rose, and everyone who believes in Him receives forgiveness of sins through His name — Roman officers, their relatives, their close friends, and every household we would have kept outside. Heaven does not wait for our permission.

A note from the Reformers. Calvin called this chapter the firstfruits of the Gentile church and a standing rebuke to every generation that tries to fence the gospel smaller than God has opened it. The sheet on the rooftop, he taught, still descends over our own respectable prejudices.

Reflection question: Peter's fence was respectable and religious, and God tore it down anyway. What line have you drawn — around a person, a family, a kind of people — that the gospel may be calling you to cross this week?
