Acts 19 — The Name Above Magic: Ephesus and the Mighty Word

Jesus in Acts — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Acts
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Jesus is the Lord whose name is no one's magic word. In Ephesus the name of Jesus proves to be the Lord's own name, not a formula to be borrowed — and where it is believed, idols lose their market and the word grows mighty. Acts 19 draws a bright line between using Jesus and trusting Jesus, and then shows what real trust costs and what it does to a city's economy of idols.

Commentary

Disciples who need the whole gospel (19:1–7). Paul returns to Ephesus as promised and finds some disciples — about twelve men — who have believed as far as they know, but they know only John's baptism and have not even heard that there is a Holy Spirit. Paul explains: John baptized with the baptism of repentance, telling the people to believe in the One who was to come after him — that is, Jesus. On hearing this, they are baptized in the name of the Lord Jesus, Paul lays hands on them, the Holy Spirit comes on them, and they speak in tongues and prophesy. It is a kind of Ephesian Pentecost, and its lesson is gentle and important: incomplete teaching is answered with fuller teaching, not contempt. Like Apollos before them, these men receive what they lack and move forward.

Two years in the school of Tyrannus (19:8–10). Paul enters the synagogue and for three months speaks boldly, reasoning and persuading about the kingdom of God. When some become stubborn and continue in unbelief, speaking evil of the Way before the congregation, he withdraws, taking the disciples with him, and reasons daily in the hall of Tyrannus. This continues for two years, "so that all the residents of Asia heard the word of the Lord, both Jews and Greeks." A rented lecture hall, daily teaching, two years — and a whole province hears the word. The strategy of Ephesus is not spectacle. It is steady, public, daily instruction in the word of the Lord, until the word outruns the teacher.

Extraordinary miracles, and the sons of Sceva (19:11–17). God is doing extraordinary miracles by the hands of Paul — even handkerchiefs and aprons carried from his body to the sick bring healing and drive out evil spirits. Luke calls them extraordinary himself; this is not the daily pattern but a special attestation in a city drunk on magic. Which is why the next scene matters so much. Some itinerant Jewish exorcists — the seven sons of Sceva, a Jewish chief priest — undertake to invoke the name of the Lord Jesus over those who have evil spirits, saying, "I adjure you by the Jesus whom Paul proclaims." It is religion as technique: the name used as a formula, at second hand. The evil spirit answers with terrifying clarity: "Jesus I know, and Paul I recognize, but who are you?" And the man leaps on them, masters all of them, and overpowers them, so that they flee out of the house naked and wounded. Fear falls on all the residents of Ephesus, Jews and Greeks alike, "and the name of the Lord Jesus was extolled." The name is not a tool in anyone's kit. It is the Lord's own name, and it will not be wielded by people who do not belong to Him.

The bonfire (19:18–20). Then comes the fruit of real faith, and it has a price tag. Many of those who are now believers come, confessing and divulging their practices. And a number of those who have practiced magic arts bring their books together and burn them in the sight of all. They count the value of them and find it comes to fifty thousand pieces of silver — a fortune, going up in smoke in public. Repentance with a price tag: not selling the old life, not storing it, burning it where everyone can see. Luke's summary of Ephesus stands over the ashes:

"So the word of the Lord continued to increase and prevail mightily." — Acts 19:20 (ESV)

The riot of Artemis (19:21–41). Paul resolves in the Spirit to go to Jerusalem — and beyond it, Rome: "After I have been there, I must also see Rome." The last act of the book is already in view. But first, Ephesus answers the bonfire with a riot. Demetrius, a silversmith who makes silver shrines of Artemis and brings no little business to the craftsmen, gathers the trade and makes the most honest speech any idol ever got: our prosperity depends on this temple; Paul has persuaded multitudes that gods made with hands are not gods; the temple of the great goddess may be "counted as nothing." The gospel is bad for the idol business, and the idol business knows it. The city fills with confusion, the theater fills with a mob shouting "Great is Artemis of the Ephesians!" for about two hours — most of them, Luke notes, not knowing why they came together. Paul wants to go in; the disciples and even some friendly city officials will not let him. Finally the town clerk quiets the crowd with plain sense: the courts are open, there are proconsuls, let charges be brought lawfully; Ephesus is in danger of being charged with rioting. And he dismisses the assembly. The riot dissolves the way it formed — noise — while the word of the Lord, quietly, has prevailed mightily.

Key verses

"So that all the residents of Asia heard the word of the Lord, both Jews and Greeks." — Acts 19:10 (ESV)

"And the name of the Lord Jesus was extolled." — Acts 19:17 (ESV)

"So the word of the Lord continued to increase and prevail mightily." — Acts 19:20 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Acts 19 is the sons of Sceva: religion as technique always ends in wounds. A heart that wants Jesus as a tool — His name without His lordship, His power without repentance — will find that heaven does not lend itself out. But the good news is what real faith does in the same city: believers confess openly, burn what bound them at real cost, and watch the word of the Lord increase and prevail mightily — until even the idol trade feels the loss. Where Jesus is believed, idols lose their market. They still do.

A note from the Reformers. Luther warned his hearers against treating Christ's name and sacraments as charms to be used rather than a Savior to be trusted — the Sceva error in German dress. Faith, he insisted, does not use Christ; it receives Him, and receiving Him changes what we are willing to burn.

Reflection question: The Ephesian believers burned what tied them to their old life, at real cost, in public. What would your clean, costly break with the old life look like — one thing kept back, kept private, kept "just in case" that you will put away for good this week?
