Acts 9 — The Persecutor Called: Grace on the Damascus Road

Jesus in Acts — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Acts
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Jesus speaks in Acts 9 — personally, by name, in the middle of a road. The risen Christ stops the church's fiercest enemy and makes him His chosen instrument. Grace does not find good people; it makes new ones. Every conversion since has the shape of this one: Christ takes the initiative, calls by name, blinds our old sight, and sends frightened, obedient people to lay hands on the last person they wanted to help.

Commentary

Breathing threats and murder (9:1–2). Saul is "still breathing threats and murder against the disciples of the Lord." The language is animal — breathing it in and out like air. He goes to the high priest and asks for letters to the synagogues at Damascus, so that any belonging to the Way — the earliest name for the Christian faith — men or women, he might bring bound to Jerusalem. Saul is sincere. Saul is zealous. Saul is utterly wrong, and his sincerity does not soften it: zeal without grace makes a persecutor. He is running hard, with paperwork, in the wrong direction. If we are honest, every one of us outside of Christ is running hard in some wrong direction.

The light and the voice (9:3–9). Now as he goes on his way, he approaches Damascus, and suddenly a light from heaven shines around him. Falling to the ground, he hears a voice: "Saul, Saul, why are you persecuting me?" Twice his name — the way God calls people He is about to change. And the question undoes Saul's whole world. He thought he was persecuting a sect. He learns he has been fighting a Person — and that every blow landed on the church landed on its Head. "I am Jesus, whom you are persecuting." Jesus does not wait for Saul to slow down, clean up, or come looking for Him. He stops him in the middle of the road. Saul rises from the ground, his eyes open and seeing nothing, and is led by the hand into Damascus — the hunter led like a child. For three days he is without sight, and neither eats nor drinks. Three days of darkness for the man who thought he saw everything clearly.

Ananias, the frightened disciple (9:10–19a). In Damascus there is a disciple named Ananias — an ordinary believer, not an apostle — and the Lord calls him in a vision. His assignment: go to the street called Straight, to the house of Judas, and lay hands on Saul of Tarsus, who is praying. Ananias argues, honestly and understandably: "Lord, I have heard from many about this man, how much evil he has done to your saints." Obedience in Acts is rarely the absence of fear; it is fear answered by a promise. And the promise is one of the great sentences of the book:

"But the Lord said to him, 'Go, for he is a chosen instrument of mine to carry my name before the Gentiles and kings and the children of Israel. For I will show him how much he must suffer for the sake of my name.'" — Acts 9:15–16 (ESV)

So Ananias goes, enters the house, and lays his hands on the persecutor — and his first words are "Brother Saul." Brother. Something like scales falls from Saul's eyes, he regains his sight, rises, and is baptized, and taking food, he is strengthened. The hands that came to bind are now baptized. The church's worst enemy is now its brother, and the one who welcomed him was a nobody whose name we know only because he obeyed while afraid.

The hunter preaches (9:19b–31). Immediately Saul proclaims Jesus in the synagogues of Damascus: "He is the Son of God." All who hear him are amazed — is this not the man who made havoc in Jerusalem? Saul increases in strength and confounds his opponents, proving Jesus is the Christ. Within the chapter, the plot turns: the Jews plot to kill him, his disciples lower him through the wall in a basket at night, and in Jerusalem the believers are all afraid of him until Barnabas — the son of encouragement — takes him to the apostles and tells them how the Lord appeared to him. Barnabas vouches for the man nobody trusts; that is what encouragers do. Saul preaches boldly in Jerusalem, disputes with the Hellenists, is sent away to Tarsus for his safety, and Luke closes the section: "So the church throughout all Judea and Galilee and Samaria had peace and was being built up. And walking in the fear of the Lord and in the comfort of the Holy Spirit, it multiplied."

Peter at Lydda and Joppa (9:32–43). The chapter ends with Peter, and two miracles that point forward. At Lydda he heals Aeneas, paralyzed and bedridden eight years: "Jesus Christ heals you; rise and make your bed" — and all the residents of Lydda and Sharon turn to the Lord. At Joppa, a disciple named Tabitha — Dorcas — full of good works and acts of charity, dies, and the widows stand by Peter weeping and showing the tunics she made. Peter kneels and prays, turns to the body, and says, "Tabitha, arise." She opens her eyes, and he gives her back to the saints and widows, alive. And Peter stays in Joppa many days — in the house of Simon, a tanner. Luke plants that detail quietly. A tanner works with dead animals and is ritually unclean, and Peter is lodging with him. The fence is already cracking. Chapter 10 will break it down.

Key verses

"And he asked, 'Who are you, Lord?' And he replied, 'I am Jesus, whom you are persecuting.'" — Acts 9:5 (ESV)

"Go, for he is a chosen instrument of mine to carry my name before the Gentiles and kings and the children of Israel." — Acts 9:15 (ESV)

"And immediately he proclaimed Jesus in the synagogues, saying, 'He is the Son of God.'" — Acts 9:20 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Acts 9 is written across Saul's life before the light: a man can be sincere, religious, zealous — and fighting Jesus Himself with every blow. Outside of Christ, every one of us is running hard in some wrong direction. But the good news is that Jesus does not wait for us to slow down or come looking for Him. He stops His enemies in the middle of the road, calls them by name twice, and makes them His chosen instruments. Grace did not find a good man on the Damascus road. It made a new one — and it still does.

A note from the Reformers. Luther read his own conversion in Saul's: a man not seeking Christ, seized by Christ. That is sola gratia at walking pace — salvation that begins in heaven's initiative, not man's decision, and therefore can reach people no one else would have picked.

Reflection question: Jesus stopped Saul mid-road and mid-sin. Where might He be stopping you — not to shame you, but to turn you toward the work He has for you? And is there an Ananias-assignment you are afraid of that you will obey anyway?
