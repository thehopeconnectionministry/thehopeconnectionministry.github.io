Acts 26 — Not Disobedient: Paul Before Agrippa

Jesus in Acts — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Acts
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Jesus is the risen Lord who opened Paul's eyes in order to open others' — from darkness to light, from the power of Satan to God, by faith in Christ. Before a king, Paul gives the fullest account of his conversion in Acts, and its commission is the whole gospel in one sentence. The Jesus of Acts 26 appoints servants and witnesses, promises to deliver them, and sends them to Jews and Gentiles alike with forgiveness of sins.

Commentary

The king gives permission (26:1–3). Agrippa says to Paul, "You have permission to speak for yourself." Then Paul stretches out his hand and makes his defense — and begins with gratitude, not grievance: he counts himself fortunate to stand before a king who is familiar with all the customs and controversies of the Jews. "Therefore I beg you to listen to me patiently." The prisoner instructs the court in courtesy. What follows is the third and fullest telling of Paul's conversion in Acts — Luke gives it the most room because Agrippa is the audience who can understand it best, and because this is the testimony's final, royal hearing.

What Paul was (26:4–11). Paul tells his story in the three parts every testimony needs. First, what he was: his manner of life from youth, known to all the Jews; the strictest party of the religion, a Pharisee; standing now on trial for his hope in the promise made by God to the fathers — "Why is it thought incredible by any of you that God raises the dead?" Then the darkness, unhidden: "I myself was convinced that I ought to do many things in opposing the name of Jesus of Nazareth. And I did so in Jerusalem. I not only locked up many of the saints in prison after receiving authority from the chief priests, but when they were put to death I cast my vote against them. And I punished them often in all the synagogues and tried to make them blaspheme, and in raging fury against them I persecuted them even to foreign cities." Raging fury. Paul does not edit his past into respectability. The worse the first act, the greater the grace of the second — and every hearer in the hall knows men like the man Paul was.

What Jesus did (26:12–18). In this purpose he journeyed to Damascus with the authority of the chief priests. At midday — brighter than the sun at its height — he saw a light from heaven, and heard the voice in Hebrew: "Saul, Saul, why are you persecuting me? It is hard for you to kick against the goads." Goads: the sharp sticks used to drive oxen, which injure only the animal that kicks. Paul's whole campaign against Christ was self-destruction. "Who are you, Lord?" "I am Jesus, whom you are persecuting. But rise and stand upon your feet, for I have appeared to you for this purpose, to appoint you as a servant and witness to the things in which you have seen me and to those in which I will appear to you, delivering you from your people and from the Gentiles — to whom I am sending you." And then the commission, the sentence Luke chose as this chapter's memory verse, the whole gospel in one breath:

"To open their eyes, so that they may turn from darkness to light and from the power of Satan to God, that they may receive forgiveness of sins and a place among those who are sanctified by faith in me." — Acts 26:18 (ESV)

Darkness to light. Satan's power to God. Forgiveness of sins. An inheritance among the sanctified — by faith in Christ. Every sermon Paul ever preached is a footnote to this sentence.

Not disobedient (26:19–23). "Therefore, O King Agrippa, I was not disobedient to the heavenly vision." That is Paul's whole defense of his life: a man met the risen Jesus and obeyed. He declared first to those in Damascus, then in Jerusalem and throughout Judea, and also to the Gentiles, "that they should repent and turn to God, performing deeds in keeping with their repentance." Repentance that bears fruit — not a feeling but a turned life. For this reason the Jews seized him in the temple and tried to kill him. "To this day I have had the help that comes from God" — the prisoner credits his survival entirely to heaven — "and so I stand here testifying both to small and great, saying nothing but what the prophets and Moses said would come to pass: that the Christ must suffer and that, by being the first to rise from the dead, he would proclaim light both to our people and to the Gentiles." Nothing new, nothing invented: a suffering Christ, a risen Christ, light to Jew and Gentile. Scripture said it; Paul preaches it; the hall must decide about it.

Almost persuaded (26:24–29). As Paul makes his defense, Festus says with a loud voice, "Paul, you are out of your mind; your great learning is driving you out of your mind." Paul answers with dignity: "I am not out of my mind, most excellent Festus, but I am speaking true and rational words." Then he turns to the king: the king knows about these things; none of this was done in a corner. "King Agrippa, do you believe the prophets? I know that you believe." And Agrippa's answer has echoed for two thousand years: "In a short time would you persuade me to be a Christian?" A man can hear everything and still step back with a smile. Paul's reply is the missionary heart of the whole book:

"Whether short or long, I would to God that not only you but also all who hear me this day might become such as I am — except for these chains." — Acts 26:29 (ESV)

Such as I am — a persecutor forgiven, a prisoner free inside, a man with light in his eyes — except for these chains. It is the kindest sentence ever spoken to a court that would not believe.

The verdict (26:30–32). The king rises, and the governor and Bernice and those who sat with them withdraw and talk among themselves: "This man is doing nothing to deserve death or imprisonment." And Agrippa says to Festus the sentence that seals the irony of the whole hearing: "This man could have been set free if he had not appealed to Caesar." Innocent by every court that hears him — and bound for Rome by his own appeal, which is to say, by the Lord's own midnight promise. The bad news is how royalty receives the gospel: Festus calls it madness, Agrippa calls it almost, and both walk out unbowed. The good news is Paul's prayer standing over the hall after they leave: every hearer, such as I am — except the chains. Some in that room's story we will never know this side of heaven. The invitation outlived the audience.

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Acts 26 is how the powerful receive the gospel: Festus interrupts — "You are out of your mind!" — and Agrippa, pressed kindly and knowing the prophets, steps back with a smile and an "almost." A man can hear everything, understand most of it, and still not bow. But the good news is the commission itself, spoken before the king: the risen Jesus opens eyes, turns sinners from darkness to light and from Satan's power to God, and gives forgiveness of sins and an inheritance to all who are sanctified by faith in Him. And Paul's wish still stands over every hearer: that all who hear might become such as he is — except for the chains.

A note from the Reformers. Spurgeon preached Agrippa's "almost" as one of Scripture's most solemn warnings: almost persuaded is not persuaded, and a king can lose heaven by a courtesy. Yet he preached Paul's answer just as often — the apostle's wish for his hearers is the heart every witness should carry into every conversation.

Reflection question: Paul wished every hearer would become what he was — except the chains. If you could wish your own walk with Christ on someone you love, what would you wish for them first — and will you tell them so, plainly, this week?
