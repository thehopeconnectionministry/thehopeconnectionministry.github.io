Acts 17 — The Unknown God Made Known: Thessalonica, Berea, and Athens

Jesus in Acts — A Chapter-by-Chapter Commentary

  • Teachings
  • Acts
  • Commentary

Jesus in this chapter

Jesus is the risen Man whom God has appointed to judge the world in righteousness — and whose resurrection is the proof. Paul proclaims the unknown God made known: the Creator who raised Jesus from the dead now calls every nation to repent and believe. In Thessalonica Jesus is preached from the Scriptures, in Berea He is tested by the Scriptures, and in Athens He is announced to people who have never heard His name. One Christ, three audiences, no shortcuts.

Commentary

Thessalonica: reasoning from the Scriptures (17:1–9). Paul and Silas come to Thessalonica, where there is a synagogue, and as was Paul's custom he goes in and on three Sabbath days reasons with them from the Scriptures, "explaining and proving that it was necessary for the Christ to suffer and to rise from the dead, and saying, 'This Jesus, whom I proclaim to you, is the Christ.'" Some are persuaded and join Paul and Silas — as do a great many devout Greeks and not a few of the leading women. But jealous opponents form a mob with rabble from the marketplace, set the city in an uproar, and attack the house of Jason, who has hosted the missionaries. Dragged before the city authorities, Jason faces the charge that has followed this gospel everywhere: "These men who have turned the world upside down have come here also... and they are all acting against the decrees of Caesar, saying that there is another king, Jesus." Turned the world upside down — the accusation is the compliment. There is another King, and His kingdom does turn the world's arrangements over. Jason posts bond, and Paul and Silas are sent away by night to Berea.

Berea: searching the Scriptures daily (17:10–15). The brothers in Berea receive the word with all eagerness — and then do the thing for which the church has honored them ever since:

"Now these Jews were more noble than those in Thessalonica; they received the word with all eagerness, examining the Scriptures daily to see if these things were so." — Acts 17:11 (ESV)

They test an apostle's preaching against the written Word — daily. That is sola scriptura in action, and Luke calls it nobility. Many of them believe, therefore — searching produces faith, not skepticism — along with Greek women of high standing and men. But when the Thessalonian opponents learn the word is being preached in Berea, they come to stir up the crowds there too. The brothers send Paul away to the sea, toward Athens, while Silas and Timothy remain. The persecutors can chase the preacher; they cannot chase the examined, believed Word out of Berea.

Athens: a spirit provoked (17:16–21). While Paul waits in Athens, his spirit is provoked within him as he sees that the city is full of idols. Provoked — not entertained, not impressed. Athens is the intellectual capital of the world, glittering with temples and ideas, and Paul sees a city of lost people bowing to stones. So he reasons in the synagogue with the Jews, and in the marketplace every day with those who happen to be there — philosophers included, Epicureans and Stoics, who call him a babbler and a preacher of foreign divinities, "because he was preaching Jesus and the resurrection." They bring him to the Areopagus — Mars Hill, the council's meeting place — saying, "May we know what this new teaching is that you are presenting?" Luke adds, dryly, that Athenians spend their time in nothing except telling or hearing something new. Novelty is Athens's religion. Paul is about to give them the oldest news in the world.

The Areopagus speech (17:22–31). Paul's speech is a model of meeting people where they are. He starts with their own altar: "I perceive that in every way you are very religious. For as I passed along and observed the objects of your worship, I found also an altar with this inscription: 'To the unknown god.'" Then the sentence the whole speech hangs on: "What therefore you worship as unknown, this I proclaim to you." He preaches the Creator: the God who made the world and everything in it, Lord of heaven and earth, who does not live in temples made by man, nor is served by human hands as though He needed anything, since He Himself gives to all mankind life and breath and everything. He made from one man every nation, having determined their times and boundaries, "that they should seek God, and perhaps feel their way toward him and find him. Yet he is actually not far from each one of us, for 'In him we live and move and have our being'" — quoting their own poets back to them. Since we are God's offspring, we ought not to think the divine is like gold or silver or stone. Then the call, without softening:

"The times of ignorance God overlooked, but now he commands all people everywhere to repent, because he has fixed a day on which he will judge the world in righteousness by a man whom he has appointed; and of this he has given assurance to all by raising him from the dead." — Acts 17:30–31 (ESV)

The bad news he will not soften: God has appointed a day of judgment, and He will judge the world in righteousness by the Man He has ordained. The good news he will not hide either: that Man has been raised from the dead, God commands all people everywhere to repent, and the Creator they do not know is not far from each one of us.

Mockery, delay — and faith (17:32–34). When they hear of the resurrection of the dead, some mock. Others say, "We will hear you again about this." Athens shrugs in two directions — ridicule and postponement. But some men join Paul and believe, among them Dionysius the Areopagite — a member of the council itself — and a woman named Damaris, and others with them. No mass revival in Athens, and Luke does not pretend otherwise. A few believers, named and real, in the hardest soil in Greece. The gospel is not measured by the crowd's reaction; it is measured by the truth of the resurrection and the few who bow to it.

Key verses

"Now these Jews were more noble than those in Thessalonica; they received the word with all eagerness, examining the Scriptures daily to see if these things were so." — Acts 17:11 (ESV)

"Yet he is actually not far from each one of us, for 'In him we live and move and have our being.'" — Acts 17:27–28 (ESV)

"Because he has fixed a day on which he will judge the world in righteousness by a man whom he has appointed; and of this he has given assurance to all by raising him from the dead." — Acts 17:31 (ESV)

The bad news and the good news

The bad news of Acts 17 is Paul's unsoftened word: God has fixed a day on which He will judge the world in righteousness, and idolatry — ancient or modern, marble or digital — is ignorance He will no longer overlook. But the good news stands in the same speech: the Judge has been raised from the dead as God's assurance to all, the Creator is not far from each one of us, and He now commands all people everywhere to repent — a command that is itself mercy, since the day has not yet come. Some mock, some delay — and some believe. The Bereans show us how: search the Scriptures daily, and let the Word decide.

A note from the Reformers. The Reformers cited Berea constantly, and Calvin stated the principle plainly: the Bereans were commended not for believing quickly but for testing apostolic preaching by Scripture — the right and duty of every ordinary Christian with an open Bible. No preacher, however gifted, stands above the examination of the Word.

Reflection question: The Bereans searched the Scriptures daily to test even an apostle's preaching. Do you test what you hear by the Word — and what would daily searching look like in your life: a time, a place, and a chapter a day, starting this week?
