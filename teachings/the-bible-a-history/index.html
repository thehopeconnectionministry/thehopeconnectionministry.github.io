The Bible — A History of the Book

How God Gave His Word, and How He Kept It

  • Teachings
  • Church History
  • Scripture

The Hope Connection — Lewis Fuller

1. The Book Unlike Any Other

There are older books than the Bible. There are bigger books. But there is no other book like this one, and there never will be.

Consider what it is. Sixty-six books, written by some forty different men over about fifteen hundred years — kings and fishermen, prophets and poets, a doctor, a tax collector, a shepherd, a prisoner — and yet it speaks with one voice, tells one story, and points to one Person. A shepherd in the wilderness and an apostle in a prison cell, separated by a thousand years, wrote as if they had sat in the same room. That is because, in the deepest sense, they had the same Author.

The Bible does not merely contain the Word of God. It is the Word of God. The apostle Paul tells us exactly where it came from:

"All Scripture is breathed out by God and profitable for teaching, for reproof, for correction, and for training in righteousness, that the man of God may be complete, equipped for every good work." — 2 Timothy 3:16–17 (ESV)

Breathed out by God. Theologians call this plenary verbal inspiration: plenary, because it covers all of Scripture, every book and every part; verbal, because it reaches down to the very words. God did not hand His writers a set of vague religious feelings and leave them to dress the feelings up as best they could. He breathed out the words themselves, working through the minds, memories, vocabularies, and personalities of the men He chose, so that what they wrote was exactly what He intended — and exactly theirs, and exactly His, at the same time. Peter says the same thing from the other side: "no prophecy of Scripture comes from someone's own interpretation. For no prophecy ever came by the will of man, but men spoke from God as they were carried along by the Holy Spirit" (2 Peter 1:20–21, ESV).

That is why this Book has outlived every empire that tried to burn it, every philosophy that tried to bury it, and every critic who announced its death. Kings have chained it and it has broken the chains. Men have died rather than surrender a single page of it. It has been translated more, printed more, read more, loved more, and hated more than any book in human history — and it stands today exactly where it has always stood: on the side of the God who breathed it out.

This is the story of how He gave it, and how He kept it. It is a story of scrolls and scribes, of caves and printing presses, of translators who were strangled and burned for their work, and of a God who watched over His Word through every century — because He had promised that He would.

2. How It Was Written: Scrolls, Scribes, and Three Languages

The Bible was not dropped from heaven bound in leather. God gave it the slow way, across centuries, through the ordinary tools of writing in the ancient world.

Moses and the first books. The story of the written Word begins with Moses, the man God raised up to lead Israel out of slavery in Egypt. Tradition and Scripture itself agree that the first five books — Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, and Deuteronomy, called the Pentateuch or the Torah — came through him. Jesus Himself spoke of "the Law of Moses" and quoted those books as God's Word. Moses wrote on the materials of his day: leather and papyrus scrolls, rolled on wooden rods, stored in the tabernacle beside the ark of the covenant, where the stone tablets of the Ten Commandments — written, Exodus says, by the finger of God — were kept inside the ark itself.

Prophets, poets, and kings. After Moses came the historians who recorded Israel's story, the poets who gave us the Psalms — David the shepherd-king chief among them — and the prophets: Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, Daniel, and the twelve shorter prophetic books. These men did not write as a hobby. "Thus says the LORD" rings through their pages hundreds of times. When Jeremiah's scroll was cut up and burned by a wicked king, God simply told Jeremiah to write it all again — and Jeremiah did, with more added besides (Jeremiah 36). The Word could be burned, but it could not be stopped.

Apostles and their companions. In the New Testament, the writing passed to the apostles of Jesus Christ and the men who walked with them. Matthew and John were apostles. Mark wrote down the preaching of Peter. Luke, a Gentile doctor, investigated everything carefully and wrote both a Gospel and the book of Acts. Paul, the converted persecutor, wrote thirteen letters to churches and men he loved. James and Jude were the Lord's own brothers. Peter wrote two letters. The writer of Hebrews — whose name God chose not to give us — wrote one of the most majestic books in the canon.

Ezra and the return of the Book. One more writer deserves his own mention, because he stands between the writing of the Old Testament and its keeping. Ezra was a priest and a scribe in the days after Israel's exile in Babylon — "a scribe skilled in the Law of Moses" (Ezra 7:6, ESV) — who led a company of exiles home to Jerusalem and did something that shaped everything after: he read the Book aloud to the whole assembled people, from early morning until midday, while the Levites moved among the crowd helping them understand the sense of what they heard (Nehemiah 8). It is the first great public Bible reading in history, and its pattern — the Word read, the Word explained, the people weeping and then feasting at what they heard — is the pattern of every faithful pulpit since. Jewish tradition credits Ezra and the men of the Great Assembly with gathering and ordering the Scriptures of Israel in their final form. Whether or not every detail of that tradition can be proved, the instinct behind it is sound: from the beginning, God raised up not only men to write His Word but men to gather it, guard it, and read it aloud to His people.

The materials. These men wrote on scrolls of papyrus — reeds from Egypt split, laid crosswise, pressed and dried into sheets — and on parchment, animal skins scraped and prepared until they could hold ink for a thousand years. Later, the early church pioneered the codex, the bound book with pages, because Christians wanted the whole counsel of God between two covers, easy to carry and quick to search. Every copy was made by hand, letter by letter, which makes what God did in preserving those copies all the more remarkable, as we will see.

The languages. God gave His Word in three languages. The Old Testament was written in Hebrew, the language of Israel — a language of concrete pictures, where a word like "hesed" carries God's covenant love and steadfast mercy in a single breath. Small portions were written in Aramaic, the language of the Babylonian exile: parts of Daniel and Ezra, written for a people living under foreign empires. The New Testament was written in Greek — not the polished Greek of the philosophers, but Koine, the common Greek of the marketplace and the street, the language a fisherman and a tentmaker actually spoke. That was no accident. When the fullness of time came, God had a language ready that the whole Mediterranean world could read, and He filled it with fishermen's words carrying heaven's meaning.

Three languages. Forty writers. Fifteen centuries. One Book, and one breath behind it all.

3. The Canon: Recognized, Not Invented

One of the most common questions people ask about the Bible is this: who decided which books belong in it? Behind the question sits a suspicion — that a council of men in robes sat down centuries after Christ and invented the Bible, choosing the books that suited them and discarding the rest.

The true history is almost the opposite. The word "canon" comes from a Greek word for a measuring rod or rule. The canon of Scripture is the list of books that measure up — not because the church stamped them into authority, but because the church recognized the authority that was already in them. A council no more made a book Scripture than a shepherd makes a sheep a sheep by counting it into the fold. The sheep was a sheep already. The church's work was recognition, not invention.

The Old Testament canon. The thirty-nine books of the Old Testament were received by the people of Israel over many centuries as the very words of God. By the time of Jesus, the Hebrew Scriptures stood in the threefold shape He Himself named — "the Law of Moses and the Prophets and the Psalms" (Luke 24:44, ESV). Jesus quoted these books constantly, argued from them as final authority, and never once treated any of them as doubtful. The apostles did the same. When Paul wanted to settle a matter, he appealed to what was written. The church received the Old Testament from the hands of the Lord Himself, who had already stamped it with His approval.

The New Testament canon. The twenty-seven books of the New Testament were recognized as the early churches applied, in practice, a set of tests that can be named plainly. Was the book apostolic — written by an apostle, or by a companion of an apostle writing under apostolic authority, as Mark wrote for Peter and Luke for Paul? Did it agree with Christ and with the faith once delivered — was it Christ-centered and true to the apostolic gospel, with nothing contrary to the books already received? And was it received by the churches — read in the assemblies across the world, copied, quoted by the early fathers, and used by God's people everywhere, not merely treasured by one local sect? Books that carried the apostles' own witness passed these tests in the churches' life long before any list was drawn up. Peter already called Paul's letters "Scripture" while Paul was still alive (2 Peter 3:16). Paul quoted Luke's Gospel as Scripture alongside Deuteronomy (1 Timothy 5:18). The recognition began in the apostolic generation itself.

The process took time because the church was scattered and persecuted, and doubtful books had to be weighed — a few letters circulated that claimed apostolic names but carried false teaching, and the churches, testing everything, let them fall away. By the fourth century the dust had settled. In the year 367, Athanasius, the great bishop of Alexandria who had suffered exile five times for defending the deity of Christ, wrote his Easter Letter to the churches, and in it he listed the books of the New Testament — all twenty-seven, exactly as we have them today, no more and no fewer. It is the first surviving list that matches our New Testament precisely. Later councils, at Hippo and Carthage, affirmed the same list — but they were confirming what the churches had already received, the way a man confirms the identity of a friend he has known for years.

It is worth adding that the recognition did not wait for the fourth century to begin. A list known as the Muratorian Fragment, dating from the late second century, already names most of the New Testament books as received, and the great teachers of the second and third centuries — Irenaeus, who had sat under a disciple of the apostle John; Clement of Alexandria; Tertullian; Origen — quote the four Gospels, Acts, Paul's letters, and most of the rest as Scripture, while frankly discussing the handful of shorter books about which some churches still had questions. The picture the evidence gives is not of a church suddenly inventing a Bible in the 300s, but of a family that had known its Father's letters for generations, finally writing out the full list of what it had always kept in the drawer.

The Apocrypha, honestly. Between the Testaments there is a group of Jewish writings — books like First and Second Maccabees, Tobit, Judith, and additions to Esther and Daniel — that the Roman Catholic Church receives as part of the Old Testament and calls deuterocanonical, while Protestants call them the Apocrypha. Honesty requires saying what is true on both sides. These books are genuinely old, genuinely valuable for history — First Maccabees is nearly our only detailed record of the Jewish struggle under the Greek kings — and the Reformers themselves said they were worth reading for instruction and example. But here is the Reformed position, stated plainly: they were never received as Scripture by the Jews who kept the Hebrew canon; Jesus and the apostles, who quoted the Old Testament hundreds of times, never once quoted them as Scripture; they contain teachings at odds with the received books; and the church's earliest lists of the Hebrew canon did not include them. Jerome himself, who translated them, marked the distinction. So we read them the way we read other good and ancient books — with gratitude for what they preserve and without bowing to them as the Word of God. The measuring rod is not lowered to fit every ancient page; the ancient pages are measured by the rod.

Thirty-nine and twenty-seven. Sixty-six books. Recognized by the people of God across the centuries, because in them the people of God heard the voice of their Shepherd.

4. Preservation: How God Kept His Word

Inspiration would mean little if the inspired words had been lost or scrambled beyond recovery in the copying. So God did a second work alongside the first: He preserved His Word through the hands of the most careful copyists the ancient world ever produced.

The scribes of Israel. The Hebrew Scriptures were copied by men for whom copying was a sacred trust. The scribes — and later the Masoretes, the Jewish scholars who guarded the text roughly between the years 500 and 1000 after Christ — worked under rules that stagger the modern mind. They counted the words of a book. They counted the letters. They knew the middle word and the middle letter of the Torah, so that a copy could be checked against the standard at its very center. A scroll with errors beyond a set number was not corrected and shelved; it was buried. The Masoretes also gave us the vowel points that fixed the pronunciation and reading of the Hebrew text, and the great codices they produced — the Aleppo Codex and the Leningrad Codex, our oldest complete Hebrew Bibles — carried the text into the age of printing.

But did their text stay true across all those centuries? For a long time, our oldest complete Hebrew manuscripts were only about a thousand years old, and honest men could ask the question. Then God answered it out of a cave.

The Dead Sea Scrolls. In 1946 and 1947, shepherd boys searching for a lost goat in the cliffs west of the Dead Sea threw a stone into a cave and heard pottery break. Inside were jars containing scrolls — and over the following years, eleven caves yielded the remains of more than nine hundred documents, including copies of every book of the Old Testament except Esther. The crown of the find is the Great Isaiah Scroll: a complete copy of the book of Isaiah, written around 125 years before Christ — roughly a thousand years older than any Hebrew copy of Isaiah the world had possessed. Scholars held their breath and compared it, line by line, with the Isaiah in our Bibles. The result is now famous: across a thousand years of hand copying, the text had come down essentially unchanged, the differences amounting to spelling and small slips that alter no doctrine and almost no sentence. The God who breathed the words had watched over the copying. Isaiah in the cave and Isaiah in the pulpit were the same Isaiah.

The abundance of the New Testament. If the Old Testament's preservation is remarkable, the New Testament's is without parallel in the ancient world. More than 5,800 Greek manuscripts of the New Testament survive, in whole or in part — some 10,000 more in Latin, and thousands more in other early translations — along with so many quotations in the writings of the early church fathers that, scholars have often observed, nearly the whole New Testament could be rebuilt from their pages alone if every Bible on earth were burned. Set that beside the other books of antiquity: the works of the Roman historians survive in a handful of copies, written centuries after their authors; no one doubts their text on that account. For the New Testament we have copies written within a generation or two of the apostles — a fragment of John's Gospel, held in a library in Manchester, dates to within a few decades of the apostle's own lifetime. The great codices of the fourth century, Sinaiticus and Vaticanus, carry nearly the whole Bible in single volumes.

Honest men have counted the small variations among those thousands of copies — the variant readings, as scholars call them — and here honesty must be complete: the overwhelming majority are spelling differences and slips of the pen, and not one of them touches any doctrine of the Christian faith. What the abundance actually gives us is certainty. We do not possess the Bible by a thread. We possess it by a flood of witnesses, more than for any other book of the ancient world, all saying the same thing.

The grass of empires withered. The flower of libraries faded. The Word stood — copied by candlelight, hidden in caves, buried with martyrs, and carried out the other side of every century intact.

5. The First Translations: The Word Learns New Tongues

The Bible has never been a book content to sit in one language. From the beginning, God intended His Word for every nation, and translation is as old as the church — older, in fact, than the church itself.

The Septuagint. Centuries before Christ, the Jewish people were scattered across the Greek-speaking world, and many of them, living in cities like Alexandria in Egypt, no longer read Hebrew. So, beginning in the third century before Christ, Jewish scholars translated the Old Testament into Greek. The result is called the Septuagint — from the Latin word for seventy, after the tradition that seventy-two elders did the work. It was the Bible of the Greek-speaking synagogues, the Bible the apostles themselves most often quoted, and the bridge over which the Old Testament walked into the Gentile world. God had the translation ready before the translators of the New Testament ever lifted a pen.

The Latin Vulgate. As the church spread west, Latin became its language, and the early Latin versions were rough and conflicting. Around the year 382, the bishop of Rome commissioned the finest scholar of the age, a monk named Jerome, to produce a reliable Latin Bible. Jerome went to Bethlehem — he lived in a cave near the cave of the Nativity — learned Hebrew from Jewish teachers at some risk to his reputation, and translated the Old Testament fresh from the Hebrew rather than from the Greek, a decision that drew sharp criticism in his day. His New Testament revision and his Old Testament translation together became the Latin Bible of the Western church for a thousand years: the Vulgate, the "common" version, from the Latin word for the language of the common people. There is a deep irony here, and it should not be missed. Jerome translated the Bible into the language ordinary people spoke. A thousand years later, the church that guarded his translation had made Latin a language ordinary people no longer spoke at all — and was resisting, sometimes with fire, every attempt to do for Englishmen and Germans what Jerome had done for Romans.

Other early tongues. Greek and Latin were not the only languages the Word learned early. The church in Syria received the Scriptures in Syriac, a sister language to the Aramaic Jesus spoke, in the version called the Peshitta — "the simple version" — which Syriac-speaking believers use to this day. In Egypt the Bible was translated into Coptic for the farmers of the Nile. And in the fourth century a missionary bishop named Ulfilas carried the gospel to the Goths beyond Rome's frontier and, tradition says, devised an alphabet for their language for the express purpose of giving them the Bible in it — the first Bible translation ever made for a people who had no written language of their own. The pattern held in every direction the church advanced: where the gospel went, the Book learned the local tongue. It has never stopped learning them since.

Wycliffe's English Bible. The first complete English Bible came from the Latin Vulgate, and it came through the work inspired by John Wycliffe, the Oxford scholar we met in the companion history of the Reformation. In the 1380s and 1390s, Wycliffe's followers produced the first full translation of the Bible into English — copied entirely by hand, each one the labor of months, and precious beyond price. It put the Word into the hands of plowmen and weavers, and the authorities hated it for exactly that reason. Laws were passed against it. Possessing it could cost a man his life. But the copies multiplied, and more than two hundred and fifty of those hand-written English Bibles survive to this day — more than survive of almost any other medieval English work — because ordinary believers treasured them, hid them, and handed them down.

The pattern was now set, and it would not be broken: the Word of God presses toward the language of the people, and men of God arise in every age willing to pay the price of carrying it there.

Then came the press. One more providence belongs to this part of the story. Around the year 1455, in Mainz, Germany, Johannes Gutenberg produced the first great book ever printed with movable type in Europe — and the book he chose was the Bible, Jerome's Latin Vulgate, in an edition of some one hundred and eighty copies. Before Gutenberg, every Bible on earth was the work of a scribe's hand and a year or more of a scribe's life, and cost what a house cost. After Gutenberg, a press could do in weeks what a scriptorium did in years, and the price of a book began its long fall toward the reach of ordinary men. The timing was not luck. Within two generations the Reformation would break over Europe, and it would ride on printed paper — printed Testaments, printed pamphlets, printed Bibles in the languages of the people. God set the press in place before He lit the fire, the way a man lays in firewood before the winter. The translation wars of the next section were fought with weapons Gutenberg forged.

6. The Translation Wars: Blood on the Page

If the first translations were difficult, the translations of the Reformation were war. When the Reformers recovered the doctrine that Scripture alone is the church's final authority, a question followed immediately: how can the people test the teaching of the church by a Book they cannot read? The answer cost some of the finest men of the age their lives.

William Tyndale. No name stands higher in this story. Tyndale was an Oxford and Cambridge scholar, a master of Hebrew and Greek, who burned with one conviction: that the Bible must be translated from its original languages, not from the Latin, and put into English plain enough for the common man. In a famous exchange, a learned clergyman told him, "We were better to be without God's laws than the pope's." Tyndale answered: "If God spare my life, ere many years I will cause a boy that driveth the plough shall know more of the Scripture than thou dost." Denied permission to work in England, he fled to Germany, and there, in 1526, his English New Testament came off the press at Worms — the first English New Testament translated from the Greek and the first printed in English. Copies were smuggled into England in bales of cloth and sacks of grain. The bishop of London bought up and burned as many as he could find — and the money from those sales, as the story has long been told, financed the printing of still more. Tyndale went on to translate the first five books of the Old Testament and more, working in exile, in poverty, and finally in hiding in Antwerp, until he was betrayed by a false friend, arrested, and imprisoned in the castle at Vilvoorde, near Brussels. On October 6, 1536, William Tyndale was strangled at the stake and his body burned. His last words, cried out at the stake, were a prayer: "Lord, open the King of England's eyes."

The prayer answered. God answered that prayer with a speed that looks like deliberate irony. Within a few years, that same king, Henry the Eighth, authorized an official English Bible — the Great Bible of 1539, prepared by Tyndale's friend Miles Coverdale and built heavily on Tyndale's own work — and ordered it placed in every parish church in England, chained to a lectern where any man could come and read it. The chains were not to keep the people from the Book this time. They were to keep the Book there for the people. Crowds gathered to hear it read. The plowboy was learning more of Scripture than the clergy, exactly as Tyndale had promised.

The Geneva Bible. When Bloody Mary drove England's Protestants into exile, the scholars among them gathered in Calvin's Geneva and produced, in 1560, the Geneva Bible — the first English Bible with numbered verses, the first with study notes in the margins, printed in a size a family could own and afford. It became the Bible of Shakespeare's England, the Bible of the Puritans, and the Bible the Pilgrims carried to America on the Mayflower in 1620. Its notes were too plain-spoken about kings and tyrants to please King James, which is part of why he commissioned a new translation of his own.

The King James Version. At the Hampton Court Conference in 1604, the Puritan scholar John Rainolds proposed a new translation, and James the First approved it. The work was given to six companies of translators — two at Westminster, two at Oxford, two at Cambridge — forty-seven men in all, the finest Hebrew and Greek scholars in the kingdom, working under rules designed to keep them honest: each man translated alone, his company reviewed him, other companies reviewed the company, and disputed places went to a final committee. They stood on the shoulders of the men before them, Tyndale above all; it has been reckoned that where the King James New Testament can be compared with Tyndale's, the great majority of its wording is Tyndale's own, carried over nearly word for word. The translator they burned supplied the Bible they published. The King James Version appeared in 1611, and for more than three centuries it reigned — the Bible of the English-speaking world, the Bible that formed the language of its preaching, its hymns, its laws, and its homes. Generations of believers were raised on its cadences, and its phrases soaked so deeply into English that people quote it today without knowing they are quoting it.

Tyndale died with his Old Testament unfinished and his name disgraced. Within seventy-five years of his death, his words were being read aloud in every church in England by order of the king. That is how God writes history.

7. The Modern Translations: Older Manuscripts, New Bibles

For two hundred and fifty years after 1611, the King James Version stood nearly alone in English. Then, in the nineteenth century, the ground shifted — not because the King James had failed, but because the world kept handing the church older and older copies of the Greek and Hebrew text.

Archaeologists and scholars recovered manuscripts far older than the ones available in 1611. The Codex Sinaiticus, a nearly complete Bible from the fourth century, was identified in a monastery at the foot of Mount Sinai in the 1840s and 1850s. The Codex Vaticanus, long sitting in the Vatican library, became available to scholars. Then came the papyrus finds in Egypt — early fragments and books of the New Testament preserved in the dry sand — and, in the twentieth century, the Dead Sea Scrolls for the Old Testament. The King James translators had worked faithfully with what they had: for the New Testament, a handful of late manuscripts. Their successors suddenly had thousands, some a thousand years closer to the apostles.

Out of that wealth came the modern translations, each with its own work to do. The Revised Version appeared in England and America in the 1880s, the first great revision of the King James, prized for its exactness. The American Standard Version followed in 1901, long loved by careful students for its literal faithfulness. The Revised Standard Version, completed in 1952, aimed to carry the King James tradition forward in contemporary language. The New American Standard Bible, finished in 1971, pressed the American Standard's literal approach further. The New International Version, completed in 1978, took a different road — translating thought for thought where that served clarity — and became the most widely read modern version in the world. Translation committees multiplied, and English-speaking believers found themselves, for the first time in history, choosing among many faithful translations where their grandfathers had owned exactly one.

The English Standard Version. In 2001, Crossway published the English Standard Version, and it is the translation this ministry uses, so its reasons deserve to be stated plainly. The ESV stands in the King James tradition by way of the Revised Standard Version, but its governing aim is an "essentially literal" translation: to render the original words word for word as faithfully as English allows — keeping the Bible's own metaphors instead of replacing them, keeping its connections and repetitions instead of smoothing them away — while speaking in English that ordinary people actually read. For a ministry that preaches verse by verse and asks people to open the Book and check what the preacher says, that combination is the whole point: faithfulness to the words God breathed, in language a tired man can follow at midnight. A translation is a servant, not a master. The test of it is whether it carries the reader to the original text or stands between the reader and it. The ESV passes that test, which is why its words fill the pages of this shelf.

None of this makes the older translations enemies. The King James believer and the ESV believer are reading the same Bible, preserved by the same God, and the differences between the faithful translations are differences of wording, not of gospel. What the modern age of translation proves is the point of the last section: the text was never lost. Every new manuscript dug out of the sand has confirmed the Book the church already had.

8. The Bible Today: A Book Still on the Move

The story is not finished. The Book that began on scrolls in the wilderness is still traveling, and it is traveling faster now than in any century since the apostles.

The whole Bible now exists in well over seven hundred of the world's languages, the New Testament in well over sixteen hundred more, and portions of Scripture in thousands beyond that. Translation teams — many of them mother-tongue translators, believers translating the Book into their own people's language for the first time — are at work in hundreds of languages where not one verse has ever existed. Men and women have given whole lifetimes to single languages spoken by single valleys, so that a grandmother who never left her village could hear, in the words of her childhood, that God so loved the world. Behind them stands an army of ordinary givers, printers, pilots, and teachers, most of whose names will never be known on earth.

And where the Book is most forbidden, it is most desired. In lands where owning a Bible can mean prison, believers treasure single pages the way rich men treasure gold. Testimonies come out of the persecuted church of whole congregations sharing one copy, of passages copied by hand and passed from house to house, of men in prison cells who memorized chapters because paper could be taken and memory could not. Set that beside the shelves of the comfortable West, where a household may own five Bibles and read none, and it tells you something about the Book: its value is most visible where it costs the most. The men and women who hunger for a single page are not confused about what they hold.

The Book has also come home, in a sense, to the people this ministry serves. It is the testimony of the man behind The Hope Connection — told often, and told plainly — that no program, no philosophy, and no effort of his own ever changed his life, but that this Book did, when it was opened to him in the darkest season he had known, and the God of the Book met him where he was. That story is not unusual. It is the most repeated story in the history of the Bible: a broken person, an open page, and a living God who still speaks through words written thousands of years ago as if He had written them that morning. Rehabs and recovery rooms, hospital wards and jail cells, kitchen tables at two in the morning — the Book keeps arriving in such places, because the God who preserved it through caves and fires intends it for exactly the people who are found in them.

Printing presses, radio, and now phones in the pockets of men who cannot afford shoes carry the same sixty-six books. The media change. The message has not changed by one word.

9. The Word That Stands Forever

Empires have had their say about this Book. Pharaohs and caesars, kings and commissars, critics in every century — each announced, in his turn, that the Bible was finished. The empires are in museums. The Book is in more hands tonight than ever before in the history of the world.

That is not an accident, and it is not merely the story of a resilient piece of literature. It is the keeping of a promise. Isaiah saw the empires of men as grass in the summer sun, and he heard the word of the Lord over against all of it:

"The grass withers, the flower fades, but the word of our God will stand forever." — Isaiah 40:8 (ESV)

The word of our God. It stands because He stands behind it — breathed out by Him, recognized by His people, copied by His scribes, dug out of His caves, translated by His martyrs, and carried now into languages that did not have alphabets when Tyndale died. Every link in that long chain was held, at the moment it mattered, by the hand of God Himself, often working through people who did not live to see what their faithfulness accomplished. Tyndale never held a King James Bible. The Masoretes never saw a Dead Sea Scroll. The shepherd boys never knew what their stone had struck. They only had to be faithful with their link. He handled the chain.

So the invitation of this history is the invitation of the Book itself. Do not merely admire its survival. Open it. Read it the way the Reformers taught us to read it — plainly, daily, in the confidence that God speaks in it still. Let it do to you what it has done to shepherds and kings, prisoners and plowmen, for three thousand years: let it find you where you are, and let the God of it meet you there. He has kept this Book through fire and flood so that it could be in your hands tonight. It would be a strange thing to let it sit closed.

A prayer. Lord God, You have watched over Your Word through every century — through caves and fires, through chains and scaffolds — and You have put it into our hands in our own language, plain enough for a child and deep enough for a lifetime. We thank You for the scribes who counted letters, for the translators who counted their lives as nothing, and for every unnamed believer who hid a page and passed it on. Now do in us what this Book has done in every age: open our eyes, humble our pride, heal our wounds, and anchor our hope in Jesus Christ, the Word made flesh, of whom every page speaks. Keep us faithful with our link in the chain, until the day we need the Book no longer, because we see its Author face to face. In Jesus' name, amen.

Christ Proclaimed. Jesus is Lord.
